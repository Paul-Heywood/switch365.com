Summary

What changed overnight that could change the price a UK business can actually secure today.

Iran has received the formal US response to its seven-day trust-building proposal, delivered through Qatari mediators in Doha on Tuesday and presented to President Pezeshkian's cabinet on Wednesday. The complete terms have not been published by either government or by Qatar. What is verified is that Washington has not accepted Iran's package as written: the response reads as a counterproposal in which the central dispute is sequencing, meaning who acts first on Iranian port restrictions, sanctions relief, frozen assets, nuclear commitments and the reopening of Hormuz. Iran's proposal links reopening the strait to US action on all of those fronts; Washington wants stronger nuclear conditions before substantial concessions. No verified agreement has reopened Hormuz. This is the freshest direct diplomatic development in the conflict, and it is a duration story, not a new physical outage.

The negotiating atmosphere deteriorated in parallel. Axios reports that Secretary of State Rubio ordered the Iranian delegation, including Foreign Minister Araghchi, to leave the United States on Monday evening after the White House concluded talks had reached a stalemate; the delegation boarded a flight from New York to Doha at 1:20am on Tuesday. Iran disputes that account: its UN mission says the departure followed an itinerary communicated to the State Department on 17 September and calls the expulsion claim baseless. Trump has separately denied offering sanctions relief or frozen funds upfront, writing that he offered Iran "nothing". Qatar continues to circulate compromise ideas to both sides, so the channel is strained but not demonstrably closed. Willingness to talk is not the same as agreement on the terms of a deal.

Against that sits a genuine physical counterweight. Saudi Arabia's East-West Pipeline restarted on 22 September, Yanbu crude and refined-product loadings have resumed with close to 10 million barrels observed loading, and Middle Eastern crude exports recovered to approximately 16.328 million b/d in September, the highest since the conflict began, with Gulf flows at roughly 80% of pre-war levels on Kpler's measure. US crude inventories unexpectedly rose by 922,000 barrels. Active Brent December traded at $97.00/bbl at 05:29 on 1 October, down around 1%, and WTI near $89. European gas has softened from its September peak, with TTF down almost 20% from mid-September highs, and the internal Switch365 offered board shows W-26 gas at 182.00p, down 4.00p on the day. None of this removes the diplomatic risk; it means customer pricing must be set by live NBP, TTF and supplier-book evidence, not by the headline alone.

OVERNIGHT IN 30 SECONDS

  • The main reported obstacle is sequencing, and no agreement has reopened Hormuz.

WHAT IT MEANS FOR UK ENERGY

The physical energy system remains under severe pressure, but individual markets are reacting differently. Oil is falling on recovering physical supply while diplomacy deteriorates; gas is easing from extreme September levels but sits far above last year, with Qatar's force majeure now extended into December for at least one European buyer; UK power is being set day to day by wind and gas-fired marginal pricing. Customer decisions should therefore be based on today's live supplier price, not an assumption that every wholesale market must move in the same direction. Waiting is not neutral: quotes expire, books reprice, and once a supplier withdraws a rate, the customer cannot go back and secure yesterday's price.

Risk has risen, but the system has not stopped.

Section 05

Futures Market Explained

Why a customer can act on 2027, 2028, 2029 and 2030 prices today.

The futures market shows today's price for energy delivered in a future period.

A customer does not have to wait until their contract end date to see today's price for future delivery.

Cheaper further out does not mean cheaper later.

Backwardation does not mean wait.

Backwardation means nearer delivery is priced above later delivery. Today's board is heavily backwardated: gas S-30 is offered 62.5% below W-26 and power S-29 55.5% below. That can create an opportunity for future-start procurement, but it does not guarantee prices will remain available. A future-start contract can be secured now where supplier eligibility and procurement rules allow. The relevant question is: can the customer secure the cheaper future delivery today, not whether energy will definitely be cheaper when that date arrives.

Section 08

British Gas 18-Month Window

Connecting the extended renewal window to a backwardated market.

British Gas may allow an eligible customer to secure a future-start contract up to 18 months before their current contract ends. The new contract starts after the existing contract finishes, not today.

  • Eligibility must be checked; not every customer is automatically eligible, and supplier availability, term and credit remain relevant.
  • With the board this backwardated, a customer may be able to secure a future-start price well below today's front-winter levels: the offered S-27 and S-28 gas seasons sit 28.3% and 53.7% below W-26.
  • Supplier volatility cuts both ways: books can be pulled, suspended or repriced while a customer waits, and the price is not protected until the contract is accepted and confirmed.
  • Duration risk is exactly what the window hedges: the sequencing deadlock and disputed delegation exit weaken confidence in rapid normalisation, so a cheaper future delivery secured today is a known price, not a hope.
British Gas 18-month eligibility turns this market intelligence into a practical customer action: check eligibility, check the live future-start price against the actual contract end date, and treat the decision as a comparison, not a guess about where the market goes next.
