Section 01

Summary

Kpler tracked four commodity-vessel transits through the Strait of Hormuz on Monday, down from ten on Sunday. The tracked Monday movements consisted of two dry-bulk vessels exiting (one laden, one in ballast) and two oil tankers entering, both in ballast. AIS-based tracking does not capture every physical movement, so the correct wording is that Kpler tracked four transits, not that only four ships used Hormuz. Even so, this is the weakest tracked daily count since the impairment deepened, and it is today's freshest physical energy development: the primary chokepoint for Gulf energy is moving less cargo, not more.

The East-West Pipeline remains offline, and the question has changed from whether it was hit to how long it stays down. Saudi Arabia says drone attacks originating from Iraq forced the shutdown of its principal Hormuz bypass on 11 September; repair assessments range from a relatively quick restart to several weeks. Overnight the security picture widened: the Houthis claimed a large missile and drone attack on King Khalid Air Base at Khamis Mushait, and the Saudi-led coalition confirmed attacks on Khamis Mushait, Abha and Taif that injured 13 civilians. Saudi Civil Defence issued Tuesday-morning alerts for six cities including Yanbu, the kingdom's main Red Sea oil port, before later saying the danger had passed. Bab al-Mandeb tracked commodity traffic also fell, to 21 vessels on Monday from 28 on Sunday. The strait remains physically active; this is not a closure, but the physical shipping data has now weakened as well as the security position.

Oil repriced hard on Monday; European gas did not follow at the same rate. Brent settled Monday at $105.68/bbl after intraday trade above $108, and eased back towards the $102-104 area in early Tuesday trading. WTI settled Monday at $101.39/bbl. TTF remains extremely elevated at around €82-83/MWh but was slightly softer at the referenced points, and UK NBP references eased from 206.75p/therm on Monday to around 204.7p/therm early Tuesday. Qatar and UAE LNG exports through Hormuz remain heavily constrained, with Shell estimating around 36 million tonnes of Middle East LNG lost so far this year. For UK customers the consequence runs through LNG, TTF, NBP and then supplier price books: internal Switch365 evidence shows the offered W-26 gas range at 204.00p/therm and W-26 power at £163.00/MWh this morning, both lower than Monday's snapshots.

But the strongest new market reaction has been in oil rather than another equivalent surge in European gas, and this morning's internal offered ranges for gas and power are lower than yesterday's. Customer decisions should therefore be based on today's live supplier price, not an assumption that every wholesale market must move in the same direction. Brent is not the customer's gas quote.

Section 05

Futures Market Explained

The futures market shows today's price for energy delivered in a future period. A customer does not have to wait until their contract end date to see today's price for future delivery: the W-27, S-28, W-28, S-29, W-29 and S-30 rows in this report are all live, purchasable delivery periods, quoted by suppliers now.

Cheaper further out does not mean cheaper later. A lower S-28 or S-29 price on today's screen is not a forecast of what those periods will cost when the customer eventually gets round to buying them. It is an offer that exists today and can be withdrawn today. Backwardation does not mean wait.

The customer mistake is thinking a cheaper future period means they should wait. It does not. It means we should check whether that future period can be secured now. The future market is only useful while the customer can still buy that future delivery period, and waiting gives the market time to remove today's forward-value opportunity. Waiting is not neutral.

British Gas 18-Month Window

Switch365 internal evidence

British Gas has extended its business energy renewal window from 12 months to 18 months. This gives eligible customers an earlier chance to review future-start pricing.

The 18-month British Gas window matters because it turns the forward market into a practical customer action. With W-26 gas offered at 204.00p/therm against S-28 at 80.50p and S-29 at 67.00p, a customer whose contract ends between 12 and 18 months out can now test pricing for delivery periods that sit far below the front of the curve, rather than waiting for the renewal window to open into a possibly repriced market.

Your new contract would not start today. It would begin when your current agreement ends. Not every customer is automatically eligible, so eligibility should be checked first, then live pricing. British Gas 18-month eligibility turns this market intelligence into a practical customer action.

Waiting is not neutral. If the market moves or British Gas withdraws rates, customers cannot go back and secure today's pricing later. Cheaper further out does not mean cheaper later: it means check whether the future period can be secured now.

LPG carrier movements must never be read as LNG normalisation, and a crude tanker transiting Hormuz does not prove Qatar LNG has normalised.

Supplier price-book lag is the opportunity and the danger. Wholesale markets move first. Supplier books often follow. This morning the lag is working in the customer's favour: books eased overnight while the physical backdrop worsened. The market does not need to rise again tomorrow for customers to pay more tomorrow, because once a supplier withdraws a rate, the customer cannot go back and secure yesterday's price. Switch365 internal experience is that around the 180p/therm wholesale area some suppliers previously withdrew books, suspended pricing or returned with higher pricing; that is commercial experience, not a technical market threshold.
