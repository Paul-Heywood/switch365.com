Hormuz Is Moving, But The Standoff Is Hardening

UK Gas, UK Power, Futures Market and Procurement Position

Hormuz is moving, but the standoff is hardening. Deal hopes are fading because both sides have moved into compensation demands. Trump is now demanding compensation from Iran for people killed and wounded, while Tehran is still demanding US concessions before full reopening. Oil steadied on Tuesday at more than one-week highs amid fading hopes of a deal between the US and Iran to end their war and reopen the Strait of Hormuz.

Shipping traffic through the Strait of Hormuz fell to six vessels on Monday, compared with a 10-day average of about 11 vessels. Barclays reported that crude and refined-product exports through Hormuz fell to around 3 million barrels per day in the week ending 7 August, down from 4.4 million barrels per day the previous week. This is the strongest commercial signal that the route is constrained, not normal.

Saudi Aramco has delayed the restart of its Jazan refinery after Houthi-claimed attacks. Iran appointed hardliner Mohsen Rezaei as secretary of its Supreme National Security Council, pointing to a tougher security posture, not a softer one. The Saudi / Türkiye / Pakistan defence pact raises the consequence of any attack on Saudi energy infrastructure, but it does not mean automatic military retaliation by all three states.

UK gas rose above 142 pence per therm on Monday, recovering most of the previous week's losses. The forward curve still shows material backwardation: S-30 gas is 56.5% below W-26. The British Gas 18-month renewal window remains live. This is a review-now window, not a wait-and-see market.

Futures Market Explained

Why the forward curve matters for procurement decisions

The futures market shows the price for energy delivered in future periods. A customer renewing from 2027, 2028, 2029 or 2030 is not only interested in today's price. They should look at what the market is currently offering for their actual delivery period.

Cheaper further out does not mean cheaper later.

The future market is only useful while the customer can still buy that future delivery period.

Waiting lets the market make the decision for the customer.

As time passes, that future period becomes the near-term market and can absorb the same risk premium.

The 1 Year Ago line is context. The latest line is the customer's decision point.

A customer with a contract ending in Summer 2027 should not focus on today's W-26 price. They should focus on what the market is offering for S-27 delivery. If S-27 is 34.3% cheaper than W-26, that is the relevant comparison for their procurement conversation.

Supplier Price-Book Lag

The opportunity and the danger

Supplier price-book lag is the opportunity and the danger.

Wholesale markets move first. Supplier books often follow.

Waiting for certainty can mean missing the price movement.

Once a supplier withdraws a rate, the customer cannot go back and buy yesterday's price.

Even if oil has fallen from the highs, supplier books may still react to the move that has already happened.

Suppliers do not need a full closure of Hormuz or Bab el-Mandeb before they reprice.

Suppliers also do not need diplomacy to fail completely before they price renewed conflict risk.

When conflict spreads geographically, suppliers can price the increased probability of disruption before physical closure happens.

Falling Hormuz export flows, a Jazan restart delay, and compensation demands are all supplier-pricing risk signals.

1 Year Ago prices are useful context, but they do not protect the customer today.
