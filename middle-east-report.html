Futures Market Explained

What these numbers are, and what they are not

NBP versus TTF

NBP is Britain's virtual gas trading point, priced in pence per therm; TTF is the Dutch hub, priced in euros per megawatt hour, and the continental benchmark. They usually move together because the UK is connected to Europe by pipelines and competes for the same LNG cargoes, but they are different instruments in different currencies and do not move tick for tick. Monday showed why instrument precision matters: month-ahead TTF spiked intraday towards €96/MWh on cold-snap headlines, yet the front-month series settled at €89.74, only modestly above Friday's €88.45.

Spot, front month, seasons

A day-ahead price is gas for tomorrow. A front-month contract (December 2026) is the nearest traded month. W-26, S-27, W-27 and beyond are delivery seasons: winter 2026-27, summer 2027 and so on. A price for summer 2028 delivery is not a prediction of what gas will cost in 2028; it is what the market will sell that period for today. Historical settlements, live intraday quotes and closing indications are also different things, which is why this report labels every observation.

Offered range versus executable rate

The Switch365 tables are internal offered-season indicators: evidence of the direction and shape of supplier pricing, not a quote a supplier has committed to honour. An executable rate requires a named supplier, product, start date, full charges, credit check and validity window. A 0.85% fall in the W-26 working reference is real internal evidence; it is not a 0.85% discount on any customer's bill.

Why prices can fall while threats rise

Gas, oil, LNG and power have different supply curves. Oil softened because Libyan and Middle East barrels increased and a surplus is expected; gas is balanced against LNG availability, Norwegian flows, storage and weather. A risk premium can unwind on diplomacy or supply expectations even while physical threats, like this month's record tempo of tanker incidents, continue. Supplier rate books may lag wholesale screens in both directions, which is the opportunity and the danger of a falling day.

Procurement Window

A two-sided scenario, not a universal sign-now or wait-now instruction

Tuesday's full offered sheet fell across every row for the first time in over a week of this dataset, following Friday-to-Monday when all 8 gas and 6 power seasons had risen. Repricing reaches individual supplier books at different speeds, so the practical question is not "has the market fallen?" but "which suppliers have already passed it through, and is a valid quote available for this customer's start date?" Only a rate-locked, valid future-start quotation establishes what a customer can actually secure. This is a review-now window, not a wait-and-see market, and not a panic-fix market either.

Price-Up Risks BULLISH

  • UKMTO-verified tanker strikes keep coming (three near Khasab since Friday) and November's attack count is the highest of the war; a confirmed laden LNG carrier loss would reprice the market quickly.
  • QatarEnergy force majeure runs to end-December; Ras Laffan output is "very minute" and repairs could take years, keeping roughly a fifth of normal global LNG supply impaired.
  • EU storage at 68.9% and Germany at 56.5%, around 17 points below the seasonal norm, in active withdrawal as the heating season starts.
  • Cold-snap forecasting already drove Monday's intraday TTF spike towards €96; a sustained cold, low-wind spell would lift gas-for-power demand.
  • Supplier price-book withdrawals: once a supplier pulls a rate, the customer cannot go back and secure yesterday's price.

Price-Down Counterweights BEARISH

  • Brent at a three-month low on rising Middle East flows, the Sharara restart and an expected 1.7m bpd surplus: risk premium is draining from the energy complex.
  • US LNG exports hit their second-highest month on record in October, with Europe's share recovering to about half of cargoes.
  • Diplomatic de-escalation and physical maritime security improvements can cut the war-risk premium before any infrastructure is repaired.
  • Monday's TTF spike largely unwound by the close, showing how quickly fear premium deflates when no physical loss is confirmed.
  • Today's supplier offered range is already lower across the board; genuinely available quotes may still be competitively priced for eligible customers.

Customer Checklist Before Any Recommendation

Confirm contract end date (CED), supply start and renewal window, supplier and product, annual kWh, meter details (MPAN/MPRN), usage profile, credit position, unit rate, standing charge, pass-through charges, broker commission, tariff inclusions and exclusions, cancellation terms, price validity and the full lifetime expected bill. Check British Gas 18-month eligibility where appropriate. Make balanced, informed options available, with no artificial urgency.

British Gas 18-Month Window

Switch365 internal commercial statement, user-confirmed

British Gas has extended its business energy renewal window from 12 months to 18 months, giving eligible customers an earlier opportunity to review future-start pricing. A future-start contract begins when the present supply agreement ends, not the day the quote is signed, and remains subject to supplier product, credit and eligibility rules. On a day when the whole offered curve has just repriced lower, the extended window lets eligible customers test whether that improvement is available to them before books move again. This is a commercial window, not proof that British Gas has the cheapest price or that any rate remains open. British Gas 18-month eligibility turns this market intelligence into a practical customer action.

Ceasefire Reality Check

Diplomacy versus physical passage

The 18 June US-Iran memorandum of understanding stalled over freedom of navigation through Hormuz, and Iran's 12 July closure announcement formally still stands. Oman-mediated contacts continue, but no operative signed agreement, demonstrably reduced UKMTO incident risk or measurable recovery in shipped LNG tonnes has been verified. November's attack tempo is the highest of the war, moving in the opposite direction to a ceasefire.

  • "Diplomatic contact is not a ceasefire, and a ceasefire announcement is not verified safe passage."
  • "An intercepted missile is not a confirmed energy infrastructure hit, and a hit is not necessarily lost production."
  • "An improvement in security can reduce a risk premium before damaged infrastructure is repaired, but physical flows are the supply test."

Open questions that define a real de-escalation: Are Ras Tanura, Abqaiq, the East-West pipeline, Yanbu and Rabigh operating with export evidence? Has Egypt's authorisation become a requested deployment? Do UKMTO and operators confirm repeatable safe LNG transits? Are Qatari loadings rising sustainably, or are US spot cargoes still compensating? Is falling Brent actually accompanied by lower TTF/NBP and softer supplier books, as today's internal sheet suggests, and does it last more than a session? Never assume price direction from the geopolitical narrative alone.

HORMUZ

The world's most important energy chokepoint is operating under an Iranian closure announcement, a US naval blockade and the war's highest monthly attack tempo. UKMTO verified three projectile strikes on outbound tankers near Khasab between Friday and Saturday; the IRGC's Monday mine claim is unverified. The commercial question is no longer "can a ship physically cross?" but "can commercial energy shipping cross repeatedly, safely and at an acceptable insurance cost?" A route can remain open and still carry a substantial war-risk premium. Friction gets priced.

GULF OF OMAN

UKMTO advisory 217-26 (Sunday) reported an incident involving a tanker and military forces; whether the vessel was attacked or damaged is not established. Earlier November incidents disabled at least one tanker with an engine-room fire. This is the alternative anchorage and waiting zone, so incidents here raise costs even when the strait itself is passable.

PERSIAN GULF

Iran says it has approved only the northern shipping lane and threatens vessels using routes near Oman, which puts Qatar's Ras Laffan loadings inside the contested zone. Every Qatari LNG cargo must cross Hormuz; there is no pipeline bypass, and QatarEnergy has ruled one out.

GULF OF ADEN

Houthi capability keeps this corridor live. Yemen's Balhaf LNG complex sits in Shabwa province alongside the Gulf of Aden; the earlier Shabwa attack increased its energy-security relevance without proving direct LNG infrastructure damage. Balhaf security risk is not Balhaf supply loss.

BAB AL-MANDEB

The gate between the Gulf of Aden and the Red Sea remains exposed to Houthi missile and drone launches. Turkey's closer cooperation on Red Sea navigation protection and Egypt's defensive authorisation are responses to exactly this exposure, but neither has yet changed verified transit economics.

RED SEA

As Hormuz becomes less reliable, the Red Sea route becomes more valuable and more strategic. Saudi crude can move from eastern oilfields through the East-West pipeline to Yanbu and out via the Red Sea, reducing reliance on Hormuz. A verified attack removing East-West or Yanbu capacity would materially alter the market; none is confirmed this week.

YANBU / RED SEA CORRIDOR

Yanbu matters because it helps Saudi crude bypass Hormuz. France's protective focus on Yanbu and the East-West corridor underlines its role. Oil has more rerouting options than LNG, but only current verified flows establish the net market effect.

LNG CARRIERS

No LNG carrier has been newly confirmed hit this week; the weekend UKMTO strikes involved tankers, and the cargo types in the cyber report are oil, LPG and chemicals. LPG is not LNG. An LNG security incident matters more to UK gas than an unrelated general-cargo incident, so vessel type is checked before any claim is amplified. The security risk zone is not confined to Hormuz.

Supplier Price-Book Lag

Wholesale markets move first; supplier books often follow

Today's full offered sheet fell across every row, following Friday-to-Monday when every row had risen. That whiplash is exactly how price-book lag works in both directions: suppliers reprice at different speeds, some books will still carry Monday's higher levels this morning, and some may hold lower quotes longer than expected. Supplier price-book lag is the opportunity and the danger. The market does not need to rise again tomorrow for customers to pay more tomorrow, because a book that has not yet fallen can still be repriced before a customer acts, and once a supplier withdraws a rate, the customer cannot go back and secure yesterday's price.

The practical test is per supplier and per customer: named supplier, exact unit and standing rates, full non-commodity pass-throughs and contract reopeners, broker margin and commission, CED, kWh and profile, credit, VAT and CCL, and expiry. A working-proxy percentage is not a full-bill customer saving. The British Gas 18-month eligible renewal window remains an additional route to compare real future-start products. No artificial "last chance", and no universal instruction to fix immediately or to wait.

Waiting Is Not Neutral BOTH SIDES

Waiting might help if US LNG and Norwegian supply stay strong, weather turns mild, winds rise, Gulf shipping normalises or suppliers cut rates further. Waiting might hurt if a verified LNG loss occurs, storage draws accelerate in the cold, or supplier books reverse. Waiting only works if the market moves in the customer's favour. Show both outcomes and let the customer choose the risk trade-off, with evidence-based quote comparison behind every recommendation.
