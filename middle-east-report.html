01Summary

Report date: Tuesday 8 September 2026 · Research window: 7-8 September 2026 · Extended verification: 2-8 September 2026 · All times Europe/London

Saudi Arabia says Ansar Allah, commonly called the Houthis, launched attacks against civilian and economic sites across Abha, Khamis Mushait, Jazan and Najran early on Tuesday, injuring 73 people including women and children. The Saudi energy ministry said fires erupted at some energy facilities and that operations at those sites were halted while emergency teams assessed the damage. The Saudi-led coalition described the escalation as “dangerous” and pledged to take all necessary operational measures in response.

Separately, the Financial Times reported that Aramco's Jazan oil facilities were hit and damage was being assessed. Reuters has not independently verified the report and Aramco had not commented, so the Jazan strike remains a reported hit, not a confirmed one. In parallel, Iran's Supreme National Security Council secretary Mohsen Rezaei said “economic warfare” would be answered with a new maritime exclusion zone running from the US blockade line through the Strait of Hormuz into the Gulf, with any vessel entering it placed on Iran's sanctions list. Iranian state media also claims a new anti-ship missile was tested above a US warship; the US military says its ships have evaded attacks.

For UK customers the transmission is already visible. TTF is around €73.6/MWh, up roughly 21% over the past month, and Switch365 internal supplier evidence shows the W-26 gas offered range moving up 4.50p to 183.00p/therm and W-26 power up £4.00 to £150.00/MWh against Friday morning. The front of the gas curve has now moved through the 180p/therm level at which Switch365 has previously seen some suppliers pull books, pause pricing or return with higher rates. Supplier price-book lag is the opportunity and the danger.

What it means for UK energy

Pressure is no longer limited to the chokepoints. It is spreading towards the infrastructure feeding the alternative routes. LNG remains the bridge between Hormuz and UK gas, and with Qatar severely constrained, EU storage below seasonal norms at 66.9% and winter approaching, each new layer of Gulf risk lands on a market with a thin cushion. Wholesale moved first. Supplier books may still follow. This is a review-now window, not a wait-and-see market.

05Futures Market Explained

The futures market shows today's price for energy delivered in a future period. A customer does not have to wait until their contract end date to see today's price for future delivery: a Winter-27 or Summer-28 price quoted this morning is a live, tradeable level, not a forecast.

Cheaper further out does not mean cheaper later. The customer mistake is thinking a cheaper future period means they should wait. It does not. It means we should check whether that future period can be secured now.

Backwardation does not mean wait. Today's curve is backwardated: W-26 gas at 183.00p is priced well above every later season. That shape reflects a market paying a heavy premium for near-term delivery through a disrupted winter, not a promise that later periods will stay cheap. The future market is only useful while the customer can still buy that future delivery period, and waiting gives the market time to remove today's forward-value opportunity. Waiting is not neutral.

Where the forward value actually sits

Today's risk has hit the front hardest. W-26 gas moved +4.50p and W-26 power +£4.00 between Friday and Monday, while the furthest seasons moved a fraction of that (W-29 gas +0.41p). The later curve moved, but modestly. That leaves a wide, real forward-value gap: Summer-28 gas is offered 56.8% below the front and Summer-29 63.7% below it, and S-29 gas at 66.50p is now barely above its own 1 Year Ago level of 64.91p.

Do not read the geopolitical headlines as proof that every future delivery period is equally expensive. The curve says the opposite: the market is charging for the next two winters and offering material value from 2028 onwards. The commercial question is not whether those periods are cheaper on a screen; it is whether a supplier will let the customer secure them today. 08British Gas 18-Month Window

INTERNAL BROKER / SUPPLIER EVIDENCE Switch365 supplier evidence unless advised otherwise.

British Gas has extended its business energy renewal window from 12 months to 18 months. This gives eligible customers an earlier chance to review future-start pricing. The 18-month British Gas window matters because it turns the forward market into a practical customer action: with the curve offering Summer-28 gas 56.8% below the front and Summer-29 63.7% below it, eligibility to look 18 months out is precisely where today's value sits.

Your new contract would not start today. It would begin when your current agreement ends. That structure lets an eligible customer test today's forward prices against a market that is currently charging a heavy premium for the next two winters, without changing supply arrangements now. Not every customer is automatically eligible, so the first step is an eligibility and live-pricing check.

Waiting is not neutral. If the market moves or British Gas withdraws rates, customers cannot go back and secure today's pricing later. Cheaper further out does not mean cheaper later. It means check whether the future period can be secured now.

10Top Five Warning Signs

  1. Aramco confirms material damage. Especially to production, processing, storage or export capacity at Jazan or elsewhere. Today's reported hit is unverified; confirmation would upgrade the story to hero level and test the Saudi workaround directly.
  2. Saudi Arabia launches large-scale retaliation. The coalition has pledged all necessary operational measures. Watch Yemen, the Red Sea and Houthi launch sites; a major campaign would widen the conflict around the Bab al-Mandab artery.
  3. Iran implements the new exclusion zone. Especially if ships are turned back, detained or sanctioned beyond the existing blacklist of at least 56 vessels. Announcement is not implementation; implementation is the trigger.
  4. Hormuz traffic falls further or LNG is hit. LNG remains the critical UK gas link. A tracked count below Monday's 7, or any strike on an LNG carrier, would tighten the physical gas picture immediately.
  5. NBP, TTF or supplier books reprice sharply. The front gas offered range is already through the 180p internal warning level. The immediate customer consequence is supplier behaviour: withdrawals, revalidation demands and shortened quote validity.

Hormuz

Kpler tracked seven commodity vessels through Hormuz on Monday, down from eight on Sunday. No visible VLCC has exited the strait since 2 September at the latest reporting point; if one exits, that would be meaningful evidence of partial shipping improvement. Middle Eastern crude exports are running at roughly 11 million bpd against about 18 million bpd before the war, with recent daily Hormuz flows below 2 million bpd on some days, a moving average around 4-5 million bpd (Rystad) and other industry estimates of 6-8 million bpd: that range reflects the uncertainty in measuring dark, visible and alternative flows. Iran's blacklist stands at at least 56 vessels and the proposed exclusion zone could extend enforcement beyond named ships.

Bab al-Mandab

Kpler tracked 29 commodity vessels through Bab al-Mandab on Monday, up from 17 on Sunday. The security risk is high: the Houthis declared a naval blockade against Saudi Arabia in July and have targeted Saudi-linked tankers. But physical shipping through Bab al-Mandab increased on Monday. Bab al-Mandab is under elevated security risk, but physical commodity traffic has not collapsed.

Saudi Red Sea

Reuters / Kpler data shows Saudi exports from Yanbu fell to approximately 1.429 million bpd in August against an average of roughly 3.9 million bpd over the previous three months, under pressure from the Houthi naval blockade. Red Sea pressure is already having a physical energy consequence. If Hormuz is the problem, Saudi and Red Sea infrastructure are part of the workaround; attacks on that infrastructure make the workaround less comfortable.

Qatar LNG

Qatar exported 18 LNG cargoes during the comparable conflict period versus 509 in the equivalent period last year, an approximate 96% reduction, from a country that historically supplied around one-fifth of global LNG. QatarEnergy's force majeure affecting its Edison arrangement extends towards early November, and two damaged Ras Laffan trains (about 12.8 mtpa, roughly 17% of capacity) could remain offline for years. Rare ship-to-ship transfers outside Hormuz show workarounds show resilience and disruption at the same time. LPG carrier movements are not evidence of LNG normalisation: LPG movement is not LNG normalisation.

UAE, Egypt and the Cape

The UAE says its energy exports will not be held hostage and is developing alternative eastern port capacity, pipelines and trade corridors. Egypt's Sidi Kerir exported 2.139 million bpd in August, more than double June volumes, with Iraq at about 2.34 million bpd and the UAE around 2.9 million bpd through July-August. The Cape of Good Hope remains the longer final workaround. The market is adapting: the energy system is absorbing more friction and more risk while continuing to adapt.

Hormuz is not open as usual.
Bab al-Mandab carries high security risk, but physical traffic has not collapsed. A route can remain physically open and still carry a material war-risk premium.
Friction gets priced. War-risk insurance, freight, delays, longer routes, tanker scarcity, rerouting, STS transfers, port congestion, escorts, financing and sanctions compliance do not each feed directly into UK NBP, but together they contribute to the global energy risk premium.

17Supplier Price-Book Lag

The overnight risk changed. Have supplier books?

Yesterday's supplier book↓ Saudi economic sites attacked↓ Aramco facility reportedly hit↓ Iran threatens economic warfare↓ TTF / NBP react↓ Supplier decides: hold, pull or reprice

Monday's internal offered ranges already moved higher across the curve. Today's Saudi escalation and Iran's economic-warfare threat occurred after those prices were produced. The immediate commercial question is which suppliers have already repriced this morning and which are still holding Monday's book. Check the actual book: withdrawals, repricing, temporary suspensions, quote validity, revalidation demands, term restrictions, new books, products removed, prices increased or reduced, and unchanged books. Do not assume escalation automatically means every supplier increases.

Switch365 internal commercial evidence: when wholesale gas previously reached approximately 180p/therm, some suppliers pulled books, temporarily stopped pricing or returned with higher rates. That is an internal warning level based on observed behaviour, not a formal repricing threshold, and the W-26 offered range has now moved through it at 183.00p.

The market does not need to rise again tomorrow for customers to pay more tomorrow. Wholesale already rises, the supplier book has not caught up, the supplier reprices later, and the customer receives a higher quote. Once a supplier withdraws a rate, the customer cannot go back and secure yesterday's price.
Supplier price-book lag is the opportunity and the danger. Wholesale markets move first. Supplier books often follow. A wholesale move does not translate pound-for-pound into the customer's total energy bill, but the direction of travel in the offered range is already clear.
