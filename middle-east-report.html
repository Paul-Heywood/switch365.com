Europe's gas reserves are shrinking. Colder weather and low wind could make matters worse.

Europe's winter buffer is already unusually thin. A sustained cold spell, especially if accompanied by weaker wind generation, could increase competition for gas just as the forward market is offering lower prices.

Europe enters the latest cold spell with less gas in reserve than normal. Official GIE data show EU underground storage at 68.41%, with Germany only 55.90%, for the 23 November gas day. The latest Met Office forecast confirms cold conditions and overnight frost risk across parts of Britain. Lower temperatures tend to increase heating demand, forcing Europe to rely more heavily on withdrawals from storage and continuing pipeline and LNG imports.

Electricity adds a second possible pressure point. If wind generation weakens, gas-fired power stations may have to produce more electricity, increasing gas demand just when heating requirements are higher. But this is a conditional risk, not a claim that British wind output has collapsed today: current NESO/Elexon generation and forecast data must be checked, along with nuclear availability, imports and demand. Meanwhile Qatar LNG export constraints and persistent maritime risks reduce some flexibility, although a threat is not the same as lost supply.

SECTION 5

Futures Market Explained

NBP versus TTF

NBP is the UK wholesale gas hub, priced in pence per therm; TTF is the Dutch hub that anchors continental Europe, priced in euros per MWh. They usually move together because the UK and continent share pipeline and LNG supply, but they are different instruments and do not move identically. TTF front-month indications were around €91/MWh on Wednesday morning after Monday's intraday spike towards €96; the Switch365 W-26 working reference is a separate internal indicator and is never substituted by an exchange quote.

Spot, front month, seasons

Day-ahead prices cover gas delivered tomorrow. Front-month contracts cover the nearest full month. Seasonal contracts (Summer 27, Winter 27 and so on) cover six-month delivery blocks. A live intraday quote is not a settlement, and a settlement is not a supplier rate. Prices for 2028 delivery differ from winter 2026 because storage, LNG expansion and demand expectations differ by period; that gap is backwardation, not a promised future spot price.

Why oil and gas can diverge

The same day can see Brent fall while gas forwards rally, or the reverse, because the balances differ. Oil is reacting to recovering Gulf crude exports and surplus forecasts; UK gas reacts more directly to Qatar LNG, TTF, Norwegian flows, storage and weather. Brent is not the customer's gas quote. LNG is the bridge between Hormuz and UK gas.

From screen to supplier book

A wholesale move is not an executable customer rate. Supplier books reprice with a lag, and a quoted rate carries a timestamp, validity window, product rules and credit conditions. No forward-curve value guarantees a future spot price, and no comparison of two delivery seasons produces a total-bill savings percentage.

SECTION 14

Ceasefire Reality Check

Diplomatic contact is not a ceasefire, and a ceasefire announcement is not verified safe passage. An intercepted missile is not a confirmed energy-infrastructure hit, and a hit is not necessarily lost production. An improvement in security can reduce a risk premium before damaged infrastructure is repaired, but physical flows are the supply test.

  • Has any signed, operative agreement replaced the November diplomatic signalling (G7 statements, Qatar's Paris briefing, Iran's conditional proposals)? No signed ceasefire is verified in this research run.
  • Do UKMTO and ship operators confirm repeatable, insured, safe LNG-carrier and tanker passage through Hormuz, the Gulf of Aden and Bab al-Mandeb? Partial transits continue below pre-war pace; incidents and claims persist.
  • Have Qatar's actual loadings and export tonnage risen sustainably, or do US spot cargoes continue to compensate? Force majeure reportedly stands to end-2026; one successful crossing is not recovery. Qatar needs predictable safe passage, not occasional successful crossings.
  • Is Norwegian gas running without new outages, are US LNG deliveries continuing, and is European storage in withdrawal mode? Storage is withdrawing (about 2.8 TWh a day around 22 November on third-party tracking); Norway and US LNG remain the bearish buffer to verify daily.
  • Is falling Brent accompanied by lower TTF/NBP and real supplier books, or only oil-market relief? This week's internal sheets show supplier-season indicators falling; wholesale screens and named supplier books still need same-day verification.

Never assume price direction from the geopolitical narrative alone.

Shipping Risk, Route by Route

Hormuz

Primary chokepoint: about 93% of Qatar's and 96% of the UAE's LNG exports transit the strait, roughly a fifth of global LNG trade (IEA). Partial Qatari transits have resumed below the pre-war pace of roughly three LNG shipments a day. Monday's tanker-mine reports remain Iranian media claims pending UKMTO, operator and tracker confirmation. Earlier Hormuz oil-flow figures conflict (about -32% on one Kpler total-clearance baseline versus about -18% on a different baseline); neither is republished as a verified current figure until denominators are reconciled.

Gulf of Oman

Staging area for shuttling tactics and the Fujairah bypass (Abu Dhabi Crude Oil Pipeline, about 1.8 mb/d current capacity). No new verified incident overnight; earlier tanker strike west of Bukha remains dated context with crew safe and fire extinguished.

Persian Gulf

Iranian claims of vessels catching fire in the northern Gulf remain claims. Vessel-by-vessel verification (name, IMO, cargo, laden or ballast, damage, flag state) is required before any count changes. Do not double-count strike, aftermath and sinking of the same ship.

Gulf of Aden

Shabwa province (site of the earlier security-camp missile attack) contains the Balhaf LNG complex and borders the Gulf of Aden. The attack occurred in a province containing Yemen's Balhaf LNG complex, increasing its energy-security relevance without proving direct LNG infrastructure damage. Balhaf security risk is not Balhaf supply loss.

Bab al-Mandeb

Houthi capability keeps this corridor under daily watch. The security risk zone is not confined to Hormuz. Egypt's Red Sea interests and Turkey's navigation-protection discussions raise the corridor's strategic weight.

Red Sea

As Hormuz becomes less reliable, the Red Sea route becomes more valuable and more strategic: Saudi crude can exit west via the East-West pipeline to Yanbu. Houthi attacks remain a live risk; Turkey and Egypt are moving closer to defensive roles.

Yanbu / Red Sea Corridor

Yanbu bypasses Hormuz for Saudi crude. A verified attack removing East-West pipeline, Yanbu loading, terminal or refinery capacity would materially alter the market; current status: claimed targets, no verified capacity loss in cited evidence.

LNG Carriers

An LNG security incident matters more to UK gas than an unrelated general-cargo incident. For every incident: vessel type, laden or ballast, origin, destination, cargo owner, damage and whether passage was interrupted. No LNG-carrier loss is verified in this research run.

Tanker War-Risk Insurance

A route can remain open and still carry a substantial war-risk premium. Friction gets priced. Even where oil cargo volume finds alternative paths, routing, insurance and freight can remain abnormal; differentiate reduced physical supply from elevated delivered cost.

SECTION 17

Supplier Price-Book Lag

Wholesale markets move first. Supplier books often follow. This week's internal sheets show offered seasons falling twice in succession, yet a seasonal internal indicator and a customer's eligible, signed, executable quote are different things. Check CED, future start, supplier product, rate validity, whole-bill costs, kWh, standing charge, credit, broker fees and pass-through or reopener terms before treating any screen move as an available tariff.

Supplier price-book lag is the opportunity and the danger. Once a supplier withdraws a rate, the customer cannot go back and secure yesterday's price. Waiting is not neutral: waiting only works if the market moves in the customer's favour, and low storage plus cold weather is exactly the backdrop against which falling prices can reverse.
