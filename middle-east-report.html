Middle East Energy Risk Update

UK Gas, UK Power, Futures Market and Procurement Position
Report date: 29/07/2026 Research window: 28/07/2026 – 29/07/2026 Extended verification: 23/07/2026 – 29/07/2026
Reuters · 28–29 Jul AP · 29 Jul CENTCOM / Saudi MoD · 29 Jul Trading Economics · 29 Jul Internal broker / supplier evidence · 28 Jul AM
Yesterday was relief pricing. This morning the pause has cracked.

Iran launched ballistic missiles at US military installations in Jordan, and Jordanian air defences intercepted them — US Central Command said all the missiles were intercepted in what Washington called an “attempted surprise attack”, and Jordanian state media said Jordan shot down five missiles coming from Iran. Within hours, the US and Saudi Arabia struck sites in eastern Iraq that they said were used by Iran-backed groups to direct drone attacks on Saudi oil facilities. Associated Press described the exchanges as “shattering a brief pause in fighting”.

Hormuz remains unresolved. Tehran has ruled out Oman’s proposal for regional joint management of the strait, telling Reuters it has “no chance of success”, while the IRGC claimed — not independently verified — to have struck and stopped three oil tankers. Tanker traffic remains a fraction of normal, and the Red Sea has deteriorated in parallel, with the Houthis claiming an attack on a Saudi tanker and a self-declared blockade of Saudi shipping through Bab el-Mandeb.

Oil has bounced after three sessions of falls. Brent settled at $84.09/bbl on 28 July after falling from $100.69 on 23 July, then rebounded roughly 3.7–3.9% to around $87/bbl on 29 July as the interception and strikes landed. NBP and TTF front contracts are also edging back up.

The cleaner wording is not simply “Iran attacked Jordan”. The cleaner wording is that Iran launched ballistic missiles at US forces in Jordan, and Jordanian air defences intercepted them.

Customers should not read lower oil as lower business energy automatically. The energy bill conversation is about NBP, TTF, UK power, supplier books, contract end date and forward delivery period. The internal curve supplied this morning still shows deep backwardation — gas S-28 to S-30 is priced 50.0% to 55.8% below W-26, and power S-28 / S-29 sits 44.1% to 47.4% below W-26. That gap is the opportunity, and supplier price-book lag is still both the opportunity and the danger.

Quick read for the sales team

  • Yesterday was relief pricing. This morning the pause has cracked. Three down sessions in oil ended with a ~3.8% Brent rebound on 29 July.
  • This is not full normalisation. It is relief pricing being challenged by fresh kinetic escalation.
  • Customers waiting because oil came off the highs are missing the point. Wholesale moved first — supplier books may still follow.
  • Once suppliers withdraw rates, customers cannot go back and secure previous pricing.
  • This is a review-now window, not a wait-and-see market. Use the % vs W-26 column to prove the forward-value gap on every call today.

Futures Market Explained

Why the delivery period matters more than today’s headline price.

The futures market shows the price for energy delivered in future periods. A customer renewing from 2027, 2028, 2029 or 2030 is not only interested in today’s price. They should look at what the market is currently offering for their actual delivery period.

Right now the curve is in backwardation: the front (W-26) carries the war-risk premium from Hormuz, the Jordan interception and the Iraq strikes, while later delivery periods are offered at large discounts. That is visible in the tables above — gas S-29 at -55.8% vs W-26, power S-29 at -47.4%.

Cheaper further out does not mean cheaper later.
The future market is only useful while the customer can still buy that future delivery period.
Waiting lets the market make the decision for the customer.
As time passes, that future period becomes the near-term market and can absorb the same risk premium.

The practical point for every conversation today: a cheaper 2028 or 2029 price is not a forecast of what the customer will pay if they wait. It is a live offer that exists now, and it can be repriced or withdrawn the moment supplier books catch up with this morning’s escalation.

Market Drivers

The verified forces moving UK gas, UK power and oil today.

1 · Jordan interception — the pause cracked 28–29 Jul

US Central Command said it intercepted multiple Iranian ballistic missiles launched towards US forces in the Middle East in an “attempted surprise attack”. The IRGC said it fired several ballistic missiles at a US air base and a US CENTCOM centre in Jordan; Axios reported the target was a US base in Jordan. Jordanian state media said Jordan shot down five missiles coming from Iran. No US casualties were reported.

2 · US / Saudi strikes in Iraq 28–29 Jul

CENTCOM and the Saudi armed forces struck what Washington called Iran-backed militant logistics and weapons sites across eastern Iraq, in response to “over 30 IRGC-directed aerial drone attacks in the last 72 hours”. A US official said the strikes came after the missile barrage and the two were not connected. It is the first time Riyadh has taken responsibility for strikes inside Iraq since the war began; Iraq’s Popular Mobilisation Forces reported several killed and wounded and called it a violation of sovereignty. A specific death toll remains unverified.

3 · Saudi drone interceptions & denials 27–29 Jul

Saudi Arabia said it intercepted and destroyed drones targeting Eastern Province oil facilities — and Riyadh — for a second consecutive day, blaming Iran-backed groups operating from Iraqi airspace. The Houthis separately claimed drone strikes on Saudi oil infrastructure; IRGC-affiliated media claimed a fire at Abqaiq, which Saudi officials have not confirmed. Iran denied involvement and said attributing the attacks to Tehran was a “major miscalculation”.

4 · Hormuz impasse 29 Jul

Iran ruled out Oman’s proposal for regional joint management of the strait — “no chance of success”, a senior Iranian official told Reuters — one day after Qatar- and Pakistan-led mediation was described as making “significant” progress. The IRGC claimed to have struck and stopped three oil tankers; that claim is not independently verified. Ship-tracking shows ~19 transits on 28 July versus 63 on 3 July, with mainline operators staying away.

5 · Red Sea second chokepoint 27–29 Jul

The Houthis claimed a missile attack on the Saudi tanker NCC Ghazal, saying it was forced to turn back, under a self-declared blockade of Saudi shipping; UKMTO reported a tanker hearing explosions in the southern Red Sea with crew safe. Bab el-Mandeb tonnage is down ~50%, Saudi crude is being rerouted via Egypt’s Sidi Kerir (SUMED), and at least one Indian refiner is tendering on avoidance of both the Red Sea and Hormuz.

6 · Oil, storage and the money 21–29 Jul

Brent briefly hit $102 last week, fell three sessions to $84.09, then rebounded ~3.8% on 29 July; US gasoline is $4.11/gal. EU-27 gas storage at 57.7 Bcm is 12.0 Bcm below last year, tightening Europe’s cushion into winter. Hegseth put the war’s cost at $37.5bn so far, defending an $87.6bn supplemental request inside a wider ~$95bn package; the House narrowly passed a war-powers resolution last week.

Kazakhstan / CPC / Black Sea: no fresh verified development in this research window — it remains a secondary supply-route issue and has dropped down the priority list. No fresh NESO Electricity Margin Notice or Gassco Norwegian outage was verified in this window.

Shipping Risk

Two chokepoints under pressure at the same time — the physical backbone of the price story.

Strait of Hormuz Unresolved

Status: legally open, effectively constrained. Iran demands ships use a route near its coastline under its own authority and has said it can charge fees; the US disputes Iranian control. Iran has now rejected Oman’s regional joint-management plan.

Traffic: ~19 transits on 28 July versus 63 on 3 July, skewed to Iranian-flagged and small regional craft; only one VLCC completed transit among limited international movements. Wartime commodity-vessel average has run at roughly 55 per week — a fraction of the pre-war norm on a waterway that carries about a fifth of the world’s traded oil and ~20% of global LNG. No LNG tankers were visible entering at the last weekend checkpoint.

Claims vs fact: the IRGC says it “struck and stopped” three oil tankers on 29 July after they ignored warnings. Not independently verified in this research run — treat as a claim. Earlier July attacks on tankers (7, 12, 14, 20 July) are documented.

Bab el-Mandeb / Red Sea Deteriorating

Status: the Houthis have declared a blockade of Saudi shipping and claimed a missile attack on the Saudi tanker NCC Ghazal, saying it was forced to turn back. UKMTO reported a tanker hearing explosions in the southern Red Sea; crew and vessel safe.

Impact: Bab el-Mandeb tonnage down ~50%; Saudi crude being rerouted via Egypt’s Sidi Kerir terminal (SUMED pipeline) with AIS suppression reported at Yanbu loadings; at least one Indian refiner (MRPL) tendering on explicit avoidance of both the Red Sea and Hormuz.

Why it matters for the UK: Suez/SUMED and Yanbu are the relief valves for Gulf supply that cannot use Hormuz. If both chokepoints are constrained at once, freight, insurance and delivery-risk premia feed straight into the oil and LNG complex that sets the marginal UK gas and power price.

Shipping confidence: low and falling. Operators were already going dark (AIS off) through Hormuz, and insurance has “remained available throughout” only at elevated cost. There is no verified return to normal scheduling by any mainline carrier in this research window.

Supplier Price-Book Lag

Why the gap between wholesale and quoted rates is today’s biggest opportunity — and biggest danger.
Wholesale moved first. Supplier books may still follow.

Supplier price books do not reprint in real time. Yesterday’s relief pricing pulled wholesale offers down — internal gas W-26 -6.25 p/th and power W-26 -£4.00/MWh on the day — and supplier books were only part-way through reflecting that when this morning’s escalation landed. Supplier price-book lag is still the opportunity and the danger.

The opportunity: books priced off the relief window can still carry yesterday’s softer assumptions for hours or days, while the forward curve remains deeply backwardated — gas S-28 to S-30 at -50.0% to -55.8% vs W-26, power S-28 / S-29 at -44.1% to -47.4%. Customers quoted now can still catch that gap.

The danger: the same lag works in reverse. As desks absorb the Jordan interception, the Iraq strikes and the ~3.8% Brent rebound, books will be repriced, quote validity shortened, or rates withdrawn entirely. Once suppliers withdraw rates, customers cannot go back and secure previous pricing.

Operating rule for today: any quote generated this morning is perishable. Get decisions inside the validity window, and do not promise customers that yesterday’s prices will still exist tomorrow.
