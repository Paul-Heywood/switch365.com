01

Summary

What changed over the weekend that could change the price a UK business can actually secure today.

Saudi Arabia says drone attacks originating from Iraq forced the temporary shutdown of its East-West oil pipeline, the roughly 1,200km link that has carried crude across the kingdom to Yanbu on the Red Sea since Hormuz became severely disrupted. The line had recently been moving around 4 million barrels a day, roughly 4 to 5 percent of global oil supply. Industry sources estimate Yanbu holds only five to seven days of exportable stocks if flows do not resume, and repair estimates range from a relatively quick partial restart to several weeks. The precise chain of responsibility remains under investigation: Riyadh and Baghdad both trace the drones to Iraqi territory, Iraq has dismissed the Maysan operations commander after launchers were found near the border, and President Trump said he believes Iran was probably responsible.

In the same week, Houthi forces completed a rapid advance down Yemen's Red Sea coast. Mocha fell on Thursday, Dhubab was taken on Friday and government forces withdrew from Perim, the island that sits inside Bab al-Mandeb itself, according to Yemeni government sources. That is materially more important than fighting near the strait. Even so, Bab al-Mandeb is still physically flowing: weekend tracking showed traffic close to its recent average, and the Houthi claim that navigation is safe for all but Saudi vessels is a claim, not a commercial guarantee.

Hormuz remained severely impaired across the weekend, with only single-digit daily commodity transits against a recent ten-day average near 14, and UKMTO reported a vessel struck by an unknown projectile that caught fire before the crew was evacuated. The Gulf-Iran meeting on Hormuz shipping planned for today in Salalah has been postponed, with no replacement date. On gas, QatarEnergy is reportedly negotiating multi-year US LNG supply contracts through 2031, a clear shift from disruption management to duration planning. NBP and TTF both reopened higher this morning, and with internal offered W-26 gas at 211.00p/therm and W-26 power at £167.50/MWh on Monday morning's snapshot, the question for UK businesses is no longer whether wholesale has moved, but how much far-curve value survives as books reprice the weekend.

What it means for UK energy

Today's hero is an oil, shipping and route-redundancy story, not a direct UK gas supply cut: Brent is not the customer's gas quote. But the transmission into UK business energy is real. LNG remains the bridge between Hormuz and UK gas, European storage is already weak, Qatari replacement cargoes are being competed for globally, and TTF and NBP both gapped higher this morning. Wholesale has already moved. The question now is when supplier books catch up, and Friday's internal offered ranges may not survive the week unchanged.

05

Futures Market Explained

Why the shape of today's curve is a procurement signal, not a reason to wait.

Business energy is bought from the forward market. The price for winter 2026 delivery and the price for summer 2030 delivery are different products trading at different levels, and today they are extraordinarily far apart.

Today's market is in severe backwardation: the front winter carries the war, the damaged Qatari trains, the shut Saudi pipeline and the weak storage position, while later periods are priced on the assumption that some of this normalises. On Monday's internal books, gas falls from 211.00p (W-26) to 66.74p (S-30) and power from £167.50 to £66.86. That gap is not a forecast that energy will be cheap in 2030. It is the price available today for 2030 delivery.

Cheaper further out does not mean cheaper later. Backwardation does not mean wait. The future market is only useful while the customer can still buy that future delivery period, and waiting gives the market time to remove today's forward-value opportunity.
Waiting is not neutral. If the front winter stays elevated, suppliers progressively re-mark the far curve upwards, as Monday's +8.00p jump on W-26 gas and the firming of every power row showed. If the war ends, the front falls fastest and the gap closes from the top. Either way, the customer who needed the far period and did not secure it loses the advantage that exists on the screen this morning.

The commercial question is never "will prices be lower next year?". It is: can the customer secure the cheaper forward period now? That is a question about live supplier books, quote validity and delivery-period availability, which is exactly what Sections 7, 8 and 17 address.

Procurement Window

Where each contract position stands this morning, and what to do about it.

This is a review-now window, not a wait-and-see market.

The internal Monday snapshot shows the front end repricing the weekend, with W-26 gas up 8.00p to 211.00p and W-26 power up £3.50 to £167.50, while far-curve gas and power remain at deep discounts to the front. Wholesale and the front of the book have moved together; the far curve is the remaining lag. Supplier price-book lag is the opportunity and the danger. Far-curve rows priced off calmer assumptions are still live this morning; they may not be by Wednesday.

By position

  • Inside 90 days: you are buying the front of the curve in a crisis market. Review live pricing now and secure where commercially appropriate; the risk of further repricing while you wait outweighs the chance of catching a dip.
  • Inside 12 months: do not wait for renewal panic. Your renewal date does not oblige you to buy W-26; check today's future-start pricing against your actual contract end date, because S-27 gas is 34.6 percent below the front.
  • British Gas customers inside 18 months: the extended renewal window means you may be able to lock a future-start contract today. See Section 8.
  • High usage, outside 12 months: the far curve is the story. S-28 gas at 81.75p and S-29 power at £66.86 are the kind of levels that disappear when books reprice a prolonged-disruption scenario.
  • 2027 to 2030 contract end dates: compare your actual delivery period against W-26 and against 1 Year Ago. Your delivery period matters more than the headline.
The market does not need to rise again tomorrow for customers to pay more tomorrow. It only needs suppliers to finish repricing what has already happened.

British Gas 18-Month Window

Internal Switch365 evidence Internal

British Gas has extended its business energy renewal window from 12 months to 18 months. This gives eligible customers an earlier chance to review future-start pricing.

The 18-month British Gas window matters because it turns the forward market into a practical customer action. A customer whose contract ends in, for example, late 2027 can in many cases be priced today against the S-27 and W-27 periods that sit 33 to 37 percent below the front on both fuels, rather than waiting for the renewal letter to arrive in a market that may have re-marked those periods upwards.

  • Your new contract would not start today. It would begin when your current agreement ends. There is no double-paying and no early exit from the existing deal.
  • Eligibility is not automatic. Not every customer is inside the window or eligible for every product, so the first step is checking eligibility and live pricing rather than assuming availability.
  • The window converts analysis into action. British Gas 18-month eligibility turns this market intelligence into a practical customer action: the far-curve discounts in Sections 3 and 4 become securable, not just observable.
Waiting is not neutral. If the market moves or British Gas withdraws rates, customers cannot go back and secure today's pricing later. Once a supplier withdraws a rate, the customer cannot go back and secure yesterday's price.

Ranked by their power to move UK business gas and electricity prices this week.

1. Qatar LNG / Hormuz impairment

Bullish gas

What happened: Hormuz remains severely impaired, QatarEnergy force majeure notices run into November, and Reuters reports QatarEnergy negotiating US LNG contracts through 2031. Confidence: high on impairment and the 2031 talks (Reuters, multi-source); the repair timeline is an estimate. Why UK businesses should care: LNG remains the bridge between Hormuz and UK gas. Around 12.8 mtpa of Qatari capacity is damaged, and roughly 21.5 bcm of EU-contracted Qatari volumes for 2026 are exposed (ACER). This is the number one UK gas fundamental.

2. NBP / TTF at crisis-era levels

Bullish

What happened: the public NBP quarterly series closed Friday at 201.06p after touching 208.69p, TTF closed at €79.52, and both gapped higher Monday morning (TTF ≈ €83.1, +4.5%; NBP front series ≈ 204.9p). Confidence: high (exchange and vendor prints). Why it matters: wholesale has already moved; supplier books are the lagging edge of the same move. Note Friday itself proved extreme geopolitical risk does not force prices higher every day.

3. European storage

Bullish

What happened: EU storage stood at 67.63% full on 11 September (GIE AGSI), roughly 14 to 19 points below the five-year seasonal norm, with Germany at 55.29% and the Netherlands at 51.4%. Confidence: high (GIE). Why it matters: storage is a buffer, not immunity. Europe does not need to run out of gas for low storage to increase winter pricing; a thin buffer means every new supply scare lands harder.

4. Norway

Bearish buffer

What happened: no major new unplanned Norwegian outage surfaced in this research run; the Gassco system continues to supply around 30% of EU gas imports. Confidence: medium-high (no adverse Gassco reporting found; formal nominations check recommended intraday). Why it matters: strong Norwegian supply materially cushions missing LNG and is the single biggest reason TTF is not higher still.

5. US / replacement LNG

Bearish buffer

What happened: US LNG feedgas averaged 17.3 Bcf/d in August, up 8.4% year on year, with Golden Pass started up and exports near records (EIA/Rystad via AGA). Confidence: high. Why it matters: US LNG remains one of the strongest bearish buffers against Qatar disruption, and QatarEnergy itself reportedly wants 2 to 3 mtpa of it through 2031. The buffer exists, but Asia is bidding for the same cargoes.

6. Supplier price books

Mixed / very high risk

What happened: Monday's internal snapshot showed the front jumping (W-26 gas +8.00p to 211.00p, W-26 power +£3.50 to £167.50) while far-curve discounts of 34 to 68 percent remain on the board. Internal experience: when wholesale neared 180p, some suppliers pulled books, suspended pricing and reissued higher. Confidence: high on internal evidence. Why it matters: this is where wholesale movement becomes the customer's actual price.

7. Saudi pipeline / Bab al-Mandeb / shipping friction

Bullish oil · mixed gas

What happened: the East-West pipeline is shut, Yanbu has an estimated 5 to 7 days of export stocks, and Houthis hold Dhubab and Perim. Tanker rates reached record levels last week and war-risk premiums on Gulf transits have been quoted at 7.5 to 10 percent of hull value (Marsh, July). Confidence: high on the shutdown and territorial sequence (Reuters, satellite imagery); medium on the stock estimate (industry sources). Why it matters: a route does not need to close for the cost of energy to rise. Friction gets priced, and the oil move bleeds into energy sentiment and inflation even though Brent is not the customer's gas quote.

8. US-Iran duration

Mixed

What happened: the war is in its seventh month. Trump says it will end "immediately after the election" while his advisers are confronting the possibility it lasts through his term (WSJ). The Oman meeting postponement removes this week's de-escalation channel. Confidence: statements verified; outcomes unknowable. Why it matters: the evidence increasingly supports planning for prolonged disruption, but it does not tell us when political pressure will finally produce an agreement.

9. Weather / demand

Bearish near-term

What happened: UK temperatures run above the seasonal mean to 17 September, and wind plus solar is forecast well above norm from Tuesday after a weak weekend. Confidence: medium (forecast). Why it matters: mild, windy weather caps near-term gas-for-power demand; it does nothing for the winter risk premium embedded in W-26.

10. Brent above $100

Mixed

What happened: Brent settled Friday at $104.61 and traded around $106.8 Monday morning with an intraday high of $108.46. Confidence: high. Why it matters: oil above $100 feeds inflation, freight and sentiment, and the physical market is splitting between oil that can move freely and oil trapped behind high-risk chokepoints. It influences NBP psychologically; it does not set it.

Top Five Warning Signs

Ranked by potential to move the price a UK business can secure this week.

1

The pipeline outage outlives Yanbu's stocks

If East-West flows do not restart within the estimated five to seven day stock window, Saudi Red Sea exports begin physically falling and the threatened 4 percent of global supply starts becoming real rather than hypothetical. Watch repair statements, pumping-station assessments and Yanbu tanker nominations daily. The repair timeline is uncertain, with industry estimates ranging from a relatively quick partial restart to several weeks.

2

Houthi positions around Bab al-Mandeb turn into action

Holding Dhubab and Perim gives the Houthis a dramatically stronger ability to contest the strait. Their statement that navigation is safe "except for Saudi vessels" is a claim, not a guarantee, but claimed selective safe passage is not a commercial guarantee either way. A confirmed attack on a Saudi-bound tanker this week would reprice the Red Sea exit that Saudi exports now depend on.

3

Supplier books reprice or suspend while the curve is mispriced

Internal evidence shows books were pulled when wholesale neared 180p; the front is now above 200p and TTF gapped 4.5 percent higher this morning. The far-curve discounts visible on Friday's snapshot are exactly the rows most exposed to withdrawal. Watch for shortened quote validity, suspended products and reissued higher books.

4

European storage stalls below the refill trajectory

At 67.63 percent full on 11 September, the EU is tracking well below the five-year norm with roughly seven weeks to the relaxed 80 percent November target. Any Norwegian outage, cold snap or further Asian bid for US cargoes turns a thin buffer into a pricing event. Storage is a buffer, not immunity.

5

The diplomatic track seizes up entirely

The Salalah postponement has no replacement date, Iran's conditions for reopening Hormuz remain unresolved, and Washington is signalling it will not open a second front against the Houthis. If the Oman channel collapses rather than slips, the market loses its only live de-escalation path heading into the heating season.

What Changed

The structural shift over the weekend, stated plainly.

Until now

Hormuz disrupted → Saudi Arabia moves oil west → East-West pipeline → Yanbu → Red Sea → Bab al-Mandeb → global market.

Over the weekend

Hormuz disrupted → East-West pipeline attacked → pipeline shut → Yanbu draws down inventories → Houthis control stronger positions around Bab al-Mandeb → shipping and insurance risk rises around the Red Sea exit.

The workaround has now been physically hit. The energy system has lost another layer of redundancy. That is today's central market story.

And in gas, the duration story deepened

Qatar LNG infrastructure remains damaged → Qatar seeks replacement US LNG → contracts potentially through 2031 → European storage remains weak → NBP and TTF remain at crisis-era levels → supplier books remain exposed to repricing. The market is moving from disruption management to duration planning (Switch365 analysis).

What has NOT changed

  • Bab al-Mandeb remains physically active: 24 tracked vessels Saturday, 27 Sunday, against a recent average around 27.
  • The outage may prove temporary; the worst-case repair estimate is not confirmed, and Yanbu holds stored inventory.
  • The US has not directly joined the Houthi fight; Oman diplomacy is delayed, not abandoned.
  • Norway and US LNG still cushion European gas; the Saudi pipeline is primarily an oil driver and NBP does not have to follow Brent.
  • Supplier books do not have to move exactly with wholesale, which is precisely the window this report exists to exploit.

Ceasefire Reality Check

Where the diplomatic track actually stands, versus where headlines imply it stands.

The June memorandum between Washington and Tehran required a final agreement within 60 days; that window closed on 17 August with no deal. Fighting resumed, the ceasefire language was dropped, and the old June claims of a completed deal and a reopened Hormuz have failed to normalise anything. Do not treat them as current evidence.

This morning the picture is: the Salalah meeting postponed with no new date; Iran linking any Hormuz framework to US conditions and its own demands; Washington ruling out direct action against the Houthis while blockading Iranian ports; and Trump publicly expecting an end after the November midterms while his own advisers confront a war that could last through his term. A late-August Reuters/Ipsos poll showed only 31 percent of Americans support the strikes, pressure that cuts both ways: it pushes Washington towards an exit and pushes Tehran towards holding out.

A shorter period of bombing would not necessarily mean a shorter energy crisis. Repairing pipelines, LNG plants, inventories and commercial shipping confidence can take longer than stopping the shooting. Even a signed deal this autumn would leave Qatar's damaged trains, the pipeline repair, war-risk premiums and Europe's storage deficit in place into winter.
The evidence increasingly supports planning for prolonged disruption, but it does not tell us when political pressure will finally produce an agreement. Plan procurement around what is securable today, not around a diplomatic date that does not yet exist.

Shipping Risk

Route by route. Not every route is closed; every route is repriced.

Route 1 · Strait of Hormuz Severe impairment

Four tracked commodity vessels exited and ten entered across the weekend against a recent ten-day average near 14 transits a day. A vessel was struck by an unknown projectile late Saturday and caught fire (UKMTO 134-26). Counts exclude AIS-dark vessels: visible vessel count and physical energy volume are different measures.

Route 2 · East-West Pipeline Temporarily shut

Saudi Arabia's 1,200km workaround, carrying around 4 million bpd to Yanbu, shut after Thursday's drone attacks. Repair estimates range from a relatively quick partial restart to several weeks. The duration of the outage now matters more than the fact of the attack itself.

Route 3 · Yanbu / Bab al-Mandeb Operating, risk higher

Traffic still flowing near recent averages, but Houthis now hold Mocha, Dhubab and Perim. Around 8 percent of world oil passes the strait (EIA). War-risk premiums for southern Red Sea voyages were already quoted at 1 to 2 percent of hull value in July and are likely to be repriced.

Route 4 · Egypt / SUMED Limited alternative

Saudi stocks are accessible via Ain Sukhna and the SUMED pipeline to Sidi Kerir, and Kpler tracked rising charterer interest in Sidi Kerir loadings. Capacity and stored volumes are finite; this cushions weeks, not months.

Route 5 · UAE eastern ports Alternative for UAE supply

Fujairah sits outside Hormuz and continues to offer the UAE an outlet. It does nothing for Qatari LNG or Saudi Gulf crude, and its capacity cannot absorb the region's displaced volumes.

Route 6 · Cape of Good Hope Longer and expensive

The release valve: rerouting around Africa can add roughly 22 days to some journeys, and bunker fuel has become scarce and expensive. Tanker rates reached record levels last week. Friction gets priced: a route can remain physically open and still carry a material war-risk premium.

Two chokepoints, one system. Hormuz remains the immediate physical bottleneck. Bab al-Mandeb is the growing second-chokepoint risk. The East-West pipeline outage weakens the bridge between the two. Energy availability is now as much a logistics problem as a production problem, with freely moveable barrels trading at widening premiums to barrels trapped behind high-risk chokepoints.

Supplier Price-Book Lag

Major commercial priority. Internal Switch365 evidence Internal

Wholesale has already moved. The question now is when supplier books catch up.

Switch365 internal experience is direct: when wholesale gas previously reached around 180p/therm, some suppliers pulled books, suspended pricing and reissued higher. That is internal commercial evidence, not a formal industry threshold, and the public NBP quarterly series has since moved above 200p with Monday's front prints around 205p and TTF at €83. Monday's internal snapshot confirms books have begun to follow, with W-26 gas up 8.00p to 211.00p. The lag between that wholesale reality and the remaining far-curve discounts is where today's customer value sits.

What Monday's snapshot showed

  • Gas: the front jumped hard (W-26 +8.00 to 211.00p, W-27 +2.75, S-30 +1.70) while W-28 and W-29 actually eased (−1.00 and −0.17). The far curve is still lagging the front's reprice.
  • Power: every row firmed on Monday, W-26 +£3.50 to £167.50 and W-28 +£1.91. The far curve is now moving too, but S-29 at £66.86 is still 60.1 percent below the front.

The priority question this morning

Monday's book has started to catch up (W-26 gas +8.00p). The question now is how much further repricing runs this week. Check before quoting: which books are held, pulled, repriced or suspended; quote validity windows; acceptance deadlines; any terms removed overnight; and any new or withdrawn products. Internal checks take priority over public commentary here.

Supplier price-book lag is the opportunity and the danger. Wholesale markets move first. Supplier books often follow. The market does not need to rise again tomorrow for customers to pay more tomorrow, and once a supplier withdraws a rate, the customer cannot go back and secure yesterday's price.
