Section 05

Futures Market Explained

Why a forward curve can fall on Tuesday, split on Wednesday, and still not tell you what your business will pay.

How the Curve Works

UK gas trades at the National Balancing Point (NBP) and European gas at the Dutch TTF, in pence per therm and euros per MWh. You can buy gas for next month (the front month, currently December 2026), for a quarter, or for a season: Summer 2027 (S-27) means April to September 2027 delivery, Winter 2027 (W-27) means October 2027 to March 2028, and so on down the curve to W-30.

Today's curve is in steep backwardation: the front sits far above later seasons. That shape says the market is paying a large premium for gas delivered during the current risk window, and far less for delivery in 2028-2030. Backwardation does not mean wait. It means the market currently believes the risk is concentrated near term; it says nothing certain about what prices will be when those later periods arrive.

Last trade vs settlement

An ICE settlement is a weighted average price from a defined 16:05-16:15 London window. A "last" quote on a data page is simply the most recent trade, which can be hours old. This report never splices a last trade with a settlement and calls it a day-on-day move.

Why Tuesday's Fall and Wednesday's Split Can Both Be Real

Seasonal offered prices can fall while shipping risks rise, because forward seasons price expected average conditions over a six-month delivery window, not this week's headlines. If suppliers expect US LNG, Norwegian pipeline gas and demand destruction to cover lost Qatari cargoes by 2028, the S-28 offer can fall even while the front-month jumps on a tanker attack.

Wednesday's split adds a twist: the 2027 seasons kept falling while S-28 to W-30 gas ticked up and W-26 rebounded 1.40%. That is consistent with a market pulling risk premium out of next summer while re-pricing both the immediate winter and the longer balance. It is not a signal to chase or to freeze; it is a signal that different delivery periods are answering different questions.

From wholesale to your bill

Suppliers buy along this curve, add their operating costs, margin, and non-commodity charges, then publish rate books. Wholesale markets move first. Supplier books often follow, with a lag that can work for or against the customer. A forward price is not a forecast of what buying in that year will cost, and a W-26 working reference is not a tariff.

Cheaper further out does not mean cheaper later. Lower 2028-2030 indicators are an opportunity to review eligible future-start terms against customer-specific costs and risk appetite, not a promise about the future.

  • Named suppliers withdrawing or repricing rate books off the back of Wednesday's 1.40% front-end rebound.

Conditions That Could Make Prices Fall Further

  • The 2027 seasons kept falling on Wednesday even as the front rose, showing sellers still expect supply to normalise into next summer.
  • Verified restoration and stability of Norwegian flows after the Visund fire and Troll's partial outage; strong Norway materially cushions missing LNG.
  • US LNG: record feedgas and Europe-bound cargoes, with US storage about 2% above normal and EIA forecasting Henry Hub to average $2.87/MMBtu this winter, a bearish global counterweight.
  • An oil market returning to surplus: Brent eased early Wednesday as US crude stocks rose, and the IEA sees Q4 supply length despite the conflict.
  • Diplomatic movement that turns into verified, repeatable, insured safe passage; an improvement in security can reduce the risk premium before damaged infrastructure is repaired.

How to Use This Window

This is a review-now window, not a blind sign-everything-now instruction. The commercial question is specific: which future-start rates remain valid for this customer, at what full cost, with what commission disclosure, credit terms and contract end date alignment. Waiting is not neutral, and it only works if prices move in the customer's favour. Once a supplier withdraws a rate, the customer cannot go back and secure yesterday's price.

Section 08

British Gas 18-Month Window

The extended renewal window turns this market intelligence into a practical customer action.

Who This Applies To

British Gas business customers can typically renew up to 18 months before their current contract end date, rather than the standard 12. That matters in a backwardated market: a customer whose contract ends in 2027 or early 2028 may be able to price into the part of the curve where Wednesday's indicators sit 25-56% below the front reference, depending on fuel and season.

Eligibility is not automatic and must be confirmed per customer, per product and per meter. A future-start contract begins when the existing contract expires; the customer does not pay the new tariff immediately, and today's indicative level is not locked until a valid quote is accepted.

What to Check Today

  • Confirm the customer is within the BG 18-month renewal window with a real eligibility check, not an assumption.
  • Obtain the actual quoted unit rates, standing charges, term options and quote validity timestamp.
  • Compare the full forecast bill against current-contract outcomes and against alternative suppliers' valid quotes.
  • Remember the Wednesday curve split: 2027 seasons fell again while the front rose, so renewal timing and delivery period genuinely change the answer.

Ceasefire Reality Check

Diplomatic contact is not a ceasefire, and a ceasefire announcement is not verified safe passage.

The Honest Scorecard, Wednesday 18 November

  • Signed, operative agreement? No. Oman-Iran maritime talks (17 Nov) and Iran's stated ECO proposal to reopen Hormuz are dialogue. There is no signed, enforceable ceasefire in the Gulf theatre this morning.
  • Reduced incident risk? No. The opposite: two tanker incidents on Monday, a second strike on TARA GAS, and a widening Bahrain-Kuwait-Qeshm exchange overnight. UKMTO's log remains active.
  • Safe, insured, repeatable LNG transit? Not established. October showed partial recovery (31 laden exits per Reuters), but Qatar's force majeure extensions into December tell their own story about confidence.
  • Measurable shipped tonnes recovering? Partially for oil via the East-West bypass (~4m bpd rerouted); LNG has no equivalent bypass, which is why LNG remains the bridge between Gulf security and UK gas.
An intercepted missile is not a confirmed energy infrastructure hit, and a hit is not necessarily lost production. An improvement in security can reduce a risk premium before damaged infrastructure is repaired, but physical flows are the supply test.

Never assume price direction from the geopolitical narrative alone. Wednesday's own sheet proves the point: escalation overnight, yet the 2027 seasons still fell.

Shipping Risk

Route by route. The security risk zone is not confined to Hormuz. A route can remain open and still carry a substantial war-risk premium. Friction gets priced.

Hormuz

Two UKMTO-logged tanker incidents on Monday 16 Nov: Warning 207-26 (05:58 UTC, projectile, engine-room fire, crew safe) and Warning 208-26 (~19:00 UTC, 7nm north of Khasab). The second vessel, LPG tanker TARA GAS (Panama flag), was struck again roughly 12 hours later; the crew abandoned ship, 21 of 24 Indian nationals were evacuated to Khasab, two are hospitalised and one seafarer is missing. Responsibility has not been independently established. An LNG security incident matters more to UK gas than an unrelated general-cargo incident; this was LPG, which still tightens the same insurance market.

Gulf of Oman · Persian Gulf

The Gulf of Oman corridor (Khor Fakkan, Fujairah approaches) has seen repeated strikes since October, including product and crude tankers. Inside the Gulf, Qatar's loadings remain constrained by force majeure rather than by a single new incident. Iran's claim of seven vessels hit (four afire, three mined) remains an Iranian claim; vessel-by-vessel verification via UKMTO, operators and tracking has not confirmed that count.

Gulf of Aden · Bab al-Mandeb

Houthi capability keeps this corridor live. The coalition's Wednesday strike package across four Yemeni cities targeted missile and drone infrastructure, and a renewed government offensive near Bab al-Mandeb has suffered setbacks, leaving the Houthis in control of positions near the strait (Xinhua, 17 Nov). Red Sea access for LNG remains impaired.

Red Sea · Yanbu / Red Sea Corridor

As Hormuz becomes less reliable, the Red Sea route becomes more valuable and more strategic. Saudi crude exits west via the East-West pipeline to Yanbu, which is exactly why Tuesday's Houthi claim against pumping stations No. 8 and No. 9 matters despite being unconfirmed. Turkey's Red Sea cooperation discussions and Egypt's defensive authorisation are diplomatic architecture, not escorted convoys. Yanbu and the East-West pipeline matter because they help Saudi crude bypass Hormuz; a verified hit that removed throughput would materially alter the oil market.

LNG Carriers · Tanker War-Risk Insurance

LNG carriers have been struck before in this conflict (including a Qatar-flagged carrier in July and a Marshall Islands LNG carrier on 30 October), but no LNG carrier loss is verified this week. The insurance market prices the pattern, not the cargo label of the latest victim: a twice-struck tanker, one seafarer missing, and intercepts over Bahrain and Kuwait all feed war-risk premiums. Qatar needs predictable safe passage, not occasional successful crossings.

A route can remain physically open and still carry a material war-risk premium.

Section 17

Supplier Price-Book Lag

Wholesale markets move first. Supplier books often follow. The lag cuts both ways.

Why the Lag Matters More Today Than Usual

Tuesday's sheet fell across every supplied season. Wednesday's sheet split: the front rose 1.40% while 2027 seasons fell again and 2028-2030 gas mostly rose. A supplier rate book published off Tuesday's curve may still be live this morning; a supplier repricing off Wednesday's front-end rebound may pull or lift near-dated offers while leaving longer-dated ones untouched. Neither direction is guaranteed, and neither can be assumed without a named supplier, a timestamp and a valid quote.

  • Verify before advising: for any live conversation, confirm supplier name, quote date and time, start date, term, meter profile and annual consumption, unit rate, standing charge, commission, pass-throughs, credit requirements and expiry.
  • Withdrawn means withdrawn: once a supplier pulls a rate, the customer cannot go back and secure yesterday's price. The same is true in reverse: fresh decreases may be live this morning and worth testing.
  • No invented expiry: this report names no supplier price changes because none have been verified overnight. That absence is itself the instruction: check the book before quoting the market.
  • BG window: the British Gas 18-month renewal eligibility remains relevant where applicable, confirmed per product and per customer.
The market does not need to rise again tomorrow for customers to pay more tomorrow. If a book reprices today, the old indication is gone whether or not wholesale keeps climbing.

Waiting Is Not Neutral

Waiting might help if Norwegian and US LNG supply stays strong, weather turns mild, wind recovers, Gulf shipping normalises verifiably, or suppliers cut rates further. Waiting might hurt if shipping losses reduce physical LNG, Norway stumbles again, storage withdrawals accelerate into cold weather, or supplier books reverse off Wednesday's rebound. Waiting only works if the market moves in the customer's favour. Show both outcomes and let the customer's risk appetite decide.
