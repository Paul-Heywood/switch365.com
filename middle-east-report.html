5. Futures Market Explained

The futures market shows today's price for energy delivered in a future period.

A customer does not have to wait until their contract end date to access today's pricing for future delivery.

The % vs W-26 column shows how much cheaper or more expensive each future delivery period is compared with the latest W-26 price.

The 1 Year Ago line shows whether today's forward curve is cheaper, similar or more expensive than the same delivery period was priced last year. Use the 1 Year Ago line as context, not as the customer's available price. Today's latest price is the decision point. The 1 Year Ago line shows how the market has changed.

Cheaper further out does not mean cheaper later.
Backwardation does not mean wait.

The customer mistake is thinking a cheaper future period means they should wait. It does not. It means we should check whether that future period can be secured now. The future market is only useful while the customer can still buy that future delivery period. Waiting gives the market time to remove today's forward-value opportunity.

Two Meanings of Backwardation

For storage: potentially problematic, because prompt gas is expensive relative to later gas, so buying now to store for later locks in a loss and discourages injections. Backwardation is attractive to a forward buyer but can be uncomfortable for a storage operator.

For a business buyer: potentially attractive, because future delivery can currently be secured much cheaper than near-term delivery. That distinction is commercially powerful today.

8. British Gas 18-Month Window

British Gas has extended its business energy renewal window from 12 months to 18 months. This gives eligible customers an earlier chance to review future-start pricing.

The 18-month British Gas window matters because it turns the forward market into a practical customer action. With the curve in steep backwardation, the 18-month window is precisely the tool that lets an eligible customer reach the cheaper 2027 and 2028 delivery periods visible in today's tables.

Your new contract would not start today. It would begin when your current agreement ends. Pricing can potentially be secured earlier.

Waiting is not neutral. If the market moves or British Gas withdraws rates, customers cannot go back and secure today's pricing later. Not every customer is automatically eligible; eligibility and live pricing need to be checked.

Cheaper further out does not mean cheaper later. It means check whether the future period can be secured now.

17. Supplier Price-Book Lag

Supplier price-book lag is the opportunity and the danger.

The current sequence: last week diplomacy improved, wholesale fell, and customer prices had scope to improve. Then the Bank Holiday: a US-Iran military exchange, TTF above €70, Brent above $90, and the UK returns on Tuesday to supplier books that may still carry pre-weekend assumptions.

Wholesale falls, the supplier book may lag, and the customer briefly gets an improved rate. Wholesale rises, the supplier book may lag again, and the customer may briefly retain a price that no longer reflects the market. But once supplier pricing is changed, the old rate is gone.

Wholesale markets move first. Supplier books often follow. Once a supplier withdraws a rate, the customer cannot go back and secure yesterday's price. Suppliers price probability, not certainty.

Are any suppliers still holding pre-Bank-Holiday prices?

The internal offered table (Tuesday 1 September AM) shows supplier books have already begun absorbing the weekend: every period is higher than Friday morning, led by the front. Whether any suppliers are still holding pre-Bank-Holiday prices is exactly what a same-day pricing review answers, and this morning's move suggests that window is closing fast. A price opportunity only exists while the supplier is prepared to offer it.
