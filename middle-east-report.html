Qatar LNG remains heavily constrained, European storage is below normal and Hormuz commercial traffic remains severely disrupted. 01 Summary

Answer first: what actually moved, what it means for a UK business energy price, and what to do about it today.

The central UK energy risk remains LNG. Reduced Qatari exports force Europe and Asia to compete for replacement supply at a time when European storage remains below normal. If that combination persists, TTF and NBP remain exposed. Strong Norwegian pipeline gas and improving diplomacy can soften the risk, while actual UK electricity prices will also depend on wind, nuclear and interconnector availability.

The longer Hormuz remains impaired, the longer the global LNG market has to operate around the disruption. The conflict is approaching six months and the evidence for a rapid resolution remains weak. The market increasingly has to plan around prolonged disruption, and the energy system is adapting around the conflict rather than operating normally.

Today the transmission completed one more step. After Monday's 3-4% wholesale rise, this morning's internal offered power jumped £3.75/MWh at the front and longer-dated gas periods such as W-27 rose +2.25p. Wholesale moved first; supplier books have now begun to follow.

For customers ending in 2026, the time available to wait is shrinking while the risk remains elevated. For later contract ends, the question is whether cheaper future delivery can be secured now. LNG remains the bridge between Hormuz and UK gas. Brent is not the customer's gas quote.

05 Futures Market Explained

The single most important commercial concept in this report.

  • The futures market is today's price for energy delivered later. A customer ending in 2028 does not need to wait until 2028 to see the 2028 market.
  • If suppliers will sell the future period today, it can potentially be secured today. The future market is only useful while the customer can still buy that future delivery period.
  • Cheaper further out does not mean cheaper later. The customer mistake is thinking a cheaper future period means they should wait. It does not. It means we should check whether that future period can be secured now.
  • Backwardation does not mean wait. Today's curve is heavily backwardated: the market prices the Hormuz/LNG problem as worst right now. That is a statement about today's pricing, not a promise about tomorrow's.
  • Waiting gives the market time to remove today's forward-value opportunity. Waiting is not neutral.

08 British Gas 18-Month Window

Verified Switch365 internal evidence. British Gas 18-month eligibility turns this market intelligence into a practical customer action.

  • British Gas has extended its business energy renewal window from 12 months to 18 months. This gives eligible customers an earlier chance to review future-start pricing.
  • The 18-month British Gas window matters because it turns the forward market into a practical customer action. With S-27 gas 36.0% and S-27 power 34.7% below W-26 today, the periods now inside the 18-month window carry genuine forward value, even after this morning's repricing.
  • Your new contract would not start today. It would begin when your current agreement ends.
  • Waiting is not neutral. If the market moves or British Gas withdraws rates, customers cannot go back and secure today's pricing later. This morning's +£3.75 power move is a live example of how quickly offered rates can change.
  • Cheaper further out does not mean cheaper later. It means check whether the future period can be secured now.
Check before relying on it: is the customer eligible, what is the contract end date, who is the current supplier, what is the current consumption, and is future-start pricing actually available for their meter? British Gas is one option among several; eligibility is not automatic and it is not necessarily the cheapest book on any given day.

09 Market Drivers

Ranked by likely impact on UK business prices today. Each driver: what happened, direction, transmission mechanism, and why a UK customer should care.

1 • Bullish - Direct UK gas driver

Qatar LNG disruption

Loadings remain around 60% below year-earlier levels (tracking estimate). Europe and Asia are competing for the same flexible LNG cargoes, and the UK does not need to lose a specific named Qatari cargo for the price impact to matter: the price effect can arrive through global replacement cost. Qatar LNG flows being materially below normal matters far more directly to UK gas than a small change in Brent.

2 • Bullish - Direct UK gas driver

European gas storage

EU ~62% full, Germany ~50%, UK ~28% (AGSI+, 18 Aug gas day), roughly 14 points below the five-year average. Storage is a buffer, not immunity. Storage does not need to run out for prices to rise: the market prices the risk of having insufficient gas later. If storage improves faster than expected, that is a meaningful bearish signal.

3 • Mixed - Direct UK gas driver

Norwegian gas supply

An unplanned Karsto outage removed 30 mcm/day from 20 August, during the planned maintenance season. Norway is one of Europe's strongest physical buffers (roughly 30% of combined EU/UK demand). Strong Norwegian gas is one of the biggest bearish counterweights to the Hormuz LNG story; an outage while Qatar is constrained is the combination to fear.

4 • Bullish - Direct UK gas driver

NBP / TTF movement

TTF rose about 4% Monday to ~€68.6/MWh; UK gas traded around 169 p/therm, +3.5%. TTF movement is being driven by physical gas factors (LNG replacement cost, storage deficit, Norway), not simply oil headlines. These benchmarks show whether geopolitical risk is actually transmitting into European gas prices. Today, it is.

5 • Bullish - Risk amplifier

Hormuz physical shipping

AIS-detected transits remain ~90% below pre-conflict baselines (UKMTO). A tanker was disabled by a projectile in Omani waters this morning. AIS-detected traffic and actual physical energy volume are not identical, and dark traffic can make vessel-tracking data incomplete; however, commercial traffic remaining dramatically below normal is itself important.

6 • Bearish buffer - Direct UK gas driver

Replacement LNG availability

US LNG exports are running at record 2026 levels (February +17% year on year), with JKM ($23.51) and TTF (~$23.44) near parity, so Europe remains competitive for Atlantic cargoes. The commercial question: can replacement LNG continue filling the Qatar gap? Partially yes, which is stabilising; a hard no would be strongly bullish.

7 • Mixed - Direct UK power driver

UK wind / generation

Wind forecast trough near 6.5 GW over the weekend coincided with ~1,870 MW of UK nuclear coming offline and French nuclear constraints. Record July solar (14.4% of electricity) is the offset. Low wind when gas is expensive is bullish for power; high wind would mute the gas pass-through.

8 • Bullish today - Customer transmission point

Supplier price-book movement

Offered W-26 power rose £3.75/MWh overnight to £139.00, and gas W-27 added +2.25p: supplier books are now following Monday's wholesale rise. Wholesale markets move first; supplier books follow. That sequence is exactly why review windows have a shelf life.

9 • Mixed - Risk amplifier

Iran / US sanctions and retaliation

Operation Economic Outcast designated around 60 entities, individuals and vessels and widened secondary-sanctions risk across digital assets, technology, gold, aviation and shipping, but gave countries time to comply and spared major Chinese banks. Iran vows retaliation; threat, not yet action. Sanctions matter to UK energy when they reduce physical supply, disrupt shipping finance, affect insurance, change Chinese purchasing or increase replacement demand. Sanctions headlines without a physical market consequence should not outrank LNG flows.

10 • Bearish buffer - Geopolitical trigger

Diplomacy

Pakistan reports "significant progress" after Field Marshal Munir's Tehran visit, aimed at restoring the June Islamabad MoU and reopening Hormuz; Oman's foreign minister spoke with Araghchi on Friday. Positive diplomatic language is relevant. But the energy-market test is physical: did LNG tankers return, did commercial shipping rise, did insurance costs fall, did TTF/NBP respond?

11 • Indicator only - Secondary

Brent

~$91.5/bbl, easing after sanctions. Well below the $126 March peak. Brent is a useful geopolitical thermometer. Brent is not the customer's gas quote. Oil can fall while UK gas rises, as it did on Monday.

10 Top Five Warning Signs

Not the five most dramatic headlines: the five developments most capable of materially affecting UK energy.

WARNING 1

Qatar LNG fails to recover

Flows ~60% below normal with two trains damaged for years. If September and October cargo nominations do not improve, Europe enters winter refill's final stretch still competing with Asia for every flexible cargo. That is the single most direct threat to UK gas pricing.

WARNING 2

European storage falls further behind

At ~62% with Germany near 50%, the injection pace has almost no slack. High summer prices discourage injection, which can create a self-reinforcing loop. Watch the weekly AGSI delta against the ~80% relaxed target trajectory.

WARNING 3

Norway suffers a major outage

Karsto is already down 30 mcm/day in maintenance season. A serious unplanned outage at Troll, Kollsnes or on Langeled while Qatar is constrained would remove the UK's biggest physical buffer and hit NBP directly.

WARNING 4

Hormuz LNG traffic remains near zero

Sunday saw only 4 tracked commodity-vessel transits, and this morning's projectile strike off Oman reinforces why owners stay away. Eight gas carriers crossed in three days, but six were empty and two carried LPG, not LNG. Do not confuse LPG with LNG: LPG tanker activity does not demonstrate normalisation of Qatar LNG exports.

WARNING 5

Duration risk is now reaching supplier books

Monday's 3-4% wholesale rise has been followed within 24 hours by a £3.75 jump in offered W-26 power and gains across longer-dated gas periods (W-27 +2.25, S-29 +1.00, S-30 +0.98). If longer-dated periods keep lifting, the forward-value window customers can still buy today erodes from both ends.

Qatar LNG

  • Loadings around 60% below year-earlier levels (tracking estimate, 25 Aug), partially recovered to the best rate since March. Affected cargo is not the same as lost cargo, and delayed is not cancelled, but the persistent shortfall is what keeps replacement-cost pressure in the global market.
  • Two damaged trains are out for years, so even a full maritime normalisation does not restore nameplate Qatari supply quickly.
  • UAE volumes are largely rerouted via ship-to-ship transfer outside the strait, a workaround that reduces supply loss but increases time, freight and complexity. Friction gets priced.
  • Price evidence of the squeeze: JKM $23.51/MMBtu and TTF-equivalent ~$23.44, up more than 100% year on year, with the Pacific diversion window shut. Europe and Asia are competing for the same flexible LNG cargoes.

Bab el-Mandeb

  • Commodity transits: 22 Friday, 32 Saturday, 24 Sunday (Kpler). Two VLCCs entered the Red Sea Saturday, one carrying Iraqi Basrah crude.
  • Houthi claims of attacks on Saudi tankers remain claims; Saudi Arabia is routing more oil via Mediterranean shuttles to reduce Red Sea exposure.
  • Relevance to the UK is indirect: diversion length and freight, not lost cargoes, is the mechanism.

Workarounds

  • Yanbu / East-West Pipeline, SUMED / Sidi Kerir, Cape of Good Hope, Fujairah STS and Omani routing all reduce physical supply loss but add time, freight and complexity.
  • Reuters examples indicate some Saudi-to-Asia voyages normally around 20 days can take roughly 50-60 days under major diversion, with route calculations around $2.5 million of additional transport cost in some cases. Indicative examples, not universal costs.
  • Workarounds reduce supply loss but increase time, freight and complexity. Friction gets priced, and it is part of why global replacement cost stays elevated even when oil itself keeps moving.

17 Supplier Price-Book Lag

The customer transmission point, and today's most commercially important section after the LNG story itself.

SUPPLIER PRICE-BOOK LAG IS THE OPPORTUNITY AND THE DANGER.

  • Wholesale markets move first. Supplier books often follow. Yesterday public NBP rose more than 3%; this morning offered W-26 power is up £3.75/MWh and longer-dated gas periods have lifted. The lag resolved in about 24 hours.
  • Suppliers price probability, not certainty. They can price LNG replacement costs before Britain experiences a physical shortage. They can price storage risk before storage becomes critical. They can price Norwegian outage risk immediately. They can price duration before the conflict's end date is known.
  • Suppliers price wholesale energy, volatility, credit, collateral, freight, risk, liquidity, expected future market movement and the delivery period. A customer does not need to wait for a physical UK gas shortage before supplier pricing changes.
  • Once a supplier withdraws a rate, the customer cannot go back and secure yesterday's price. Once a rate is withdrawn, it cannot be bought retrospectively. Yesterday's W-26 power at £135.25 is a live example: it no longer exists as an offered rate.
  • Waiting is not neutral. A customer waiting until newspapers say "UK gas crisis" may already have missed the earlier supplier rate.

18 Customer Action

Five groups, five actions. This is a review-now window, not a wait-and-see market. The instruction everywhere is "review live pricing now", never "blindly contract immediately".

PRIORITY 1

Contract ends in 2026 - Immediate Review

  • The time available to wait is shrinking while the duration risk is rising. For customers ending this year, waiting is becoming an increasingly concentrated gamble. Offered power at the front has just moved £3.75/MWh in a single night.
  • If the market falls, waiting may help. If the market rises, they have progressively less time to recover. Waiting only works if the market moves in the customer's favour.
  • This group should be prioritised for an immediate review. Review current live options and decide whether the remaining downside potential justifies the increasingly limited time and geopolitical risk.
PRIORITY 2

Inside 12 months - Do Not Wait for Renewal Panic

  • Check the current market against the actual contract end date rather than waiting for the renewal letter.
  • Compare supplier books: Monday's wholesale rise has now landed in power pricing; gas books are only part-way through repricing, so live quotes this week may still carry older assumptions in places.
PRIORITY 3

British Gas inside 18 months - Extended Renewal Window

  • Check future-start eligibility now that the renewal window runs 18 months rather than 12.
  • Your new contract would not start today; it begins when your current agreement ends. Eligibility is not automatic, so confirm CED, current supplier, consumption and future-start availability.
PRIORITY 4

High usage / 250,000 kWh+ - Look Further Forward

  • Larger portfolios justify checking longer forward availability now: S-28, W-28, S-29 and beyond carry today's deepest value, though parts lifted overnight.
  • Give strategic review priority: scale means the difference between W-26 and S-29 pricing is measured in material budget terms, not basis points.
PRIORITY 5

2027 / 2028 / 2029 / 2030 CED - Your Delivery Period Matters

  • Compare the actual delivery period against W-26 and against 1 Year Ago: S-29 gas is currently 61.9% below the front and still below its year-ago price; S-29 power is 53.3% below the front.
  • Check whether that backwardated value can be secured now. W-27 gas rose +2.25p overnight; the future market is only useful while the customer can still buy that future delivery period.

The customer decision framework

  • Step 1: What is today's actual supplier price for their delivery period?
  • Step 2: How does that compare with W-26?
  • Step 3: How does it compare with one year ago?
  • Step 4: What is their contract end date?
  • Step 5: How much time do they have if the market rises?
  • Step 6: Does today's price provide acceptable certainty?

Then review, not panic. We don't need to predict the market perfectly; we simply need to establish what the customer can secure today. You should know what today's option is before deciding to keep waiting.

Suggested customer explanation: "The biggest risk to UK energy prices isn't really the oil price itself. The more direct issue is LNG. Qatar's LNG flows remain heavily disrupted because of Hormuz, which means Europe and Asia have to compete for replacement gas from places such as the US. Europe is doing that with storage still below normal, so if the disruption continues, the pressure can feed through TTF into NBP and ultimately supplier pricing. Norway remains an important buffer and diplomacy could still improve the situation, so this isn't a prediction that prices must rise. The sensible approach is simply to check what your actual future contract can be secured at today before deciding whether waiting remains worth the risk."
