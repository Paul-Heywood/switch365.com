1Summary

What has changed that could change the price a UK business can actually secure today.

Iran is starting to act on the threat. A day after the IRGC threatened to "tighten the lock" on the Strait of Hormuz, Iran's Persian Gulf Strait Authority (PGSA) added 11 more vessels to its non-compliant blacklist, taking the total to 56. The list covers very large crude carriers, LNG carriers, LPG tankers and clean product vessels. Listed vessels face fines, detention or confiscation if they transit without complying with Iranian requirements. This does not mean Hormuz is closed. It means more ships now face a credible threat of penalty.

Commercial behaviour is already changing. Reuters has reported that at least three Indian refiners and a major international energy company planned to stop using blacklisted vessels, including for ship-to-ship transfers. Iran is now threatening to blacklist counterparties that cooperate with listed ships through STS transfers or transshipment. The energy significance begins when a threat changes what companies are actually prepared to do.

UK gas remains in the 180p-plus war-high zone. Internal Switch365 offered pricing shows W-26 gas at 184.75p/therm this morning, up another 8.25p day on day. Wholesale has already moved; the question is when supplier books fully catch up. Supplier price-book lag is the opportunity and the danger.

What it means

The blacklist is beginning to have real commercial consequences while Qatar LNG stays severely constrained and European storage sits well below normal. LNG remains the bridge between Hormuz and UK gas. The market does not have to rise again today for customers to pay more tomorrow: if supplier pricing currently lags the wholesale move, catch-up repricing alone can lift the price a business is quoted. This is a review-now window, not a wait-and-see market.

5Futures Market Explained

The futures market shows today's price for energy delivered in a future period. A customer does not have to wait until their contract end date to see today's market for future delivery.

Right now the curve is in steep backwardation: near delivery is expensive because the war, the Hormuz restrictions and the Qatar LNG loss are current problems, while later delivery periods are priced as if some normalisation eventually arrives. W-26 gas is offered at 184.75p/therm this morning; S-30 is offered at 67.39p.

Backwardation does not mean wait. The customer mistake is thinking a cheaper future period means they should wait. It does not. It means we should check whether that future period can be secured now. The future market is only useful while the customer can still buy that future delivery period.

Waiting gives the market time to remove today's forward-value opportunity. Do not let 185p gas make anyone assume all future gas is 185p: the tables above show exactly where current forward value remains, and the question is whether it can be locked before supplier books reprice the whole curve.

Is there still a gap between wholesale and supplier pricing?

Yes, by construction. Wholesale has risen roughly 8-10% on the front in two sessions; internal offered prices are moving up daily but by smaller increments, and supplier books reprice in steps, not continuously. That lag is temporary by definition. Wholesale moved first. Supplier books may still follow.

Waiting is not neutral. Customer waits, wholesale stays flat, supplier reprices existing book, customer pays more. Wholesale does not even need another rise for the customer's price to change. That is today's strongest commercial explanation.

9Market Drivers

Ranked by relevance to the price a UK business can secure today. Each driver: what happened, confidence, direction, and why UK businesses should care.

1. Hormuz blacklist / shipping behaviour

BullishConfidence: High (PGSA website via Reuters, 3 Sep)

Iran added 11 vessels to its non-compliant list in the past 24 hours, taking the total to 56. LNG carriers are included, and the PGSA warns that vessels cooperating with listed ships via STS transfers or transshipment will themselves be added. Iran has blacklisted 56 vessels it says are non-compliant and subject to penalties if they attempt to transit under its rules; it has not banned every ship, and not all 56 are LNG tankers. Why it matters: Iran does not have to physically sink or seize a tanker to disrupt Hormuz. If owners, insurers, refiners or energy companies decide a vessel is no longer worth the risk, capacity is effectively removed from the market, influencing freight, insurance, tanker availability, scheduling, cargo replacement, delivery times and LNG prices.

2. NBP / UK gas

BullishConfidence: High (ICE, internal evidence)

Internal W-26 offered at 184.75p/therm, up 8.25p day on day. Quarterly contract closed 2 Sep at 185.06p after a 186.90p intraday 52-week high; public CFD indication eased about 1.4% early on 3 Sep but remains in the 180p zone, the highest since January 2023. NBP is closer to the customer than Brent. Why it matters: this is the price formation behind the supplier books businesses are quoted from.

3. Supplier price-book risk

BullishConfidence: Medium-High (Switch365 internal experience)

No confirmed book withdrawal in this research run, but the risk of supplier repricing is materially elevated because wholesale has already moved. When wholesale gas previously reached about 180p/therm, Switch365 saw some suppliers withdraw price books, temporarily stop offering existing rates and reissue pricing at higher levels. Not every supplier acted identically then and they will not now. Why it matters: a 10% wholesale move does not automatically produce a 10% customer-price increase, but a withdrawn book removes yesterday's price entirely.

4. Qatar LNG

BullishConfidence: High (Edison statement, ICIS data)

Force majeure on Edison deliveries extended into early November; 29 cargoes now affected, about 3.8 bcm. Edison has replaced 21 cargoes (about 2 bcm) and says it can meet its obligations: bullish on duration, bearish on Europe's ability to source alternatives. Qatar exported 18 cargoes in the first six months of the war versus 509 in the comparable period last year. Pakistan cancellations run into October, Bangladesh beyond September. Why it matters: Qatar historically supplied about one-fifth of global LNG; LNG remains the bridge between Hormuz and UK gas.

5. European storage

BullishConfidence: High (AGSI/GIE)

EU aggregate about 64.4% at the turn of the month against a five-year norm near 82%. Germany 52.5-53.0%, Netherlands 46.4%, Italy 82.6%, France 70.0%. Injection pace around +0.25 points a day tracks only just above the relaxed 80% November target; the old 90% target is out of reach. Storage is a buffer, not immunity. Why it matters: weak storage makes Europe more sensitive to Norway outages, US LNG outages, Asian competition, cold weather and further shipping disruption.

6. US-Iran military risk

MixedConfidence: High on events (AP/CENTCOM); Low on claims

Overnight the US carried out its heaviest strikes since July against Iranian air-defence, radar, maritime, mine-laying and communications targets near the Strait; Iran responded with missiles and drones against US bases in Kuwait (Ali Al Salem), Jordan, Bahrain and Iraq's Kurdistan region. Kuwait's army said this morning it was intercepting incoming attacks; the IRGC claims US personnel were killed at Ali Al Salem, which remains an unconfirmed claim. Iran says 18 people were killed in the US strikes; the US denies targeting civilians. Trump says the campaign "won't last too long" but the US can strike "any time we want". Unless energy infrastructure, ports, LNG facilities, refineries or tanker traffic are directly hit, classify this as a risk amplifier rather than the main UK gas driver.

7. TTF

BullishConfidence: High

TTF closed 2 Sep at €73.365/MWh, marked €74.08 early on 3 Sep, and broke above €75 intraday on 2 Sep for the first time since the war began, the highest since early 2023. European gas is confirming UK NBP rather than contradicting it; the UK move is broadly in step with the European LNG and storage story. Why it matters: Britain prices at global replacement cost. Qatar supply falls, Europe and Asia seek alternatives, competition for US and Atlantic LNG rises, TTF responds, NBP responds, supplier books respond.

8. Norway

Bearish buffer, currently impairedConfidence: High (Gassco / TotalEnergies review)

September maintenance is biting: Kollsnes works removed about 40 mcm/day over the bank holiday weekend and Langeled deliveries to the UK fell 26 mcm/day, contributing to Monday's 11.60p day-ahead gap-up. Planned outages were scheduled to rise towards 77 mcm/day around 30 August before works complete around mid-September. Strong Norwegian supply materially cushions LNG disruption; this week that cushion is thinner. Why it matters: Norway is the primary pipeline buffer while LNG is constrained.

9. US LNG

Bearish bufferConfidence: High (EIA/DOE)

US exports ran at about 16.2 Bcf/d in May, up 15.5% year on year, with Europe the favoured destination; Corpus Christi Stage 3's final train is ramping. Asian spot LNG at a five-month high (around $23.20-24.60/MMBtu this week) shows Europe must still compete for cargoes. US LNG remains one of the strongest bearish buffers against Qatar disruption. Why it matters: replacement Atlantic cargoes are what stand between the Qatar loss and materially higher European prices.

10. Sanctions / duration

MixedConfidence: High (Reuters)

Bessent says a bank sanction is likely this week and another next week, with airline leasing and IRGC-linked business in scope: "We are going to economically asphyxiate this regime." Rubio warned that countries helping Iran evade sanctions will face sanctions themselves. The US sanctioned the PGSA in May, so companies engaging with Iran's transit authority face US sanctions exposure: regulatory friction on both sides. A shorter bombing campaign does not necessarily mean a shorter energy crisis; the sanctions campaign is designed to continue regardless.

12. Brent

15Shipping Risk

Hormuz blacklist

56 vessels listed as non-compliant by the PGSA, up 11 in the past 24 hours. The list spans very large crude carriers, LNG carriers, LPG tankers, clean product tankers and other commercial vessels. Owners named in earlier reporting include ADNOC Logistics and Services (the most represented, including LNG carriers), Navig8, Bahri, Nakilat (Al Rekayyat, Rasheeda), GasLog, Stolt-Nielsen, Dynacom and Shipping Corporation of India joint-venture tonnage (LNG tanker Disha). At least 14 listed vessels had already been attacked in and around the Strait in recent months. LPG traffic is not LNG normalisation; the two are never combined in this report.

STS threat: the new dimension

The restriction can spread beyond the original 56 ships. Iran says vessels cooperating with listed ships via STS oil transfers or transshipment will be added. A company may therefore avoid not just the listed vessel but counterparties, transfer operations and replacement arrangements. Kpler analysts warn the key issue is contagion: the pool of willing owners, charterers and buyers narrows, due-diligence requirements rise, and freight, insurance and risk premia increase. The commercial impact can extend beyond the 56 named vessels.

LNG ship-to-ship transfers

Reuters (Vortexa/Kpler data, 2 Sep) confirms three rare LNG STS operations outside the Strait in August: GasLog Shanghai to GasLog Savannah off Oman; Al Rekayyat to Tembek off the UAE east coast, with the Ras Laffan cargo delivered to Dahej, India on 31 August; and Mraweh to LNG Enugu off Oman, now heading for Futtsu, Japan. None of the three receiving operations has been confirmed to involve currently blacklisted vessels, and no Iranian reaction specific to these transfers has been reported in this run; that exposure question remains open. Workarounds are evidence of resilience and disruption at the same time: they show cargo can still move, but they also show normal logistics have broken down.

Physical traffic

Visible transits remain severely depressed and volatile: about 14 vessels recorded on 2 Sep (6 entering, 8 exiting) on AIS tracking, and only five identifiable commercial vessels on Monday, none of them liquid cargo tankers, against a 10-day average around 14. Kpler revised its crude-flow estimate up to about 8.6 million barrels a day once dark-fleet movement is included, still under half of pre-war levels. AIS-visible data excludes dark ships, military-controlled movements and vessels with tracking disabled; always interpret count plus vessel type plus cargo plus volume.

Oil flow: the counterpoint

Reuters reported about 17 million barrels of oil passed through Hormuz on Monday, and US officials told CNN the Navy escorted about 40 commercial vessels carrying a record ~18 million barrels on Tuesday, the largest such operation since the war began. Blacklist expansion does not equal total physical closure. The relevant questions are whether that flow can be repeated consistently, and whether LNG can do the same. Crude has more workaround flexibility than LNG, which needs specialist ships, loading, receiving terminals and cryogenic handling. A strong oil-transit day does not prove LNG normalisation.

Bab el-Mandeb

No fresh Bab el-Mandeb incident affecting energy was identified in this research window; it remains relevant only as a Saudi western export and Suez workaround route. Egypt and China jointly stressed that Red Sea security rests with littoral states.

Workarounds

Gulf producers continue shuttle runs through the Strait with STS transfer off Fujairah and Sohar; STS product activity held near recent levels in August (seven vessels versus eight in July) but listed vessels are becoming harder to book. Saudi East-West pipeline, Yanbu, SUMED, Cape routing and Omani-side transits all remain in use. Workarounds reduce outright shortage risk but increase time, complexity and cost. Friction gets priced. The more complicated it becomes to move a cargo legally, financially and safely, the more friction enters the energy market.

The blacklist matters most when it changes which ships companies are prepared to use. That change is now observable.

17Supplier Price-Book Lag

Wholesale has already moved. Have suppliers?

UK gas has moved into the 180p-plus zone: internal W-26 offered at 184.75p/therm this morning, the quarterly contract at a 52-week intraday high of 186.90p yesterday, public benchmarks at their highest since January 2023. Meanwhile Iran is tightening shipping restrictions with LNG carriers on the list. The risk of supplier repricing is materially elevated because wholesale has already moved.

Switch365 internal commercial evidence: when wholesale gas previously reached about 180p/therm, some suppliers withdrew price books, temporarily stopped offering existing rates and reissued pricing at higher levels. Not every supplier acted identically, and no withdrawal is claimed this morning. But any valid pre-surge quote may deserve urgent review.

Supplier price-book lag is the opportunity and the danger.
Wholesale markets move first. Supplier books often follow.
Once a supplier withdraws a rate, the customer cannot go back and secure yesterday's price.
The market does not have to rise again tomorrow for customers to pay more tomorrow.

The mechanics are simple: supplier catches up, existing price book withdrawn, new price book issued, customer receives higher price. And a note of accuracy: wholesale energy is only part of a business energy bill. A 10% wholesale move does not automatically produce a 10% customer-price increase; the point is not the percentage, it is that a lagging book can be pulled at any time.

18Customer Action

The action everywhere is "review live pricing now", never "blindly contract immediately".

1
Existing valid quote: top priority

Check it before the book moves

Check validity, the supplier book behind it, repricing status and the acceptance window. If the wholesale market has moved but the quote has not, the gap may be temporary. The wholesale move has already occurred and supplier pricing may still be catching up.

2
Contract ends in 2026: immediate review

Review live pricing now

Waiting only works if the market moves in the customer's favour. For customers ending this year, the amount of time available to take that gamble is getting smaller. The priority is to compare the live supplier price with the customer's current position today and secure where commercially appropriate.

3
Inside 12 months: do not wait for renewal panic

Check future-start pricing against the real end date

Future-start pricing lets the market for your actual delivery period be tested now, not at renewal. With S-27 gas 34.8% below W-26, the forward market is offering value that did not exist at the front.

4
British Gas inside 18 months: extended renewal window

Check eligibility and live pricing

The British Gas renewal window is now 18 months. The contract starts when the existing one ends, not today. Not every customer is automatically eligible; check first, then price.

5
2027 / 2028 / 2029 / 2030 CED: your delivery period matters

Use backwardation, do not wait for it

High usage outside 12 months? Look further forward: test forward-start and supplier options, and compare the actual delivery period against W-26 and 1 Year Ago. 2028-2030 gas is offered 52-64% below the front. Cheaper further out does not mean cheaper later.

Iran's shipping restrictions are now beginning to change commercial behaviour, which adds another risk to an already elevated gas market. The customer does not need another 10% wholesale rise to lose today's price. The immediate question is what is still available before supplier books catch up.
