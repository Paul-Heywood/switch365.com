Summary

Houthi forces attacked the southern Saudi cities of Abha, Khamis Mushait and Jazan with ballistic missiles and drones on Wednesday, a second consecutive day of strikes, and Saudi Arabia has responded militarily in Yemen. Pakistan has now delivered a Saudi warning to Iran to rein in the Houthis, and Defence Minister Khawaja Asif has said further spillover into Saudi territory could activate the Mecca joint defence arrangement, under which aggression against one could be treated as aggression against all three. Turkey has formally condemned the attacks in the strongest terms and reaffirmed support for Saudi sovereignty, but Pakistan and Turkey have not joined the war: no decision has been made for Pakistani forces to join retaliatory strikes, and any role would initially be defensive and inside Saudi territory.

The duration picture is now two-sided. More active fronts and more states with a security stake make the conflict harder to switch off quickly, so short-term duration risk has increased. At the same time, Pakistan's intervention is primarily intended to prevent further escalation, and Iran now faces a US blockade, tanker losses, sanctions, a Saudi military response, a Pakistani warning and Turkish political support for Riyadh. Greater regional pressure could eventually shorten the conflict, but there is no evidence yet that it has. Fresh Reuters analysis describes the confrontation as increasingly resembling a battle of attrition, with some analysts seeing a material risk it extends beyond the November midterm elections; that is an analyst assessment, not a certainty.

Physically, Hormuz remains severely impaired: Kpler tracked seven commodity vessel transits on Wednesday, half the recent average, and no LNG tanker exited the strait, which matters far more to UK gas than the one crude VLCC that did leave. UK NBP quarterly futures broke above 200p/therm on 9 September, reaching 203.18p before closing at 198.93p, and internal Switch365 offered rates moved up again this morning: W-26 gas is now offered at 195.75p/therm and W-26 power at £157.00/MWh (internal broker / supplier evidence). Wholesale has already moved. The question now is when supplier books catch up.

What It Means for UK Energy

The conflict has not yet become a Saudi-Pakistani-Turkish war, but the security architecture around it is starting to activate. For UK businesses the transmission chain is unchanged and intact: Hormuz, Qatar LNG, global LNG competition, TTF, NBP, gas-fired UK power, supplier books, customer contract. LNG remains the bridge between Hormuz and UK gas. With no LNG exiting Hormuz on Wednesday, European storage still well below seasonal norms and UK gas having traded above 200p, this is a review-now window, not a wait-and-see market. Waiting is not neutral.

British Gas 18-Month Window

British Gas has extended its business energy renewal window from 12 months to 18 months. This gives eligible customers an earlier chance to review future-start pricing.

The 18-month British Gas window matters because it turns the forward market into a practical customer action. With the front of the curve carrying crisis-era pricing and 2028-2030 delivery offered 50-67% below W-26, a customer whose contract ends inside that window can check today's price for their actual future delivery period rather than waiting for the renewal panic.

Your new contract would not start today. It would begin when your current agreement ends. Waiting is not neutral. Hormuz

Kpler tracked seven commodity vessel transits on Wednesday, against a ten-day average of 14 and 12 on Tuesday. Of the seven, four were exiting and three entering. One exiting vessel was the VLCC Finland Prosperity carrying nearly 2 million barrels of crude. Oil movement through Hormuz is not evidence that LNG has normalised: no LNG tanker exited. Visible vessel count and physical energy volume are different measures; AIS can miss dark vessels and ships running without transponders under wartime operational security. Hormuz is not open as usual, but a route can remain physically open and still carry a material war-risk premium.

LNG

No LNG tanker exiting matters more to UK gas than a crude VLCC successfully exiting. Qatar historically supplied roughly one-fifth of global LNG; comparable-period tracking showed 18 LNG cargoes versus 509 a year earlier, an approximate 96% cumulative reduction (not a daily figure). QatarEnergy's force majeure on the Edison arrangement extends into early November, duration evidence rather than a full cancellation. Rare ship-to-ship transfers outside Hormuz show resilience and disruption at the same time. LPG movement is not LNG normalisation.

Iran / US Shipping War

The US destruction of five Iranian crude-oil tankers remains continuing economic pressure rather than today's freshest story: fresh checks on vessel status, lost capacity, insurance and replacement tonnage continue. Iran's claimed attacks on 10 vessels near Hormuz following the strikes became the largest declared wave of attacks on commercial shipping by both sides since the war began (Reuters analysis); not all Iranian claims are independently established. The IRGC has warned tankers in Kuwaiti and Bahraini waters to evacuate.

Bab al-Mandab / Saudi Red Sea

Wednesday's 28 tracked commodity vessels against a ten-day average of 27 mean Bab al-Mandab physical traffic remains close to its recent normal despite Houthi attacks, the Yemen fighting and Saudi retaliation. Hormuz remains severely impaired while Bab al-Mandab carries elevated military risk but continues handling substantial commercial traffic. It does not need to close for Houthi escalation to matter: Saudi infrastructure, Yanbu, Red Sea confidence, freight, insurance and tanker willingness all feed the price. Friction gets priced.

The immediate question is whether any suppliers are still holding pricing produced before the move through 190p and 200p.

Supplier price-book lag is the opportunity and the danger. Wholesale markets move first. Supplier books often follow. Once a supplier withdraws a rate, the customer cannot go back and secure yesterday's price. The market does not need to rise again tomorrow for customers to pay more tomorrow.

Check the actual price. If an existing quote predates 190p, 200p or the latest military escalation, check whether it remains live and compare it immediately with today's live replacement book. Do not assume old automatically means cheap, and never assume a wholesale move translates pound-for-pound into the customer's total energy bill: wholesale is only part of the bill.
