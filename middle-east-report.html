PEACE HOPES HAVE LOWERED GAS PRICES, BUT THE REBOUND HAS STARTED. IS THE BUYING WINDOW CLOSING?

The market is responding to hopes of peace, but wholesale gas and supplier forward prices have begun rising again while safe, routine LNG shipping remains unresolved.

UK gas prices fell sharply as traders anticipated progress towards an Iran settlement, creating a potential buying opportunity. But the market is already reacting in the other direction. NBP December wholesale gas rose 2.25% at Wednesday's close, while Switch365's Thursday W-26 working gas reference increased from 214.10p to 219.75p/therm. Most supplied forward-price indicators also climbed, including Summer 2027 gas up 6p/therm. Prices are no longer falling in a straight line.

The G7's formal declaration recognises US escort and mine-clearing operations and a possible UK–France defensive initiative. That supports safer passage, but does not confirm that normal, insured LNG shipping has returned. More loaded LNG carriers left Hormuz in October, yet attacks on commercial vessels also increased. European gas storage is down to around 71.6%, with Germany near 58.1% on the latest cited gas day. Physical supply has improved in places, but winter vulnerability remains.

For UK businesses, this may still be a buying window, not a last-chance warning. Diplomacy and additional LNG could reduce prices further, while disrupted shipping, colder weather or supplier repricing could send them higher. The practical response is to compare valid future-start quotes, full contract costs and renewal dates against the customer's risk tolerance. Nobody can predict the bottom. Use attractive forward prices where they genuinely protect the business, without confusing hopeful diplomacy with secured energy supply.

The opportunity is to review the forward price available now, before the customer's next decision is made for them by a moving market.

Futures Market Explained

Why a "Winter 2026" or "Summer 2028" price exists at all, and what it can and cannot tell a customer.

Gas and power for future delivery periods trade today as forward contracts. A season such as Summer 2027 is bought and sold now, and its price moves every day with news, weather, storage and shipping. Suppliers buy this way to build the fixed-price contracts they offer businesses, which is why wholesale markets move first and supplier books often follow.

Four different numbers must never be confused. An exchange settlement (such as the NBP Dec-26 close of 216.04p on Wednesday) is a traded wholesale instrument. A Switch365 working reference (W-26 gas 219.75p today) is an internal near-term comparator. An offered seasonal indicator (the tables in Sections 3 and 4) shows where supplier forward pricing broadly sits. A customer quote is the only executable number, built from the customer's meter, usage, start date, term, standing charge and pass-through terms.

The curve is currently in backwardation: later delivery years are priced below near-term ones. Backwardation does not mean wait. A lower S-28 price today is a quote about today's forward market, not a forecast of what 2028 will cost when it arrives, and not a whole-bill saving. Cheaper further out does not mean cheaper later.

  • Seasonal supplier movement: the latest user-approved Wed→Thu table shows 7 of 8 gas prices up and 5 of 6 power prices up. Today's supplier picture is a broad rebound, not a broad decline.

  • Storage and demand: the EU sits at 71.61% and Germany at 58.05% on the dated 10 Nov gas day, in withdrawal; winter weather, weak wind and Asian LNG competition could amplify prices.

Bearish buffers: what argues against panic

  • A durable US–Iran agreement, real mine clearance and consistent insured vessel movements could remove more risk premium.
  • Growing US and non-Gulf LNG, expectations of a milder Asian winter, restored Norwegian flows from 16 Nov and softer demand could reduce prices further.
  • Brent's early Thursday decline to $91.29/bbl shows broader commodity sentiment is not uniformly bullish; oil is simply not a direct read-across to NBP.
  • Wednesday's rebound followed a much larger decline over preceding weeks: context, not a guarantee that the rebound persists.

Customer action inside the window

  1. Check today's executable supplier quotes for the exact customer start or renewal date and meter/usage profile; compare the whole bill rather than the wholesale headline.
  2. Identify customers inside applicable renewal or future-start windows, including British Gas and BG Lite where an up-to-18-month window applies after supplier and product checks.
  3. Keep the near-term W-26 working comparator separate from genuine offered forward seasons; prefer real supplier rates over assumed modelled savings.
  4. Where the customer wants budget certainty, assess staged or future-start fixing against the risk that peace could create further price falls.
  5. Document the trade-offs: securing a valid quote may remove future price-up exposure but may forego cheaper future markets. There is no one-size-fits-all recommendation.
This is a review-now window, not a blind sign-everything-now instruction. Waiting is not neutral, and it only works if prices move in the customer's favour.

British Gas 18-Month Window

The extended renewal window turns today's market intelligence into a practical customer action.

British Gas and BG Lite business customers can, subject to eligibility and product checks, agree their next contract up to 18 months before their current one ends, extended from the previous 12-month window. The new agreement still starts when the existing contract ends; the window simply lets the customer lock a future-start price much earlier.

In a market where later delivery years are offered well below near-term prices, that matters commercially. A BG customer whose contract ends inside 18 months can be quoted today against the same forward seasons shown in Section 6, rather than waiting for the renewal letter and taking whatever the book shows that week. British Gas 18-month eligibility turns this market intelligence into a practical customer action.

  • Confirm the customer is genuinely eligible: supplier, product, meter type, credit and consumption all apply. Not every customer is automatically eligible.
  • Check the live quote validity window: supplier books can be withdrawn or repriced at any time, and once a supplier withdraws a rate, the customer cannot go back and secure yesterday's price.
  • Compare the full contract cost, unit rates plus standing charges and any pass-through elements, against the customer's renewal date and risk tolerance.
The market does not need to rise again tomorrow for customers to pay more tomorrow.

Top Five Warning Signs

Ranked, dated and testable. Two-sided thresholds: fewer attacks, verified demining, improving inventories, LNG arrivals and lower fresh supplier prices would invalidate a bullish warning.
  1. Escalating verified energy loss. New Qatari, UAE or Saudi export infrastructure damage with operator-confirmed volume reductions, not just missiles, threats or explosions. Test: a named operator confirms lost output or extended force majeure beyond current notices. Commercial effect: immediate NBP/TTF repricing and rapid supplier-book follow-through.
  2. Hormuz security deteriorates again. Fresh UKMTO-confirmed commercial vessel attacks, insurance restrictions or falling laden LNG exits, particularly after the G7 defensive statement. The fresh 11 Nov tanker incident, with crew and damage initially unknown, is exactly the kind of event to resolve before calling the route safer. Commercial effect: war-risk premium stays priced into winter gas.
  3. European storage and weather. EU 71.61% and Germany 58.05% on the 10 Nov gas day and falling; watch accelerating withdrawals, cold forecasts, weak wind and simultaneous Asian LNG demand. Commercial effect: every cold snap lands on a thinner buffer and lifts winter contracts.
  4. Norwegian flow shock. Visund's provisional 16 Nov restart slipping, new Gassco unplanned outages or measurable export-flow deterioration. Commercial effect: less pipeline cushioning exactly when LNG is constrained.
  5. Supplier-book reversal. New withdrawals, higher offered prices or disappearing longer-term terms, confirmed by named supplier and timestamp rather than inferred from Brent or the W-26 proxy alone. Today's supplied sheet already moved up across most seasons; the warning is further acceleration. Commercial effect: valid quotes expire into a rising book.

The primary test for Thursday: has diplomacy become physical?

The tests that matter, in order: an operative signed agreement; demonstrably reduced UKMTO incident risk; safe, insured, regular tanker and LNG transits; and measurable shipped tonnes. October's S&P/Kpler figures demonstrate partial recovery (31 laden exits; 42 loadings on a different measure), not full normalisation.

Supplier Price-Book Lag

WHOLESALE HAS MOVED. HAVE SUPPLIER BOOKS CAUGHT UP?

Wholesale gas rose in the most recent confirmed named-contract close, and today's user-approved supplier seasonal indicators show 7 of 8 gas periods and 5 of 6 power periods increasing. The W-26 user gas working value also rose 2.64%. That is strong evidence of a reversal within our supplied comparisons, not a guarantee that every supplier has changed every customer's executable rate.

The commercial questions for today are concrete. Which real suppliers have repriced or withdrawn offers since Wednesday? Which customers still hold genuinely favourable forward prices, and how long are those quotes valid? Every comparison must hold constant the same supplier, meter, CED, start date, term, volume, product, payment terms, credit, unit rates, standing charge, pass-throughs and commission basis. Supplier books update at different times, and today's quote can improve again if peace restores supply.

The buy-window argument is conditional: a short-term price rise rewards customers who already secured a suitable contract, but cannot establish a market bottom, guaranteed profit or automatic wholesale-to-retail pass-through.

Supplier price-book lag is the opportunity and the danger.
Wholesale markets move first. Supplier books often follow.
Once a supplier withdraws a rate, the customer cannot go back and secure yesterday's price.
The market does not need to rise again tomorrow for customers to pay more tomorrow.
