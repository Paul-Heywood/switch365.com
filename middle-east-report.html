Futures Market Explained

Why the forward curve — not today’s headline price — is the customer conversation.

The futures market shows the price for energy delivered in future periods. A customer renewing from 2027, 2028, 2029 or 2030 is not only interested in today’s price. They should look at what the market is currently offering for their actual delivery period.

  • Cheaper further out does not mean cheaper later. It means the future delivery period is priced below the front of the curve today — and that can change.
  • The future market is only useful while the customer can still buy that future delivery period.
  • Waiting lets the market make the decision for the customer.
  • As time passes, that future period becomes the near-term market and can absorb the same risk premium now sitting in W-26.
  • The 1 Year Ago line is context. The latest line is the customer’s decision point.

British Gas 18-Month Window

Verified user-provided internal evidence — treated as live.

British Gas has extended its business energy renewal window from 12 months to 18 months. This gives eligible customers an earlier chance to review future-start pricing.

  • The 18-month British Gas window matters because it turns the forward market into a practical customer action. With S-27 power at -33.5% vs W-26 and W-27 gas at -37.8% vs W-26, the periods this window can reach are exactly where the value sits.
  • Your new contract would not start today. It would begin when your current agreement ends. Securing now locks the future delivery period at today’s forward price.
  • Waiting is not neutral. If the market moves or British Gas withdraws rates, customers cannot go back and secure today’s pricing later.
  • Cheaper further out does not mean cheaper later. It means check whether the future period can be secured now.

Top Five Warning Signs

Lower oil does not remove the warning signs. Relief pricing is not the same as route normalisation.

1

Positive talks are not a deal — nothing is signed or implemented

Talks being positive is useful, but it is not the same as a signed and implemented deal. The US and Iran are still describing the talks differently, and the proposed lane-control / fee structure is exactly what Washington says it will not accept. A breakdown snaps the relief rally.

2

Hormuz is moving but still nowhere near normal

Eight vessels a day versus 130–140 before the war. The biggest route warning is that traffic remains thin, a cargo ship reported a projectile strike off Oman on Tuesday, and shipping confidence — not diplomacy headlines — is what supplier pricing follows.

3

The Iraq countdown risk is still live

Kataib Hezbollah’s 6 August deadline expires within 24–36 hours of this report. The group has called a response “inevitable” and named US “tools” in Saudi Arabia as potential targets. Saudi energy infrastructure risk sits directly behind it.

4

Red Sea risk remains live after a ship was sunk near Yemen

The Indian-flagged sinking — days after the Houthis declared a blockade of Saudi Red Sea ports — shows the alternative route is not clean. Things are spreading out geographically, and that is exactly the type of risk suppliers can price quickly.

5

The forward-value gap versus W-26 can disappear — and the front is +50–62% above last year

Today’s W-26 is far above 1 Year Ago while 2028–2030 sit at or below it. If diplomacy fails, the whole curve re-inflates; if supplier books catch up with July’s wholesale move, quoted rates rise. Either way, waiting is exposure.

  • Talks being alive is better than collapse, but no breakthrough is not implementation. The June 14-point MOU already collapsed once — Trump declared the ceasefire “over” in early July — and its 60-day deadline is about two weeks away.
  • Oman and Tehran talks are positive, but that does not mean the risk is off. Fresh diplomacy has created relief pricing, but Hormuz and wider route confidence have not fully normalised.
  • The US and Iran are still describing the talks differently. Do not confuse diplomacy being discussed with de-escalation being delivered.
  • Oil can move on mediation headlines, but shipping confidence is what matters for supplier pricing. Route normalisation matters more than political optimism.
  • Kataib Hezbollah’s 6 August deadline means the market still has to price countdown risk in Iraq.

Shipping Risk

Hormuz is moving, but it is not normal. The Red Sea route is open, but it is not clean.

Strait of Hormuz

  • Eight vessels transited Tuesday — five tankers and three bulk carriers, steady versus Monday — versus 130–140 daily pre-war transits. Eight vessels through Hormuz is movement, but it is not the same as 130 to 140 daily pre-war transits.
  • LNG: ADNOC’s Mubaraz is on its third Das Island cargo since the war began, due at Dahej, India, on 05 Aug. A QatarEnergy tanker exited last week — the first since 11 July — reportedly with Iranian permission. One LNG tanker moving does not mean LNG movement has normalised.
  • Proposal on the table: inbound lane Iranian-controlled, outbound lane Oman-managed, with service fees discussed; linked to lifting the US port blockade. The US objects to Iranian control or tolls — “no party controls the lanes”.
  • UKMTO: a cargo ship reported being hit by an unknown projectile off Oman on Tuesday; the vessel sustained damage.
  • Hormuz normally carries around one fifth of global crude oil and LNG shipments. It remains the biggest structural risk for LNG confidence.
Reuters/Kpler · LSEG · AP · UKMTO

Bab el-Mandeb / Red Sea / Suez

  • Bab el-Mandeb is not fully closed, but it is still a route-risk market: 20 vessels crossed Tuesday (10 in / 10 out), steady — some vessels are sailing dark and do not appear in counts.
  • Indian-flagged MSV Faize Noore Oliya capsized and sank after a projectile strike near Yemen; all 14 crew rescued by the Yemeni Coast Guard. Yemen’s internationally recognised government blames the Houthis; the group has not commented — attribution unconfirmed.
  • Saudi Arabia has launched a 14-nation maritime defence coalition covering Bab el-Mandeb, the Red Sea and the Gulf of Aden, after Houthi attacks on Saudi tankers and the group’s declared blockade of Saudi Red Sea ports. London insurers widened the Red Sea high-risk zone last week.
  • Suez can help, but it is not a magic fix. Yanbu and the East-West pipeline let Saudi bypass Hormuz — but fully laden VLCCs are not a simple Suez solution. The workaround adds friction, and friction gets priced.
  • Egypt / Damietta: the 29 July drone strike set fires on two gas vessels including the US-owned Energos Winter. Unclaimed — attribution must be treated carefully unless a responsible actor is confirmed.
  • Kazakhstan / CPC: Black Sea loadings resumed 27 July after drone strikes halted them for a week — a secondary route issue, but another proof point that risk is spreading geographically.
Reuters/Kpler · Euronews · AP · Astana Times

Supplier Price-Book Lag

  • Supplier price-book lag is the opportunity and the danger.
  • Wholesale markets move first. Supplier books often follow. UK gas rose ~38% across July — even if oil has fallen from the highs, supplier books may still react to the move that has already happened.
  • Waiting for certainty can mean missing the price movement.
  • Once a supplier withdraws a rate, the customer cannot go back and buy yesterday’s price.
  • Suppliers do not need a full closure of Hormuz or Bab el-Mandeb before they reprice. They also do not need diplomacy to fail completely before they price renewed conflict risk.
  • When conflict spreads geographically, suppliers can price the increased probability of disruption before physical closure happens. This week added Iraq, Saudi, the Red Sea and Egypt to the same risk map.
  • 1 Year Ago prices are useful context, but they do not protect the customer today.
