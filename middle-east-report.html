HORMUZ FLOW RECOVERY COLLIDES WITH RISING TANKER ATTACKS

Yemen front expands while crude and LNG continue to get through

Middle East crude and LNG exports have recovered materially despite the continuing Hormuz conflict, with September LNG shipments through the Strait reaching their highest monthly level since the war began. But the route has become more dangerous again, with UKMTO recording fresh tanker strikes over the weekend, while Yemen's Saudi-backed government has launched a major offensive against the Houthis and the group claims new attacks on Saudi Aramco infrastructure. The physical supply picture has improved, but shipping, insurance and escalation risk remain extremely elevated.

01

Summary

The weekend produced an unusual combination: security risk deteriorated while physical energy flows showed material resilience. Kpler data show Middle East crude exports returned to around pre-war levels by late September, with roughly 10 million barrels per day crossing Hormuz on average in September and weekly flows approaching 13 million barrels per day, while September LNG shipments through the Strait reached their highest monthly level since the war began. At the same time UKMTO recorded three commercial vessels struck by unidentified projectiles in and around Hormuz between 1 and 4 October, including a tanker hit on Sunday with damage to its engine room. More energy is getting through Hormuz, but the route is getting more dangerous.

Yemen escalated materially. On 4 October the internationally recognised, Saudi-backed government announced a major military operation to retake Houthi-held territory after Ansar Allah, commonly called the Houthis, seized the key Taiz to Aden supply route. Independent analysts had not observed offensive action by government forces at the time of writing. The Houthis separately claimed ballistic missile and drone attacks on Saudi Aramco sites in Riyadh and Khurais, with fires observed near a Riyadh refinery and, according to an independent geospatial analyst, near a pumping station at Khurais on the East-West Pipeline route. Material energy damage had not been confirmed by Saudi Arabia or Aramco at the time of reporting.

Markets have so far priced the resilience story more than the escalation story. Brent slipped back towards $101.6 a barrel on Monday morning after the G7 agreed a 100 million barrel reserve release and OPEC+ held November targets steady. TTF front month opened firmer near €75.2/MWh and NBP November traded around 190p/therm in early Monday dealing, both well above pre-war norms but below recent panic peaks. Norway has completed summer maintenance and declared the system winter ready, US LNG exports hit 10.9 million tonnes in September with Europe outbidding Asia, and EU storage sits at 72.4 per cent, weak for the season. The transmission chain that matters for UK business customers runs through LNG, TTF, NBP and supplier price books, and supplier books are where the wholesale move becomes the customer's actual price.

Over the weekend in 30 seconds

  • September LNG shipments through Hormuz reached the highest monthly level since the conflict began: S&P Global counted 19 cargoes, Kpler counted 21.
  • Middle East crude exports recovered strongly, returning to around pre-war levels on Kpler's late-September measures, with Saudi Arabia exporting on both coasts again.
  • Security deteriorated: UKMTO recorded three tankers struck by unidentified projectiles between 1 and 4 October, including an engine-room hit on Sunday.
  • Yemen's Saudi-backed government announced a major offensive against the Houthis on 4 October after the group captured the Taiz to Aden supply route.
  • Houthis claimed attacks on Saudi Aramco facilities in Riyadh and Khurais; smoke and fire were observed, but material production damage requires independent confirmation.
  • Norway and US LNG remain major bearish buffers while European storage at 72.4 per cent remains weak for the season.

What it means for UK energy

Does the improvement in physical LNG supply outweigh the rising security and shipping risk? This morning, partly. Recovered crude flows, record post-war LNG movement, a restarted East-West Pipeline, full Norwegian availability and strong US LNG explain why NBP and TTF are firmer but not spiking despite three tanker strikes and a widening Yemen war. The physical supply picture has improved, but the cost and risk of moving energy remain extreme, and weak European storage means the market stays sensitive to any reversal in LNG flows. For UK businesses the wholesale verdict is visible in our internal offered ranges: W-26 gas is offered at 184.70p/therm, up 2.54p on the previous snapshot, while every later delivery period sits 28 to 63 per cent cheaper. That forward-value gap is the commercial story of the day.

Futures Market Explained

The futures market shows today's price for energy delivered in a future period. A customer renewing from 2027, 2028, 2029 or 2030 should look at the price available today for their actual future delivery period. A customer does not have to wait until their contract end date to see today's price for future delivery.

WHOLESALE FORWARD MARKET (NBP / TTF / UK power futures)
↓
SUPPLIER PRICE BOOK (offered ranges by delivery period)
↓
FUTURE-START CUSTOMER CONTRACT (price secured now, starts at CED)
  • Cheaper further out does not mean cheaper later. A lower price for S-28 or S-30 delivery is today's price for that period, not a forecast of where prices will be in 2028.
  • Backwardation does not mean wait. The customer mistake is thinking a cheaper future period means they should wait. It does not. It means we should check whether that future period can be secured now.
  • The future market is only useful while the customer can still buy that future delivery period. Waiting gives the market time to remove today's forward-value opportunity.
  • The 1 Year Ago line is context. The latest line is the customer's decision point.
  • Waiting is not neutral.

Top Five Warning Signs

1
LNG movement through Hormuz reverses

September's improvement fails and Qatar-linked movement falls sharply. Watch weekly cargo counts from S&P, Kpler and LSEG, and any fresh Ras Laffan disruption. This is the fastest route to a TTF and NBP repricing.

2
Hormuz tanker attacks begin to reduce physical transits

Three vessels struck since 1 October have not yet cut flows. The warning trigger is security risk becoming measurable flow loss: falling daily transit counts, shuttle operators suspending, or escorts failing.

3
Bab al-Mandeb traffic falls during the Yemen offensive

The government counteroffensive meets Houthi coastal control. If territorial war turns into commercial shipping disruption, Red Sea routing, the East-West workaround and Suez economics all deteriorate at once.

4
Verified Saudi energy infrastructure damage removes capacity

Houthi claims on Riyadh and Khurais remain claims until Aramco, the Saudi Energy Ministry or independent evidence confirms production, processing or export loss. A confirmed hit on the East-West bypass after Sunday's reported Khurais fire would upgrade this immediately.

5
NBP / TTF rise and suppliers pull or reprice books

The direct UK customer consequence. With W-26 gas offered at 184.70p, above the ~180p region where books have previously been withdrawn, a further front-end rise raises the probability of books catching up. Once a supplier withdraws a rate, the customer cannot go back and secure yesterday's price.

Ceasefire Reality Check

There is no ceasefire to check this morning; there is a live conflict with recovering physical flows inside it. The honest test is physical, not rhetorical:

  • Direct US-Iran attacks: military operations dialled back in recent weeks towards economic pressure; a third US carrier group reportedly deploying.
  • Tanker attacks: increasing. Three vessels struck since 1 October; 91 damage incidents since February per UKMTO.
  • Hormuz physical flows: improving. Crude and LNG movement at post-war highs in September.
  • Qatar LNG: more cargoes moving, including dark transits; nowhere near pre-war normal.
  • Houthi activity: expanding. Red Sea coast held, Taiz to Aden road taken, Saudi strikes claimed.
  • Bab al-Mandeb: contested war front; physical traffic must be measured separately from territorial control.
  • Diplomacy: indirect contacts alive, core demands unchanged, no agreement and no implementation.
  • Insurance and freight: war-risk pricing persists regardless of flow recovery.
A pause in bombing is not the same as normalisation of the energy system. Talks being alive is better than collapse, but discussion is not implementation. Route normalisation matters more than political optimism.

A shorter period of bombing would not necessarily mean a shorter energy crisis. Repairing pipelines, LNG plants, inventories and commercial shipping confidence can take longer than stopping the shooting. Equally, physical flows can recover before the war ends, and the current Hormuz export recovery demonstrates exactly that.

Shipping Risk

Hormuz

Hormuz is not open as usual. Roughly 10 million bpd of oil crossed daily on average in September, with weekly flows near 13 million bpd, around three quarters of pre-war levels; confirmed Saudi crude crossing the strait jumped from 0.7m bpd in August to 2.8m bpd in September (Kpler). September LNG transits were the highest of the war. Against that, three tankers have been struck since 1 October, including Sunday's unknown-projectile hit that damaged an engine room. Visible vessel count and physical energy volume are different measures: dark transits and shuttle operations mean AIS counts understate what is actually moving. Insurance and freight remain at war-risk levels, and responsibility for the latest strikes has not been independently established.

Qatar LNG

S&P Global counted 19 September LNG shipments through Hormuz while Kpler counted 21, both showing a substantial improvement from earlier in the conflict. Three more Ras Laffan-linked carriers (Al Kharaitiyat, Al Gharrafa and Milaha Qatar) reappeared outside the strait last week after AIS-dark transits; the Milaha Qatar was discharging at Hazira, India. One Qatar-linked cargo getting through is evidence of resilience, not evidence of normalisation, but several successful cargoes matter, and the sustained improvement is a genuine bearish development for TTF and NBP. LPG movement is not LNG normalisation, and a VLGC is generally associated with LPG unless the source establishes LNG.

Bab al-Mandeb

Houthi control of key positions around Bab al-Mandeb materially increases their ability to threaten or restrict shipping, but physical traffic must be assessed separately. Territorial control and physical traffic are different measures. The declared government offensive and the Houthi capture of the Taiz to Aden road raise the risk that the land war moves closer to the coast. Bab al-Mandeb may remain physically active while carrying materially higher security risk; fresh traffic counts are the metric to watch this week.

Saudi Red Sea

Saudi Arabia's East-West Pipeline has resumed operations following September's drone attack, restoring part of the kingdom's Red Sea export option around Hormuz. Argus put throughput at about 5.5m bpd on 2 October, above 75% of the 7m bpd capacity, with a temporary bypass routing crude around the most badly damaged of three hit pumping stations. Yanbu loadings have resumed, with an estimated 4 to 4.5m bpd available for export. The reported 4 October fire near a Khurais pumping station on the pipeline route is unverified for operational impact and must be fresh-checked before the pipeline is described as either fully normalised or newly impaired. Restarted is not the same as full capacity restored. Current Yanbu inventory is not verified.

Workarounds

If Hormuz is the problem, Saudi and Red Sea infrastructure are part of the workaround, and workarounds show resilience and disruption at the same time. The UAE's ADCOP line to Fujairah reaches open water outside the strait; Saudi exports are running on both coasts with Ras Tanura at about 3.6m bpd in September; ship-to-ship transfers in the Gulf of Oman remain part of the system. Suez can help, but it is not a magic fix: fully laden VLCCs are not a simple Suez solution and typically need SUMED, part-discharge or smaller vessels. Cape of Good Hope routing adds weeks and cost. Pressure on the workaround matters even before the workaround stops working. A route can remain physically open and still carry a material war-risk premium. Friction gets priced.

Switch365 internal commercial evidence: when wholesale gas previously reached around 180p/therm, some suppliers withdrew books, suspended pricing or returned with higher pricing. That is internal experience, not an official threshold or industry rule. With W-26 gas now offered at 184.70p, the market is above that region again.

Supplier price-book lag is the opportunity and the danger. Wholesale markets move first. Supplier books often follow.
  • Wholesale has already moved. The question now is when supplier books catch up.
  • Once a supplier withdraws a rate, the customer cannot go back and secure yesterday's price.
  • The market does not need to rise again tomorrow for customers to pay more tomorrow: a book catching up to Friday's wholesale is enough.
  • Waiting for certainty can mean missing the price movement. We do not claim repricing has occurred; no book movement is confirmed this morning.
