Summary

Visible Qatar-linked LNG traffic through the Strait of Hormuz has increased materially. GasLog Skagen reappeared off Sri Lanka on 27 September carrying a Ras Laffan cargo, and Al Shamal, Mesaimeer, Al Ghashamiya, Al Samriya, Al Daayen and Shandong Redwood have all made visible Qatar-linked movements over the past fortnight, while Al Mafyar re-entered the Strait in ballast. Because many vessels run dark with AIS switched off, visible movements may still understate actual traffic. This is a meaningful bearish physical gas signal: it proves some Qatar LNG movement is possible. It does not prove normal operations have returned.

Contractual supply failure has extended at the same time. QatarEnergy told Edison on Monday that force majeure is extended until early December, cancelling six further cargoes and taking the total affected to 35 cargoes, approximately 4.6 bcm. Edison has replaced 23 cargoes, mainly with US supply, and says customer commitments are covered. Asian clients, including Bangladesh and Pakistan, have had force majeure extended into November. Visible LNG movement is not contractual supply normalisation; the correct gas signal is improving movement alongside continuing delivery failure.

Diplomacy continues without agreement. US and Iranian officials spoke separately with mediators on Monday about an amended version of Iran's proposal. Iran says its negotiated solution remains available, but Trump denied offering sanctions relief or frozen funds for nuclear concessions, writing that he offered Iran "NOTHING". His earlier post-midterm timing for a deal conflicts with his latest claim that the war will be won "very soon", so treat timing as contradictory political signalling, not a timetable. Brent traded at $106.77/bbl, up 1.4%, and WTI at $93.94/bbl, up 1.5%, at 03:26 GMT Tuesday, and benchmark British gas rose approximately 3% on Monday after the Iranian proposal was rejected. The commercial test is the exact live NBP, TTF, power and supplier price the customer can secure today.

Futures Market Explained

What the forward curve is

The futures market shows today's price for energy delivered in a future period. A customer does not have to wait until their contract end date to see today's price for future delivery.

The customer mistake

Cheaper further out does not mean cheaper later. The customer mistake is thinking a cheaper future period means they should wait. It does not. It means we should check whether that future period can be secured now.

Backwardation

Backwardation does not mean wait. Today's gas curve is steeply backwardated: W-26 at 186.00p versus S-30 at 67.49p. The future market is only useful while the customer can still buy that future delivery period.

Waiting

Waiting is not neutral. Waiting gives the market time to remove today's forward-value opportunity, and once a supplier withdraws a rate, the customer cannot go back and secure yesterday's price.

British Gas 18-Month Window

British Gas has extended its business energy renewal window from 12 months to 18 months. This gives eligible customers an earlier chance to review future-start pricing. The 18-month British Gas window matters because it turns the forward market into a practical customer action: with S-28 gas 54.6% and S-29 power 54.6% below their front-winter levels, eligibility can be checked against live forward value rather than against a renewal panic later.

Your new contract would not start today. It would begin when your current agreement ends. British Gas 18-month eligibility turns this market intelligence into a practical customer action, but eligibility is not automatic and supplier appetite can change while wholesale remains above the levels at which books have previously been withdrawn.

Waiting is not neutral. If the market moves or British Gas withdraws rates, customers cannot go back and secure today's pricing later. Cheaper further out does not mean cheaper later. It means check whether the future period can be secured now.

Supplier price-book lag is the opportunity and the danger. Wholesale markets move first. Supplier books often follow. Once a supplier withdraws a rate, the customer cannot go back and secure yesterday's price. The market does not need to rise again tomorrow for customers to pay more tomorrow.
