Summary

Today's hero: the Hormuz threat, UK gas above 180p/therm and supplier price-book risk, in that order.

The energy risk moved directly into UK gas prices this morning. ICE UK NBP October is trading above 181p/therm, November is approaching 186p/therm and Q4 is above 183p/therm, putting UK gas at or around the highest levels seen since the war began. Near-term UK gas has moved around 10% in a single session, and the internal offered range now confirms the move has reached the customer-facing market: W-26 gas is 184.75p/therm on the Wed 02 Sep AM snapshot, up 8.25p on the day.

The move follows the most serious US-Iran military exchange in weeks. US forces completed a major wave of strikes on Tuesday against IRGC air-defence sites, radar systems, maritime assets and facilities, mine-laying capabilities and communications sites, and Axios reports the US struck two IRGC tankers under a new "tanker for tanker" policy, the first direct US strikes on Iranian ships of the war. Only four tracked commodity vessels crossed Hormuz on Tuesday, against a recent ten-day average of roughly 13 to 14.

The most important customer question today is not whether wholesale gas will go higher. It is whether supplier price books have fully caught up with a 10% wholesale move. Wholesale markets move first. Supplier price books often follow. The Wednesday morning internal snapshot has already repriced the front of the curve, so the window on pre-surge pricing is closing season by season, not all at once.

Futures Market Explained

Sixty seconds of context so the rest of the report lands properly.

The futures market shows today's price for energy delivered in a future period. A customer does not have to wait until their contract end date to see today's market for future delivery. Winter 2027, Summer 2028 and beyond are all priced and tradable today.

  • Cheaper further out does not mean cheaper later. It means the future period is priced lower today, and the question is whether it can be secured today.
  • Backwardation does not mean wait. A downward-sloping curve reflects today's stressed front end, not a promise about tomorrow's prices.
  • The future market is only useful while the customer can still buy that future delivery period. Waiting gives the market time to remove today's forward-value opportunity.
  • The customer mistake is thinking a cheaper future period means they should wait. It does not. It means we should check whether that future period can be secured now.

The Risk
  • IRGC threat: the IRGC says US attacks will "tighten the lock" on the Strait of Hormuz. The significance of the threat will ultimately be measured by what happens to ships.

  • Qatar LNG: force majeure now extending towards early November on the Edison arrangement; 29 cargoes affected, approximately 3.8 bcm of gas, under that specific supply arrangement.
  • Storage: EU storage at about 65% is well below the seasonal norm at the point the injection season starts to tail off. Europe does not have to run out of gas for low storage to increase prices.
  • Wholesale surge: near-term UK gas has moved around 10% in a single session and the internal front season has followed to 184.75p. The market is now at or around the highest levels seen since the war began.
The Opportunity
  • Supplier price-book lag, now season-specific: the Wednesday snapshot has repriced the front but not the far curve. Wholesale markets move first. Supplier books often follow, and the following is still visible in the out-years.
  • Backwardation: S-28, S-29 and S-30 gas are priced 56% to 64% below W-26. That value exists only while it can still be bought.
  • Future-start contracts: a new contract would not start today; it would begin when the current agreement ends. Today's forward curve can still be relevant to a customer whose deal ends next year.
  • British Gas 18-month window: eligible customers can review future-start pricing earlier than the old 12-month rhythm allowed.
  • Quotes issued before this morning: any quote produced against Tuesday's or older books now sits below the Wednesday curve. Where a supplier is still prepared to honour it, that gap may not remain open.
Is there a window between the wholesale move and supplier repricing? Partially, and it is narrowing season by season. The front has already repriced in the internal Wednesday snapshot; the far curve has not fully followed. We have seen this sequence before: when wholesale gas previously reached around 180p/therm, suppliers began withdrawing prices and returning with more expensive books. We are back above that wholesale level this morning. Suppliers could withdraw or reprice at any time.

Supplier Price-Book Lag

WHOLESALE HAS MOVED. HAVE SUPPLIERS?

Current NBP Surge: Through 180p/therm (user-provided ICE evidence, 02/09/2026, plus internal W-26)

ICE UK NBP contracts and the internal W-26 offered range, morning of 02/09/2026. The horizontal zone above 180p/therm is the level that Switch365 internal experience links to supplier price-book withdrawals. 180p is not a magical technical barrier; it is a useful supplier-repricing warning level based on recent Switch365 experience.

ICE NBP Nov-26 is around 185.82p/therm and has moved approximately 10.7% in one session. This morning's internal snapshot answers part of the supplier question directly: the Wednesday AM W-26 range is 184.75p/therm, up 8.25p on the day, so the front of the customer-facing curve has now caught up with the wholesale surge. The lag that remains is in the out-years, which repriced by between 0.86p and 5.75p. Switch365 has seen suppliers react to previous moves around the 180p level by pulling books, temporarily stopping pricing and reissuing at higher levels. We have seen this sequence before: when wholesale gas previously reached around 180p/therm, suppliers began withdrawing prices and returning with more expensive books. We are back above that wholesale level this morning.

  • Supplier price-book lag is the opportunity and the danger.
  • Wholesale markets move first. Supplier books often follow.
  • Once a supplier withdraws a rate, the customer cannot go back and secure yesterday's price.
  • Any supplier still holding a pre-surge price could represent a temporary window, and this morning that window is most visible in the 2028 to 2030 seasons.
A 10% wholesale move does not translate directly into a 10% customer bill increase, because customer pricing also includes shaping, risk, network charges, levies, standing charges, supplier margin and contract structure. But it materially increases supplier repricing pressure, and this morning's internal snapshot shows that pressure already acting on the front of the curve.

Price-Book Lag, Explained Simply

Wholesale market jumps, then the supplier's existing book may remain open briefly. A customer may still access a price calculated from the previous market. Then the supplier reviews its exposure, withdraws the book, and a new book is issued at a higher level. The sequence is mechanical, which is why the window is real but short. This morning's snapshot shows the sequence mid-way through: front repriced, far curve still catching up.

Waiting Is Not Neutral

Customer waits, wholesale does not necessarily need to rise further, the supplier catches up with today's wholesale movement, and the customer receives a higher price. The market does not need to rise again tomorrow for customers to pay more tomorrow. Wholesale has already moved. The question now is when supplier books catch up, and this morning the answer at the front is: they already have. There are multiple credible events capable of pushing the wholesale market higher again, but customers do not even need another rise for supplier prices to increase from here.
