SECTION 01

Summary

Houthi forces captured the Red Sea port city of Mocha on Thursday 10 September after Saudi-backed government forces withdrew, and have since pushed south along Yemen's western coast, seizing Zuqar Island and attacking the Hanish Islands. Government forces are regrouping towards Dhubab, the town that sits directly on Bab al-Mandeb opposite Perim Island. Reuters reports that Yemeni government, Iranian and regional sources say Iran's Revolutionary Guards provided guidance, weapons, funding and senior officers for the campaign. Tehran publicly denies directing the Houthis. The Houthis do not control Bab al-Mandeb itself, but their ability to exert pressure on the approaches has increased.

The balance matters as much as the escalation. Kpler data shows 26 commodity vessels transited Bab al-Mandeb on Thursday against a 10-day average of roughly 27, so physical traffic has not collapsed. Axios reported, citing US officials, that Saudi Crown Prince Mohammed bin Salman twice asked President Trump for US strikes on the Houthis and that Trump declined, with Washington seeking to avoid another direct military front; Reuters could not independently verify the account. Pakistan has clarified that no military response under the Mecca defence pact is currently being discussed, while reserving the right to act when the time comes.

Hormuz remains severely impaired, with Kpler tracking seven commodity vessel transits on Thursday against a 10-day average of 15 and a pre-war norm near 125 large vessels a day. Yet the QatarEnergy-linked carrier Al Marrouna berthed at Port Qasim in Pakistan on Thursday with a Ras Laffan cargo, the first known Qatar-linked LNG shipment through Hormuz since late July, and two further ballast QatarEnergy-linked vessels, Al Ghashamiya and Al Daayen, have appeared inside the strait. UK NBP quarterly futures remain around 200p/therm after Wednesday's 203.18p intraday high, with Thursday's settlement broadly flat at 199.11p, while Brent settled at $107.63/bbl on Thursday and traded between $107 and $110 early Friday. The supplier-book question is now the commercial centre of the story: internal Switch365 evidence shows the offered W-26 gas range already at 202.00p/therm on Thursday morning, up 6.25p on the day.

SECTION 05

Futures Market Explained

The futures market shows today's price for energy delivered in a future period. A customer does not have to wait until their contract end date to see today's price for future delivery. When the curve is in backwardation, as it is now, the market is charging a crisis premium for near-term delivery and far less for 2027 to 2030.

Cheaper further out does not mean cheaper later. The customer mistake is thinking a cheaper future period means they should wait. It does not. It means we should check whether that future period can be secured now. Backwardation does not mean wait.

The future market is only useful while the customer can still buy that future delivery period. Waiting gives the market time to remove today's forward-value opportunity. Waiting is not neutral.

Hormuz→ Qatar LNG→ Global LNG competition→ TTF→ NBP→ UK gas→ Gas-fired power→ Supplier book→ Customer contract

SECTION 08

British Gas 18-Month Window

British Gas has extended its business energy renewal window from 12 months to 18 months. This gives eligible customers an earlier chance to review future-start pricing. The 18-month British Gas window matters because it turns the forward market into a practical customer action.

Your new contract would not start today. It would begin when your current agreement ends. With S-27 gas offered 33.7% below the W-26 front and W-27 37.1% below, a customer whose contract ends within the window can test today's forward pricing against a front market at crisis-era levels, without taking delivery risk now.

Waiting is not neutral. If the market moves or British Gas withdraws rates, customers cannot go back and secure today's pricing later. Cheaper further out does not mean cheaper later: it means check whether the future period can be secured now.

Two chokepoints, two different problems. Hormuz is the physical disruption; Bab al-Mandeb is the growing territorial risk. Kpler tracked seven commodity vessel transits of Hormuz on Thursday: visible vessel count and physical energy volume are different measures, since some vessels operate with AIS transponders off. Hormuz is not open as usual, but a route can remain physically open and still carry a material war-risk premium.

Qatar LNG. Al Marrouna berthed at Port Qasim on Thursday morning with roughly 142,000 cubic metres of LNG from Ras Laffan, having transited Hormuz on 7 September under a government-to-government arrangement. Two ballast QatarEnergy-linked vessels, Al Ghashamiya and Al Daayen, appeared inside the strait on 9 September, and Bloomberg-cited traders expect a further Qatari cargo for Pakistan in the coming days. If several LNG cargoes begin moving, this could become a major bearish gas development. One Qatar-linked LNG cargo getting through is evidence of resilience, not normalisation.

Bab al-Mandeb. The strait is about 29km wide at its narrowest point and carried roughly 7% of global oil output in June (Reuters / Kpler). Security risk around the southern Red Sea has increased materially following the capture of Mocha, while physical traffic through Bab al-Mandeb continues. Bab al-Mandeb does not need to close for higher shipping friction to become economically important.

Saudi Red Sea and workarounds. If Hormuz is the problem, Saudi and Red Sea infrastructure are part of the workaround: the East-West Pipeline to Yanbu, UAE eastern ports, Egypt, SUMED, Suez, ship-to-ship transfers and the Cape. SECTION 17

Supplier Price-Book Lag

Wholesale is around 200p. What price is still sitting on supplier books?

Late Aug ~167p→ 1 Sep 180p+→ 8 Sep 190p+→ 9 Sep 203.18p intraday→ 10 Sep 199.11p close→ 11 Sep: offered W-26 already 202.00p

Switch365 previously observed supplier books being withdrawn and repriced when wholesale gas was around 180p/therm. The quarterly benchmark has now traded above 200p. Thursday's internal snapshot shows the front of the offered range has now caught up: W-26 gas at 202.00p, up 6.25p in a day. The far curve, by contrast, has barely moved: S-30 gas is still offered at 65.74p and W-29 actually fell 0.33p.

Supplier price-book lag is the opportunity and the danger. Wholesale markets move first. Supplier books often follow. The market does not need to rise again tomorrow for customers to pay more tomorrow: a book repriced overnight costs more even on a flat day. Once a supplier withdraws a rate, the customer cannot go back and secure yesterday's price.

Check the actual supplier book. The correct Friday action is not to watch the war; it is to test which suppliers are still writing 2027 to 2030 delivery at yesterday's levels, and for how long those quotes remain valid.
