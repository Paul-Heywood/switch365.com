01Summary

What has changed that could change the price a UK business can actually secure today?

Yemeni government sources say the Houthis launched a broad offensive across western Red Sea coast frontlines overnight, intended to advance towards areas bordering the Bab al-Mandab Strait. Heavy fighting was reported west of Taiz and along the corridor towards Mokha, with government forces saying the Houthis fired more than a dozen ballistic missiles towards areas around Mokha and al-Khokha in Hodeidah governorate, alongside drone attacks. Military sources reported government and Houthi casualties on both sides, and government-aligned forces said two explosive-laden boats detonated near Mokha Port after being engaged. These battlefield claims remain attributed to Yemeni military sources; what is independently visible is that fighting on the western coastal front has intensified materially within the last 24 hours.

The importance is geographical. Hormuz remains severely impaired: commodity transits fell to around 7 vessels on 3 September against a recent 10-day average near 13, and Iran's PGSA blacklist of "non-compliant" vessels was expanded by 11 ships on Wednesday to a total of 56. That impairment has increased the importance of Saudi Arabia's East-West pipeline, Yanbu, the Red Sea, Bab al-Mandab, Suez and SUMED as alternative routes for Middle Eastern energy. The energy risk is no longer concentrated around one Middle East chokepoint. If Hormuz remains impaired while security deteriorates around Bab al-Mandab, the flexibility available to reroute Middle Eastern energy becomes more limited and more expensive.

Markets, however, pulled back before this news landed. Internal Switch365 supplier evidence shows W-26 gas offered at 181.00p/therm on Thursday morning, down 3.75p on Wednesday, with every internal gas season from W-26 to S-30 still priced 33-64% below the front of the curve. Public NBP references eased to around 178-180p/therm on Thursday after exceeding 183p earlier in the week, and TTF front-month eased to roughly €71.3/MWh. Today's open market question is whether the new Red Sea risk reverses Thursday's gas relief.

Overnight in 30 seconds

What it means for UK energy

Bab al-Mandab is not closed, but its security risk has increased at exactly the moment Hormuz disruption has made the Red Sea route more strategically important. That is a risk amplifier rather than an immediate physical supply loss: it widens the range of events capable of repricing UK gas, power and supplier books quickly. With gas still historically elevated after this week's war-high spike and supplier price books reacting at different speeds to the surge and the pullback, the practical task today is unchanged: check what price the customer can actually secure now.

Interpretation

1. Internal supplier rates. Thursday's internal snapshots show the front of the gas curve giving back a slice of this week's spike: W-26 eased 3.75p to 181.00p/therm while S-27 through S-29 edged 0.50-0.75p higher and W-29 and S-30 fell back. This week's surge was concentrated at the front; the pullback has not fully travelled down the curve.

2. NBP. Public NBP references closed Thursday around 178-180p/therm, down roughly 1.4-2.3% on the day, after exceeding 183p/therm earlier in the week, the strongest levels since early 2023. UK gas remains historically elevated after retreating from this week's war-high spike.

3. TTF. European front-month gas eased about 3.1% to roughly €71.3/MWh on Thursday after touching around €74.3/MWh earlier in the week, and was little changed near €71.6 early Friday. Europe is trading the same story as NBP, not a separate one.

4. Qatar LNG. Conflict-period exports of around 18 cargoes versus about 509 in the comparable period a year earlier (roughly a 96% reduction on a cumulative comparable-period basis) remain the biggest physical link between the Gulf conflict and UK gas. QatarEnergy has extended force majeure affecting some LNG deliveries under its Edison contract until early November.

5. Storage. EU storage was 65.4% full as of 30 August, below the five-year minimum, with Germany materially weaker than normal. Storage is a buffer, not immunity. Europe does not need to run out of gas for low storage to increase prices.

6. Norway. Thursday brought reduced Troll capacity and a Kollsnes process issue, partly offset by rising Langeled flows to the UK; no fresh major Norwegian outage was found in this morning's check. Strong Norwegian supply materially cushions LNG disruption.

7. US LNG. US LNG remains one of the strongest physical buffers against Qatar disruption: record production, strong export flows and US storage above the five-year average, with Henry Hub near $3/MMBtu.

8. Hormuz. Hormuz remains severely impaired. Around 7 commodity vessels transited on 3 September versus a 10-day average near 13, and the blacklist expansion to 56 vessels keeps commercial behaviour defensive. Visible vessel count and physical energy volume are different measures: more than 17 million barrels of oil moved through Hormuz on Monday, so this is not a total physical closure, but the consistency of those transits is the open question.

9. Bab al-Mandab as risk amplifier. The overnight Houthi offensive towards the Mokha corridor does not automatically reprice NBP pound-for-pound. It raises the risk around the very route that has become more valuable as a Hormuz workaround. That is a risk amplifier.

10. Supplier price-book response. The commercial question for today: did Thursday's wholesale pullback reach customer prices before the new Red Sea risk arrived? Internal evidence says the front season has eased; whether every supplier book has followed is exactly what needs checking on live quotes.

Transmission chain, locked: Hormuz → Qatar LNG → global LNG competition → TTF → NBP → UK gas → gas-fired UK power → supplier books → customer contract. Britain does not need to lose a specific Qatari cargo for Qatar disruption to raise UK gas prices.

05Futures Market Explained

The futures market shows today's price for energy delivered in a future period. A customer does not have to wait until their contract end date to see today's price for future delivery.

Right now the internal curve is in steep backwardation: W-26 gas at 181.00p/therm prices the conflict premium into this coming winter, while S-29 gas at 67.50p/therm prices a market that expects normalisation. 180p near-term gas does not mean all future gas costs 180p.

Cheaper further out does not mean cheaper later. The customer mistake is thinking a cheaper future period means they should wait. It does not. It means we should check whether that future period can be secured now. Backwardation does not mean wait. The future market is only useful while the customer can still buy that future delivery period. Waiting gives the market time to remove today's forward-value opportunity.

Hormuz

Hormuz is not open as usual. Commodity transits fell to around 7 on 3 September versus a recent 10-day average near 13, traffic has concentrated on the Iranian-route corridor, and the blacklist now covers 56 vessels across crude, LNG, LPG and product tonnage, with STS partners threatened with listing. At least three Indian refiners and one major international energy company planned to avoid some listed vessels. That is the blacklist working through commercial behaviour, not just rhetoric.

Bab al-Mandab

Bab al-Mandab has not been closed, but its security risk has risen. Overnight, Houthi forces opened a broad offensive on the al-Khokha to Mokha axis, with ballistic missiles, drones and, according to government-aligned forces, two explosive-laden boats destroyed near Mokha Port. Independent shipping data shows traffic down about 15% since the 20 July Houthi blockade declaration on Saudi-linked shipping, but stabilised: 202 traceable transits in the week to 30 August, and no confirmed commercial-vessel attack since 24 August. A workaround is only valuable while it remains commercially usable.

Two-chokepoint risk

Before the Iran conflict, Hormuz and Bab al-Mandab were two separate shipping-risk stories. Now they are connected: Hormuz disruption pushed more Gulf energy towards the Saudi western route (East-West pipeline to Yanbu, then Bab al-Mandab or Suez/SUMED). If Bab al-Mandab security deteriorates, ships can still use the Cape and physical energy does not automatically disappear, but distance, journey times, freight, insurance and vessel availability all tighten. Friction gets priced.

Qatar LNG physical movements

LNG remains the bridge between Hormuz and UK gas. Qatar's conflict-period exports of around 18 cargoes versus about 509 a year earlier remain the core physical driver. A QatarEnergy-managed tanker exited Hormuz in late July, the first such exit since 11 July, and individual Qatar-controlled tankers have periodically headed into the strait since, but nothing in this morning's check indicates normalisation. LPG movement is not LNG normalisation.

Oil counterpoint

US officials said more than 17 million barrels of oil moved through Hormuz on Monday. Meaningful physical oil can still transit, large vessels and dark traffic matter, and vessel count does not equal energy volume. The open questions are whether those large oil transits can be repeated consistently, and separately whether LNG is normalising. Oil and LNG are not the same story.

STS workarounds

Reuters reported three Qatar / UAE LNG cargoes being transferred between ships outside Hormuz for onward delivery to India and Japan. Ship-to-ship LNG transfers are unusual. Workarounds show resilience and disruption at the same time: workarounds reduce outright shortage risk, but increase time, cost and complexity.

Saudi workarounds and the Cape

The East-West pipeline and Yanbu remain the principal Gulf workaround, which is precisely why the overnight offensive towards the Mokha corridor matters. A 13-nation Saudi-led maritime coalition for Bab al-Mandab reached initial operational capability in late July, but escort tasking remains sovereign opt-in. The Cape of Good Hope remains the ultimate longer route, at higher freight and insurance cost.

17Supplier Price-Book Lag

Wholesale moved hard this week. What have suppliers done?

UK gas surges into 180p+ territory (Wed: internal W-26 184.75p)
↓
Supplier repricing risk rises
↓
Wholesale subsequently pulls back (Thu: internal W-26 181.00p, -3.75)
↓
Some supplier books may still reflect the higher market; others may still reflect older pricing

Switch365 internal commercial experience: when wholesale previously reached approximately 180p/therm, some suppliers withdrew books, temporarily stopped pricing, and returned with higher prices. Today's task is to check what suppliers actually did after this week's surge: who pulled books, who repriced, who is still holding, who has reopened, who shortened validity, and who is cheaper after Thursday's pullback.

Supplier price-book lag is the opportunity and the danger. Wholesale markets move first. Supplier books often follow. Once a supplier withdraws a price, the customer cannot secure it retrospectively. And a wholesale pullback does not mean every supplier has already reduced its price. Check the book, not just the wholesale screen. The correct question is what price the customer can actually secure today, not what wholesale did yesterday.
