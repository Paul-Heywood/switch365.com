DURATION RISK: RISING

CONFLICT HARDENS AS UAE CUTS IRAN TIES AND US THREATENS GLOBAL ECONOMIC ISOLATION

The evidence for a rapid resolution is weakening while the economic and physical consequences of the conflict continue to spread.

The Middle East energy crisis is increasingly moving from a short-term shipping shock towards a prolonged economic and geopolitical problem. The UAE has suspended all trade, commercial exchanges and financial transactions with Iran after saying it detected two Iranian ballistic missiles targeting maritime traffic, an allegation Tehran denies. President Trump has now threatened major economic consequences against any country providing Iran with a commercial or financial lifeline, describing the approach as “economic warfare and isolation on an unprecedented scale”. At the same time, the US and Iran remain publicly deadlocked over negotiations and Hormuz, with the latest confirmed Kpler data showing only six commodity-vessel transits on Tuesday against a pre-war norm of well over a hundred vessel movements a day. Brent settled at $91.62 on Wednesday, its highest close since 24 July. For UK businesses, the key question is no longer simply whether another attack happens tonight. It is whether prolonged route disruption, LNG replacement costs and supplier risk premiums are becoming embedded before the customer's next contract starts.

Section 05

Futures Market Explained

What the forward curve is — and what it is not — for a UK business customer deciding when to fix.

The futures market shows the price available today for energy delivered in a future period. It is a current pricing relationship, not a forecast.
If 2027, 2028, 2029 or 2030 is cheaper than W-26, that is a current pricing relationship. It is not a forecast that the customer should wait.
Cheaper further out does not mean cheaper later. A cheaper future period can be repriced upwards at any time before it is secured.
Backwardation does not mean wait. Waiting gives the market time to remove today's forward-value opportunity.
The future market is only useful while the customer can still buy that future delivery period. Once suppliers reprice or withdraw, the opportunity is gone.
The customer mistake is thinking a cheaper future period means they should wait. It does not. It means we should check whether that future period can be secured now.

British Gas 18-Month Window

British Gas has extended its business energy renewal window from 12 months to 18 months. This gives eligible customers an earlier chance to review future-start pricing.

Why it matters today

The 18-month British Gas window matters because it turns the forward market into a practical customer action. The backwardation visible in today's offered curves is only useful if a supplier will let the customer buy it — British Gas now will, up to 18 months out.
Your new contract would not start today. It would begin when your current agreement ends — at the pricing secured now.

The risk of waiting

Waiting is not neutral. If the market moves or British Gas withdraws rates, customers cannot go back and secure today's pricing later.
Cheaper further out does not mean cheaper later. It means check whether the future period can be secured now — and for eligible British Gas customers, it can be.

Market Drivers

The forces actually moving UK business energy pricing this morning, in priority order. Facts, direction, commercial meaning.

1. UAE–Iran Economic Rupture

  • UAE suspended all trade, commercial exchanges and financial transactions with Iran until further notice.
  • Trigger: UAE says two Iranian ballistic missiles targeted maritime traffic; both fell into the sea. Iran rejects the allegation as baseless.
  • Dubai has been one of Iran's most important commercial and financial lifelines.
  • Meaning: removing that channel raises pressure on Tehran — compromise may become more attractive or more difficult. Either outcome can generate volatility.

2. Trump Economic-Isolation Threat

  • Trump: any country whose banks, businesses, airports or government entities provide Iran a lifeline faces “tremendous economic consequences”.
  • Described as “economic warfare and isolation on an unprecedented scale” and an “economic D-Day”.
  • No named countries, no enforcement mechanism yet; Treasury's Bessent says “maximum pressure” ordered.
  • Meaning: a duration-risk signal — Washington is preparing economic tools beyond a short military confrontation.

3. Hormuz Still Restricted

  • Six commodity-vessel transits Tuesday, nine Monday; 10-day average 11.
  • Pre-war norm: roughly 130–140 vessel movements a day; a fifth of global oil and LNG supply.
  • Iran says shut until conditions met; Trump says open. Physical flows are the reality check.
  • Meaning: the route is commercially impaired even where physically navigable.

4. Iran “Fully Offensive” Posture

  • A senior Iranian official told Reuters (17 Aug) Tehran is shifting from defence to a “fully offensive” posture.
  • Iran warned it could escalate within weeks if its conditions are not implemented.
  • FT reports Iran eyeing military targets in Europe if Trump escalates.
  • Meaning: one of the strongest signals Tehran is preparing for escalation rather than assuming diplomacy succeeds.

5. Diplomatic Deadlock

  • 60-day negotiating window under the June MoU expired Monday without a final settlement; Trump refused extension.
  • Trump Tuesday: “no talks or conversations going on, or scheduled.” Kushner Monday: “very positive and active conversations.”
  • Iran: open to dialogue, will not negotiate on the basis of surrender.
  • Meaning: backchannel communication is not the same as a formal negotiation.

6. Qatar LNG

  • QatarEnergy force majeure now in a fourth month; Edison cargoes cancelled through mid-September (21 cargoes, ~2.7 bcm).
  • ~17% of Qatari capacity (12.8 mtpa) could remain offline for up to five years after the Ras Laffan attacks.
  • Asian and European buyers compete for the same Atlantic / US / African replacement cargoes.
  • Meaning: LNG remains the direct bridge between Hormuz and UK gas.

7. Shipping Adaptation

  • Two Chinese state-controlled shippers (>100 VLCCs, ~half of China's pre-war Middle East oil trade) avoiding both chokepoints since late July.
  • Saudi Aramco resumed some loadings inside Hormuz and is offering STS transfers off Fujairah.
  • Oman–China VLCC freight ~$140k/day vs $30–40k pre-war.
  • Meaning: when the world's largest tanker operators redesign routes, that is not normalisation.

8. Bearish Counterweights

  • US crude inventories +4.4m bbl to 428.8m (week ended 14 Aug); stocks at five-year average.
  • Saudi workarounds: three VLCCs loaded 12–16 Aug; six more possible this month; Sidi Kerir ~670k bpd for Asia.
  • Norway flowing strong on a light maintenance year; French nuclear curbs easing from 18 Aug; UK wind/solar above norm.
  • Meaning: the market is not universally bullish — but each counterweight is partial.

9. Forward Curve & Supplier Books

  • Internal offered curves: gas S-29 at -61.1% vs W-26; power S-29 at -51.6%.
  • Front up two days running (+6.25p on W-26 gas since Tuesday); far gas periods S-29/W-29/S-30 now below their 1 Year Ago levels.
  • Supplier price books typically lag wholesale; repricing risk now runs both ways.
  • Meaning: the forward-value gap exists today; it is not guaranteed to exist next month.

  • Chinese state shippers: >100 VLCCs kept clear of both chokepoints since late July; ~12 VLCCs scheduled for Fujairah / Oman loadings Aug–mid-Sep.

Workarounds prove energy can move — but they also prove operators are redesigning trade around the conflict. Some energy continues to move; workaround capacity should not be confused with normal trade. The workaround adds friction, and friction gets priced.
The normalisation test is not whether one vessel can cross. It is vessel counts, crude volumes, LNG carriers, VLCCs, insurance, shipowner willingness, dark voyages, attacks, blockade enforcement, Iranian restrictions and freight costs — and on those measures, normal commercial energy flows have not returned.

Section 17

Supplier Price-Book Lag

The strongest commercial section of this report — because it is where geopolitics becomes a customer's invoice.

Supplier price-book lag is the opportunity and the danger. Wholesale markets move first. Supplier books often follow.
Waiting for certainty can mean missing the price movement. Once a supplier withdraws a rate, the customer cannot go back and secure yesterday's price.
Suppliers do not need Hormuz to fully close before repricing. They do not need the war to be officially forecast to last another year. They do not need Qatar to lose every LNG cargo. They price probability, volatility, replacement cost, freight, collateral and risk.
The widening economic confrontation — the UAE embargo and Washington's isolation threat — gives suppliers another reason to reassess risk even without a spectacular overnight attack.
This week's evidence: NBP public gas at a four-week high, TTF up roughly €8–10 in a fortnight, Brent at four-week highs, and the internal offered gas curve up two days running at the front — +6.25p on W-26 and +6.25p on S-27 since Tuesday morning. Wholesale moved first. Supplier books may still follow.
The lag cuts both ways for the customer: it is the reason today's forward-value gap can still be bought — and the reason it may not be buyable for long.
