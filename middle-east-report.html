Futures Market Explained

How the forward curve works for business energy procurement

The futures market shows the price for energy delivered in future periods. A customer renewing from 2027, 2028, 2029 or 2030 is not only interested in today's price. They should look at what the market is currently offering for their actual delivery period.

Cheaper further out does not mean cheaper later.

The future market is only useful while the customer can still buy that future delivery period. Waiting lets the market make the decision for the customer. As time passes, that future period becomes the near-term market and can absorb the same risk premium.

The 1 Year Ago line is context. The latest line is the customer's decision point. Backwardation does not mean wait.

The % vs W-26 column shows the current forward-value gap.

A negative percentage does not mean the market will be cheaper later. It means that future delivery period is currently cheaper than the front of the curve. The customer mistake is thinking a cheaper future period means they should wait. It does not. It means we should check whether that future period can be secured now.

Supplier Price-Book Lag

The opportunity and the danger

Supplier price-book lag is the opportunity and the danger.

Wholesale markets move first. Supplier books often follow. Waiting for certainty can mean missing the price movement. Once a supplier withdraws a rate, the customer cannot go back and buy yesterday's price.

Suppliers do not need a full closure of Hormuz before repricing. Suppliers do not need Qatar to cancel every LNG cargo before pricing LNG risk. Suppliers do not need Britain to lose all French imports before pricing tighter European electricity. Suppliers can price probability before physical supply fails.

Today's market has several separate repricing triggers: Hormuz route risk, Qatar LNG uncertainty, French nuclear restrictions, high gas-for-power demand and Saudi shipping disruption.
