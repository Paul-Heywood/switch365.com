Futures Market Explained

The futures market shows the price for energy delivered in future periods. A customer renewing from 2027, 2028, 2029 or 2030 is not only interested in today's price. They should look at what the market is currently offering for their actual delivery period.

Cheaper further out does not mean cheaper later. The future market is only useful while the customer can still buy that future delivery period. Waiting lets the market make the decision for the customer. As time passes, that future period becomes the near-term market and can absorb the same risk premium.

The 1 Year Ago line is context. The latest line is the customer's decision point. Backwardation does not mean wait.

The % vs W-26 column shows how much cheaper or more expensive each future delivery period is compared with the latest W-26 price. A negative percentage does not mean the market will be cheaper later. It means that future delivery period is currently cheaper than the front of the curve. The customer mistake is thinking a cheaper future period means they should wait. It does not. It means we should check whether that future period can be secured now.

Top Five Warning Signs

1

Hormuz Visible Traffic Virtually Stopped

Five Saturday, zero Kpler-registered Sunday versus 31 previous weekend. The AIS caveat matters, but the collapse in visible shipping traffic is still an extreme route-confidence signal. A route can remain physically open and still carry an extreme risk premium.

2

Repeated Attacks on ADNOC Vessels

Third vessel incident on Friday. Repeated attacks change operator behaviour, crew risk, insurance pricing and willingness to transit. Repeated attacks can restrict a route without anybody physically sealing it.

3

US-Iran Talks Remain Stalled

Iran has not decided to resume negotiations. No decision to resume negotiations is not a peace breakthrough. The absence of progress matters more when physical shipping is deteriorating at the same time.

4

Qatar LNG Uncertainty Extends into September

Physical gas-supply duration risk. 56 affected cargoes under force majeure. When Asian buyers replace missing Gulf cargoes, they compete with Europe for the same alternative molecules. Replacement cost matters.

5

Bab el-Mandeb Is Not a Clean Alternative

49 weekend transits but no tracked Saudi oil shipments. The Red Sea can reduce dependence on Hormuz, but it does not remove route risk. Friction gets priced.

Lower oil for one session does not remove the warning signs. Visible Hormuz traffic deteriorated materially over the weekend. Qatar LNG uncertainty converts route risk into physical gas-supply risk. Bab el-Mandeb being technically open does not make it a risk-free alternative.

Supplier Price-Book Lag

Supplier price-book lag is the opportunity and the danger. Wholesale markets move first. Supplier books often follow.

Waiting for certainty can mean missing the price movement. Once a supplier withdraws a rate, the customer cannot go back and buy yesterday's price.

Suppliers do not need Hormuz to be physically sealed before repricing. Suppliers can price a collapse in shipping confidence. Suppliers can price repeated tanker attacks. Suppliers can price LNG replacement risk. Suppliers can price the probability of disruption before every physical consequence appears.

Five Saturday and zero registered Sunday is the kind of physical evidence suppliers can react to quickly.

Lower oil for one session does not mean supplier gas and power books have become safer. Wholesale moved first. Supplier books may still follow.
