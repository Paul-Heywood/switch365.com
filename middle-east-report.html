01

Summary

Status: Duration risk rising

The central energy-market issue is increasingly duration. After almost six months of conflict, meaningful direct US-Iran negotiations remain absent, the naval blockade continues and Washington is opening a major new economic phase today. Iran has responded by warning that continued economic warfare could lead it to seek a halt to oil exports across the Persian Gulf, while UKMTO says visible Hormuz traffic remains approximately 90% below pre-conflict levels. Only four tracked commodity vessels crossed on Sunday. Commercial operators continue to use workarounds, and refined-fuel markets are now showing deeper physical distortions from the prolonged disruption. Pakistan and Oman are actively pursuing diplomacy, so a settlement remains possible. But the assumption that businesses can simply wait a few weeks for everything to return to normal is becoming harder to justify.

Monday's internal offered sheet opened the week mixed rather than higher: W-26 gas and power both edged 0.25 lower versus Friday morning, W-28 power fell 3.32, and the longest-dated gas periods moved below their own 1 Year Ago equivalents. Softer offered prices ahead of the sanctions detail are not de-escalation; they are the market waiting for policy detail.

Customer translation. For customers ending in 2026, the remaining time available to wait is shrinking while duration risk is rising. For customers ending later, check whether today's cheaper future delivery periods can be secured before prolonged risk is absorbed further into supplier pricing.

Quick read

  • Conflict approaching six months without a permanent settlement; the June negotiating framework has expired with no successor.
  • US "economic D-Day" sanctions package due to be detailed by Treasury Secretary Bessent today (press conference expected 19:00 London time).
  • Iran's SNSC secretary Mohsen Rezaei threatens broader Gulf oil-export disruption if the economic war continues: a conditional threat, not an implemented shutdown.
  • Hormuz visible traffic approximately 90% below the pre-conflict AIS baseline (UKMTO, week ending 21 August).
  • Only four tracked commodity vessels crossed Hormuz on Sunday (Kpler initial data), down from 16 on Friday.
  • LPG carrier movements do not equal Qatar LNG normalisation; Qatar's force majeure on some deliveries runs to end-September.
  • Internal offered rates mixed on Monday morning: W-26 gas and power both -0.25, W-28 power -3.32, long-dated gas below 1 Year Ago.
  • Iran-linked hackers reportedly disrupted a small UK peaker plant; Pakistan's army chief is in Tehran today and Oman's foreign minister follows tomorrow.
The important change is duration. The evidence for a quick resolution is getting weaker. This does not prove the conflict will last for years. It does mean businesses should be increasingly cautious about basing procurement decisions on the assumption that everything returns to normal within a few weeks.

Futures Market Explained

Why forward prices are actionable now

The futures market shows today's price for future delivery. Customers do not need to wait until 2028 to see today's 2028 market. If that future period can be secured now, the price is actionable now.

A prolonged geopolitical problem can move a future delivery price long before the customer's current contract ends. Monday's sheet proves the point in reverse: W-28 power moved 3.32 in a single snapshot, and long-dated gas swung below its year-ago level. The forward market moves whether or not the customer is watching it.

Suppliers do not need to know the exact date the war ends before they price the probability that disruption lasts longer.

Cheaper further out does not mean cheaper later.
Backwardation does not mean wait. A downward-sloping curve describes today's relative prices, not a promise about tomorrow's.
The future market is only useful while the customer can still buy it. Withdrawn rates are not retrospective.

Procurement Window

Review-now window

The internal curve, public benchmarks and the geopolitical evidence now point the same way: duration risk is being priced, and the front of the market is carrying it first. Monday's mixed sheet does not change that picture; it shows the market waiting for today's sanctions detail rather than relaxing.

Evidence stack

  • Internal curve: W-26 gas -0.25 and power -0.25 versus Friday morning; W-28 power -3.32; long-dated gas now below 1 Year Ago.
  • NBP / TTF: both near five-month highs; TTF up roughly 6% on the week with EU storage near 62% versus 74% a year ago.
  • Oil: Brent around $93 after two consecutive weekly gains; softer this morning ahead of sanctions detail, which is profit-taking, not de-escalation.
  • Sanctions: Washington is due to announce the "economic D-Day" package today; measures not yet published at time of writing (press conference expected 19:00 London).
  • Iran threat: a conditional threat to seek a halt to Gulf oil exports if the economic war continues; broader than Iranian exports alone, not yet implemented. Tehran calls the sanctions "extraterritorial" and unlawful.
  • Hormuz: 16 / 13 / 4 tracked commodity transits Friday to Sunday; roughly 90% below pre-conflict AIS baseline; selective Iraqi tanker permissions are controlled access, not normalisation.
  • LNG: Qatar force majeure on some deliveries extended to end-September; LPG carrier movements do not signal LNG normalisation.
  • Bearish counterweights: Bab el-Mandeb functioning (24 vessels Sunday), Pakistan in Tehran today, Oman tomorrow, Saudi workarounds, alternative LNG supply, softer Monday oil and a mixed internal sheet.

Commercial position

Review now does not mean sign blindly. It means establish the customer's live position before deciding whether continuing to wait is commercially justified.
2026 urgency. For businesses ending this year, the commercial decision should increasingly be based on today's available price versus the risk of continuing to wait, not on hope that the war will soon disappear.
Later CEDs. Longer-duration geopolitical risk matters because today's 2027, 2028, 2029 or 2030 forward price can change long before the customer's existing contract ends, as W-28 power's 3.32 single-day move illustrates.

Norway remains a genuine bearish counterweight on supply, though mid-August saw unscheduled maintenance at Kollsnes and the autumn maintenance season approaches. Strong Norwegian flows help; they do not remove the duration premium embedded in winter delivery.

British Gas 18-Month Window

Internal Switch365 evidence

British Gas has extended its business-energy renewal window from 12 months to 18 months. The 18-month window turns future-market intelligence into practical customer action: a customer whose contract ends within the next 18 months may be able to review and secure forward pricing today rather than waiting for the traditional renewal window.

How it works

Your new contract does not start today. It starts when the existing agreement ends. Securing early locks the rate, not the start date.

Why it matters now

British Gas 18-month eligibility turns this market intelligence into a practical customer action: the forward-value gap visible in today's curve can be tested against live pricing immediately.

The risk

Waiting is not neutral. If today's rate disappears, the customer cannot return later and buy it retrospectively. Cheaper further out does not mean cheaper later.

Not every customer is automatically eligible; eligibility and live pricing should be checked against the actual contract end date. The action is to review, not to sign blindly.

Shipping workarounds

Saudi East-West pipeline to Yanbu, SUMED/Sidi Kerir, Cape of Good Hope, Fujairah/Oman ship-to-ship transfers and replacement crude and LNG buying keep energy moving at additional time, cost and complexity. Some workarounds can add around a month at sea; longer voyages tie ships up for longer, reducing effective tanker availability. Friction gets priced.

22 / 32 / 24 commodity vessels Friday to Sunday, including two VLCCs entering the Red Sea on Saturday, one with Iraqi Basrah crude. The Red Sea route is functioning better than Hormuz, but it is not a clean, risk-free substitute.

Ranked

The quick-resolution case continues to weaken

Almost six months of conflict and no permanent settlement. The previous US-Iran negotiating framework expired without a successor and meaningful direct talks have not occurred since June.

2

Economic war enters a new phase

3

Iran threatens the wider Gulf export system

The Rezaei statement targets all Gulf oil exports, not merely Iranian oil, and frames third-country participation as an act of war. Conditional, unimplemented, but materially broader tail risk.

4

Hormuz visible traffic remains around 90% below normal

Four tracked commodity vessels on Sunday, fewer than twenty across the whole weekend. UKMTO counts 23 projectile-strike incidents since 6 July. A chokepoint does not need to be sealed by a physical barrier to become commercially impaired.

5

Physical distortion is spreading through the energy system

Refined-fuel shortages, longer voyage times, higher freight and insurance, selective tanker access and now a suspected Iran-linked cyber incident at a UK generator: the disruption is propagating beyond headline crude flows.

Strait of Hormuz

Hormuz is not open as usual. Tracked commodity transits fell from 16 on Friday to 13 on Saturday and just 4 on Sunday, fewer than twenty across the whole weekend. UKMTO's weekly report counts 89 vessels exiting and 103 entering across the seven days to 21 August, with tankers 45% of traffic (56% of those crude, products or chemicals; LPG carriers a further 24%), and AIS-detected traffic approximately 90% below pre-conflict baselines, declining since the late-June peak. UKMTO counts 23 projectile-strike incidents since 6 July causing bridge, engine-room and structural damage. Kpler data indicates around 86% of crude-laden tankers transiting since 7 July sailed dark. Repeated attacks change shipowner, crew and insurer behaviour even when a waterway remains physically navigable.

  • Selective Iraqi access: Iran permitted a number of Iraqi tankers to transit after Baghdad's requests (IRNA, Saturday), secured during speaker Ghalibaf's Iraq visit. Selective vessel access is not normal trade; it strengthens Hormuz's value as political and economic leverage.
  • LPG is not LNG: eight very large gas carriers transited over three days through Sunday; six entered empty and two exited carrying Iranian LPG (propane/butane), not LNG. LPG movement does not prove Qatar LNG has normalised. This distinction is commercially critical for a UK gas report.
  • Weekend detail: Friday's 16 included two empty VLCCs entering with transponders switched off, one heading to Iraq and one to Bahrain; a VLCC carrying 2 million barrels of Emirati crude exited on Thursday.
  • AIS caveat: some vessels switch off transponders, so tracked counts are not a perfect census; equally, 90% below AIS baseline does not mean physical volumes are exactly 90% lower.

Bab el-Mandeb / Red Sea

Bab el-Mandeb remains operational: 22 commodity vessels Friday, 32 Saturday, 24 Sunday, including two VLCCs entering the Red Sea on Saturday, one carrying Iraqi Basrah crude. The Red Sea route is functioning better than Hormuz, but it is not a clean, risk-free substitute: Houthi-aligned attacks have continued there, and insurance remains elevated.

Workarounds

Pipelines

Saudi East-West to Yanbu avoids Hormuz but still faces Red Sea risk; Sidi Kerir / SUMED moves oil via Egypt to the Mediterranean.

Long routes

Cape of Good Hope avoids Bab el-Mandeb entirely. Some normal Saudi-to-Asia journeys of around 20 days become 50-60, an extra 30-40 days; Reuters has estimated some detours add around $2.5m per voyage, with Oman-China VLCC freight previously reported near $140,000/day.

Replacement supply

Crude from the US, Brazil, Africa and Iraq; LNG from the US, Oman, Nigeria and Angola. Fujairah and Oman ship-to-ship transfers let large tankers receive cargo outside the Gulf.

Workarounds do not mean the problem is solved. They mean the market has found ways to move some energy at additional time, cost and complexity. Workarounds reduce the risk of complete shortage while increasing time, freight and complexity. Friction gets priced.

Supplier Price-Book Lag

The opportunity and the danger

Supplier price-book lag is the opportunity and the danger. Wholesale markets move first. Supplier books often follow. Waiting for certainty can mean missing the price movement.

Suppliers do not need the conflict to last another year before they price duration risk. They do not need Iran to successfully stop every Gulf oil shipment. They do not need Hormuz to be literally sealed. They do not need every Qatar LNG cargo to disappear.

They price probability. They price replacement cost. They price freight. They price liquidity. They price volatility. They price duration.

The market can reprice the risk before the worst-case event occurs.

Once a supplier withdraws a rate, the customer cannot go back and secure yesterday's pricing. This week illustrates both directions: W-26 gas and power each moved +5.00 between Thursday and Friday, then -0.25 into Monday, while W-28 power fell 3.32 in a single snapshot. Books move quickly, in both directions.
Today's evidence is exactly the kind suppliers price: an expiring negotiating framework, a new sanctions phase, a conditional Gulf-wide export threat, 90%-below-baseline shipping and refined-fuel distortions spreading through the physical system.
