Futures Market Explained

How forward pricing works for business energy procurement

The futures market shows the price for energy delivered in future periods. A customer renewing from 2027, 2028, 2029 or 2030 is not only interested in today's price. They should look at what the market is currently offering for their actual delivery period.

Cheaper further out does not mean cheaper later. The future market is only useful while the customer can still buy that future delivery period. Waiting lets the market make the decision for the customer. As time passes, that future period becomes the near-term market and can absorb the same risk premium.

The 1 Year Ago line is context. The latest line is the customer's decision point. If today's future periods are still materially below W-26 but higher than 1 Year Ago, the message is: the forward-value opportunity still exists, but the market has already moved.

Backwardation does not mean wait. It means check whether the future period can be secured now, before the market moves again.

Supplier Price-Book Lag

The opportunity and the danger

Supplier price-book lag is the opportunity and the danger.

Wholesale markets move first. Supplier books often follow. Waiting for certainty can mean missing the price movement. Once a supplier withdraws a rate, the customer cannot go back and buy yesterday's price.

Even if oil has fallen from the highs, supplier books may still react to the move that has already happened. Suppliers do not need a full closure of Hormuz or Bab el-Mandeb before they reprice.

Suppliers also do not need diplomacy to fail completely before they price renewed conflict risk. When conflict spreads geographically, suppliers can price the increased probability of disruption before physical closure happens.

1 Year Ago prices are useful context, but they do not protect the customer today.
