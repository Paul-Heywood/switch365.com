Daily Brief — What the Sales Team Needs to Know

Kataib Hezbollah's 7-day deadline is today's biggest fresh energy-risk trigger. The Iran-aligned Iraqi group has given Baghdad until 6 August to prove it can defend Iraq's sovereignty, after joint US-Saudi strikes on 29 July killed at least 20 PMF members and four IRGC advisers. The group says a response is "inevitable" and has warned it could target US "tools" in Saudi Arabia.

The market may be getting short-term relief from some vessel movement — 37 commodity ships transited Bab el-Mandeb on Tuesday, the highest in over a week — but the risk map has widened. Reuters reports that Yemen's Houthis attacked Saudi Arabia this week from Iraqi territory in coordination with Iraqi armed groups, with strikes on oil facilities in the kingdom's Eastern Province. That turns Iraq into a potential launch platform against Saudi energy infrastructure, while Hormuz, the Red Sea, Bab el-Mandeb and Egypt / Suez remain live route risks.

Markets do not like countdowns, deadlines or revenge language. Things are spreading out geographically, and that is a bad sign for energy risk. Brent has swung from $100.69 (23 Jul) down to $84.09 (28 Jul) and back to ~$89 — relief pricing on flow data, followed by repricing on escalation. UK gas remains sharply elevated: NBP is up ~40% month-on-month and ~64% year-on-year.

Customers should not read lower oil as lower business energy automatically. The energy bill conversation is about NBP, TTF, UK power, supplier books, contract end date and forward delivery period. The supplied forward curve still shows deep backwardation — S-29 gas is -56.8% vs W-26 and S-29 power is -47.6% vs W-26 — and the far end of the curve (S-29, W-29, S-30 gas; S-28, W-28, S-29 power) is still priced below last year, even though the front and mid-curve have moved well above their 1 Year Ago levels. The forward-value opportunity still exists — and on the far curve it is now cheaper than it was a year ago.

Futures Market Explained

Why the delivery period matters more than today's headline price

The futures market shows the price for energy delivered in future periods. A customer renewing from 2027, 2028, 2029 or 2030 is not only interested in today's price. They should look at what the market is currently offering for their actual delivery period.

  • Cheaper further out does not mean cheaper later. It means that period is priced as future delivery today — and that pricing can be secured while it is still available.
  • The future market is only useful while the customer can still buy that future delivery period. Access depends on contract end date and the supplier's renewal window.
  • Waiting lets the market make the decision for the customer. As time passes, that future period becomes the near-term market and can absorb the same risk premium.
  • The 1 Year Ago line is context. The latest line is the customer's decision point. A year ago W-26 gas was ~91 p/th; today it is 142.50 — waiting through escalation is what that move looks like in practice.
Backwardation does not mean wait. It means the cheaper part of the curve may be available now — and waiting gives the market more time to take it away.

Ceasefire Reality Check

Diplomacy discussed is not de-escalation delivered

The fragile US-Iran ceasefire collapsed earlier this month, and both sides have been carrying out daily strikes. The brief pause around 25 July was shattered within days by the Jordan intercept, the US-Saudi strikes in Iraq and the Damietta drone attack.

  • The pause has cracked, but that does not mean diplomacy is dead. It means the market can no longer price the pause as clean de-escalation.
  • Fresh countdown risk in Iraq means the market has to price renewed uncertainty, even if talks continue in the background.
  • Talks being alive is better than collapse, but no breakthrough is not implementation. Oman, Pakistan and Qatar channels are active; Iran rejected the 50-50 Hormuz lane plan and tabled its own proposal instead.
  • Oil can move on mediation headlines, but shipping confidence is what matters for supplier pricing. Route normalisation matters more than political optimism.
  • Do not confuse diplomacy being discussed with de-escalation being delivered. Trump's "good talks" claim and Araghchi's "breach of commitments" accusation were made in the same week.

Shipping Risk

Chokepoint-by-chokepoint status as at 31 July 2026

Strait of Hormuz

Hormuz normally carries around one fifth of global crude oil and LNG shipments. On Tuesday only five commodity ships transited (three in, two out; one was the empty VLCC Nissos Kea). The IRGC claims its naval forces struck three oil tankers that "ignored warnings" — treat as a claim pending independent confirmation. Iran has rejected Oman's 50-50 lane-management plan and proposed routing one lane entirely through its own territorial waters. Meanwhile the US "steel wall blockade" had redirected 18 commercial vessels, disabled two and boarded two as of 28 July, and the US and UK are planning a Hormuz protection coalition.

Hormuz may be moving, but moving is not the same as normal. One LNG tanker moving does not mean LNG movement has normalised.

Bab el-Mandeb / Red Sea

Thirty-seven commodity ships transited on Tuesday — the highest since 19 July — but none were VLCCs or LNG tankers, and Monday saw just 11, the lowest in months. The Houthis have declared a naval blockade on Saudi-linked shipping, claimed ballistic-missile fire at a Saudi oil tanker, and struck two Saudi tankers on 23 July; the Saudi vessel NCC Masa took minor hull damage. Saudi Arabia's defence ministry met nearly 50 countries in Riyadh; 14 — including Turkey, Egypt, Pakistan and Nigeria — affirmed support for a maritime defence coalition covering Bab el-Mandeb, the Red Sea and the Gulf of Aden.

Bab el-Mandeb is not fully closed, but it is still a route-risk market. The Red Sea route is open, but it is not clean.

Egypt / Suez / SUMED / Yanbu

A drone struck the US-owned FSRU Energos Winter and the Greek-owned LNG carrier Gaslog Salem at Damietta on 29 July during cargo discharge; fires were brought under control, crews are safe, and both vessels were moved outside the port. Operations at unaffected berths were expected to resume overnight. No party has claimed responsibility and Egyptian authorities have not determined attribution — Egypt / Damietta attribution must be treated carefully unless a responsible actor is confirmed. Analysts note this is the first strike affecting Egypt's Mediterranean LNG infrastructure since the conflict intensified, and canal-area strikes could raise war-risk premiums for Suez transits.

Suez can help, but it is not a magic fix. Fully laden VLCCs are not a simple Suez solution. The workaround adds friction, and friction gets priced.

Kazakhstan / CPC / Black Sea

Kazakhstan resumed CPC loadings at Novorossiysk on 27 July after a suspension triggered by drone attacks on civilian vessels in the Black Sea. This is now a secondary supply-route issue — resumed, but explicitly conditional on the security situation.

War-Risk Insurance

With declared blockades, tanker strikes, a coalition in formation and an unclaimed drone attack on LNG infrastructure, war-risk premiums and freight costs remain elevated across the Gulf, Red Sea and now the Eastern Mediterranean. Insurance reprices faster than physical supply returns.

Supplier Price-Book Lag

The opportunity and the danger — in one mechanism

Supplier price-book lag is the opportunity and the danger. Wholesale markets move first. Supplier books often follow.

  • Waiting for certainty can mean missing the price movement. Once a supplier withdraws a rate, the customer cannot go back and buy yesterday's price.
  • Even if oil has fallen from the highs, supplier books may still react to the move that has already happened. Wholesale moved first. Supplier books may still follow.
  • Suppliers do not need a full closure of Hormuz or Bab el-Mandeb before they reprice. They also do not need diplomacy to fail completely before they price renewed conflict risk.
  • When conflict spreads geographically, suppliers can price the increased probability of disruption before physical closure happens. An Iraq countdown, a Saudi targeting assessment and an unclaimed strike at Damietta are exactly that kind of input.
  • 1 Year Ago prices are useful context, but they do not protect the customer today. The latest line is the decision point.
