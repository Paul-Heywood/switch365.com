Futures Market Explained

What these prices actually are

NBP versus TTF. NBP (National Balancing Point) is the UK wholesale gas benchmark; TTF (Title Transfer Facility) is the Dutch benchmark that sets the tone for continental Europe. They usually move together because the UK is connected to Europe by pipelines and both compete for the same LNG cargoes, but they are different instruments and do not move identically. UK power is heavily influenced by gas because gas-fired plant often sets the marginal price of electricity.

Spot versus forward. Day-ahead prices are for gas or power delivered tomorrow. Front-month contracts (currently December 2026) are the nearest tradable month. Seasonal contracts such as Winter 2026 (W-26) or Summer 2028 (S-28) cover six-month delivery blocks. A business renewing today for a 2028 start is buying those later seasons, not today's spot price.

Settlements versus intraday quotes. An exchange settlement is an official end-of-day price. Monday's reported TTF high of €96.08/MWh and the later ~€92.50 are intraday observations during a live session, not a Monday closing settlement. The same discipline applies to NBP: any public quote must name the instrument, the date and the price type.

Offered-season indicators versus executable rates. The Switch365 screenshot shows supplier offered-season indicators: evidence of the direction of supplier pricing, not a guaranteed tariff. An executable rate exists only as a named, valid, customer-specific quotation with full costs and an expiry.

Why 2028 differs from today. Monday's curve is heavily backwardated: gas for Summer 2028 delivery is offered at 90p/therm while the W-26 working reference is 227.030p. Winter 2026 carries an immediate risk premium from low storage, cold forecasts and shipping risk; later seasons price a more normal balance. Cheaper further out does not mean cheaper later: a 2028 delivery price says nothing about what the market will do before 2028 arrives.

Gas up, oil down. The same day can produce a sharp gas rally while Brent falls, because gas responds to wind, weather, storage, Norwegian flows and LNG availability, while oil is currently pricing ample crude supply and surplus expectations. Brent is not the customer's gas quote.

Supplier lag. Wholesale screens move first; supplier rate books often follow with a delay. That lag is where both the opportunity and the risk sit for a business customer this week.

Procurement Window

Every supplied gas and power season rose from Friday to Monday, and the W-26 working references are up about 10% since Thursday. Repricing may reach supplier books at different times; only rate-locked, valid future-start quotations establish what a customer can secure. This is a review-now window, not an instruction to sign everyone immediately, and not a manufactured deadline.

Price-up risks

  • Further UKMTO- or operator-confirmed loaded LNG incidents, rising war-risk premiums, unsafe repeated Hormuz crossings or actual lost Qatari export cargoes.
  • Verifiable Saudi refinery or pipeline throughput reductions, or additional output lost at major Gulf infrastructure (a Houthi claim alone is not this).
  • EU storage at 69.17% and Germany at 56.63% (21 November gas day) combined with colder weather, low-wind gas-for-power demand and stronger Asian cargo competition.
  • Gassco-reported Norwegian outages or lower measured exports; actual supplier price-book withdrawals or higher named valid rates.

Price-down counterweights

  • A milder forecast, stronger wind generation and reduced gas burn; stabilising storage withdrawals.
  • Norwegian field recovery, additional US LNG cargoes delivered, more regular safe Qatar LNG loadings and insured transits.
  • Diplomatic de-escalation, improved maritime security and a larger global oil surplus. Brent can fall without gas matching it.
  • Genuine supplier quotations still competitively priced, with costs and eligibility conditions checked.

Customer action

Confirm contract end date (CED), supply start or renewal window, supplier and product, annual kWh, meter details (MPAN/MPRN), usage profile, credit position, unit rate, standing charge, pass-through charges, broker commission, tariff inclusions and exclusions, cancellation terms, price validity and full lifetime expected bill. Check British Gas 18-month eligibility where appropriate. Present balanced, informed options with no artificial urgency.

British Gas 18-Month Window

Extended renewal window Switch365 internal commercial statement

British Gas has extended its business energy renewal window from 12 months to 18 months. Eligible customers can review future-start pricing earlier than under the old 12-month window, which matters in a week when every supplied season has just repriced higher.

A future-start contract begins when the present supply agreement ends, not the day the quote is signed, and remains subject to supplier product, credit and eligibility rules. This is a commercial window, not proof that British Gas has the cheapest price or that any particular rate is still open. British Gas 18-month eligibility turns this market intelligence into a practical customer action: check eligibility, obtain the live quote, and compare it against genuinely available alternatives before the supplier book catches up with the wholesale rebound.

Market Drivers

Ranked for Monday 23 November 2026. Each driver ends with its transmission mechanism into NBP/TTF, supplier pricing and real customer choices, and both directions are shown.

Driver 1 Bullish

Broad supplier-season rebound across the whole curve

All eight gas seasons and all six power seasons in the user's Friday 20 AM to Monday 23 AM comparison rose, led by S-27 gas (+8.00p) and S-27 power (+£5.30). The direct Monday W-26 gas reference of 227.030p is +10.35% on Thursday's 205.730p, with modelled power +9.32% to £175.72. Confidence: high (internal evidence).

Transmission: offered-season indicators are the closest evidence to what supplier books may do next. A rally reverses if wholesale settles back this week; it extends if today's intraday strength holds into settlements.

Driver 2 Bullish

Cold forecasts and weak wind across north-west Europe

Monday reporting attributes much of the TTF rally to forecasts of temperatures 3°C to 5°C below the 30-year average this week, and 5°C to 8°C below normal into early December. Britain has already seen the pattern: NESO issued an electricity margin notice on 9 November during a low-wind lull, calling up around 1.4 GW of extra capacity before cancelling by mid-morning. Forecasts are expectations, not observed demand; actual NESO/Elexon output confirms only after the fact.

Transmission: cold lifts heating gas demand directly; weak wind lifts gas-for-power burn. Both feed NBP and TTF prompt prices, then supplier winter pricing. A mild, windy turn would unwind this driver quickly.

Driver 3 Bullish

Storage buffer at record lows for the date

GIE/AGSI+ gas day 21 November (provisional): EU 69.17% full (about 783 TWh), Germany 56.63%. The EU has run below its 2011-2025 range for the date every day since 1 August, and net withdrawals are under way. Low storage is a winter vulnerability, not proof the UK will physically run out of gas.

Transmission: a thin buffer means each cold day or supply scare moves prices further. Storage is a buffer, not immunity. Stabilising withdrawals or strong LNG arrivals would soften this driver.

Driver 4 Mixed

Two UKMTO attack warnings near Hormuz on Saturday

Warnings 215-26 (05:00 UTC, about 11 nm north of Khasab) and 216-26 (05:50 UTC, about 17 nm east) were issued on Saturday 21 November. Regional reporting describes a tanker hit by an unknown projectile and a second tanker struck in its engine room; Iran separately claimed a vessel hit a mine east of Hormuz. Vessel identity, cargo, damage extent and attribution remain under verification. A projectile strike is not automatically a removed LNG cargo or liquefaction damage.

Transmission: war-risk premiums and rerouting raise delivered LNG cost even where cargoes still move. If no LNG carrier or Qatari loading is actually affected, the risk premium can fade within days.

Driver 5 Mixed

Houthi claim on Rabigh refinery, coalition interception reported

On Sunday 22 November the Houthis claimed a direct hit on the Rabigh refining and petrochemical complex on Saudi Arabia's Red Sea coast; the Saudi-led coalition reported intercepting a drone on Sunday, and Saudi authorities confirmed no refinery damage in sources checked. Rabigh is oil, refining and petrochemicals; it is not an NBP gas supply source.

Transmission: direct gas impact is minimal unless verified damage removes refining or export capacity. Confirmed output loss would lift the regional risk premium across energy, including LNG shipping insurance.

Driver 6 Mixed

Hormuz oil flows still below October, but oil is not gas

Reuters' Monday Kpler context put Hormuz oil flow at around 9.5 million bpd across the ten days to 19 November, against a 12.2 million bpd October average on matched scope. This is an oil traffic measurement, not LNG loadings and not all shipping; older -18% and -32% figures used different baselines and are not reconciled.

Transmission: reduced oil flow mainly supports crude and freight. For UK gas, the parallel question is Qatari LNG loadings and safe passage, which recovered only partially through November.

Driver 7 Bullish

Monday TTF intraday spike, session still live

Dutch month-ahead TTF was reported up as much as 8.6% to about €96.08/MWh before easing towards €92.50 during Monday's session. No Monday closing settlement exists at report time. UK NBP must be checked by named instrument, date and price type; the internal 227.030p W-26 reference is never a substitute for an ICE NBP settlement.

Transmission: if the rally holds into settlement, UK gas forwards typically follow within sessions; if it fades, supplier books may never fully reprice. Intraday spikes are signals, not settled fact.

Driver 8 Mixed

Norway constraints, US LNG buffer, partial Qatar recovery

Monday reporting cited Norwegian maintenance and field outages disrupting pipeline flows to Europe. Qatar's Ras Laffan loadings ran at about 13 to 15 shipments per ten-day rolling average in November, up from roughly 11 in October but only around half of pre-war levels. US LNG remains the major bearish buffer, but long-term contracts starting 2030-31 deliver nothing now.

Transmission: strong Norway materially cushions missing LNG; renewed outages do the opposite. US LNG cannot automatically replace all lost Qatari volumes, so net supply decides direction.

Driver 9 Bearish for oil

Brent falling on surplus expectations

Brent traded near $92/bbl on Monday (Reuters: $91.97 at 08:44 GMT, -0.65%), a fourth weekly decline, as the IEA's roughly 2 million bpd Q4 surplus forecast and ample exports outweighed geopolitical risk. Goldman Sachs still models Brent at $140 in 2027 if Gulf output stays 3.5 million bpd below its 2025 average. Oil and gas need not move together.

Transmission: falling Brent does not lower a customer's gas quote. Gas-specific balances (storage, weather, LNG, Norway) set NBP and TTF.

Driver 10 Customer-specific

Executable supplier books decide the real outcome

A seasonal indicator is not a supplier tariff. What matters is a named supplier's valid quote: credit, meter, CED and start date, unit rate, standing charge, pass-throughs, commission, term and expiry. Some books may still carry pre-rebound rates; others may already have moved.

Transmission: supplier price-book lag is the opportunity and the danger. Once a supplier withdraws a rate, the customer cannot go back and secure yesterday's price.

Top Five Warning Signs

1. A new, verified Gulf supply or export loss

Watch for confirmed disruption to Qatari LNG loadings, Saudi East-West or Red Sea crude loading, or Rabigh throughput, where confirmed means physical damage plus output reduction verified by the operator or independent tracking, not a Houthi claim. Invalidated by: operator statements and ship-tracking showing normal loadings and departures.

2. Shipping security and war-risk insurance deteriorating

Further UKMTO-confirmed attacks after Saturday's two warnings, a breakdown of repeatable safe LNG passage, named vessels or cargoes hit, and rising war-risk premiums. Invalidated by: several weeks of incident-free, insured LNG transits and falling premiums.

3. Cold, low wind and faster storage draw in Europe

Track the latest GIE gas day (currently 21 November: EU 69.17%, Germany 56.63%), realised UK/EU demand, and actual NESO wind and gas generation readings rather than untested forecasts. Invalidated by: milder weather, stronger wind and stabilising storage.

4. Norwegian outages deepening or extending

Gassco UMM notices and realised pipeline export volumes are the evidence; Monday reporting already cited maintenance and field outages. Invalidated by: fields returning on schedule and flows normalising.

5. Supplier price-book surge or withdrawal

A named supplier repricing or pulling rates, with verified timestamp, exact start terms and an executable customer rate, matters more than any wholesale screen. Invalidated by: books holding pre-rebound prices with genuine validity, or fresh competitive quotes.

Ceasefire Reality Check

The G7 Paris declaration of 11 November and subsequent diplomatic contacts are policy signals, not a ceasefire and not a guarantee of safe LNG shipping. The test is physical: an operative signed agreement, demonstrably reduced UKMTO incident risk, safe insured regular tanker and LNG transits, and measurable shipped tonnes. October figures showed partial recovery (31 laden LNG vessel exits against different loadings measures), not normalisation. November's two Saturday warnings show incident risk persists.

Open questions, tested against evidence

  • Did the reported 3 November missile and drone wave cause any verified energy damage, and did Ras Tanura see a confirmed refinery or export consequence? Independent site confirmations remain absent.
  • Are Saudi Eastern Province fields, Ras Tanura, Abqaiq, the East-West pipeline, Yanbu and Rabigh operating with output and export evidence? Rabigh's status after Sunday's claim is unverified either way.
  • Has Egypt's 26 October authorisation become a requested, deployed, operational mission? No verified deployment at last check.
  • Have the late-October Iran talks and the conditional Hormuz proposals become a signed, enforceable arrangement with safe maritime transit? No.
  • Do UKMTO and operators confirm repeatable safe LNG carrier passage through Hormuz, the Gulf of Aden and Bab al-Mandeb? Not yet; Saturday produced two new warnings.
  • Have Qatari loadings risen sustainably? Partially: about 13 to 15 per ten-day rolling average in November versus roughly 11 in October, still around half of pre-war levels, with US spot cargoes continuing to compensate.
  • Is falling Brent matched by falling TTF/NBP and softer supplier books? No: gas rose sharply on Monday while oil fell.

Mandatory distinctions: diplomatic contact is not a ceasefire, and a ceasefire announcement is not verified safe passage. An intercepted missile is not a confirmed energy infrastructure hit, and a hit is not necessarily lost production. An improvement in security can reduce a risk premium before damaged infrastructure is repaired, but physical flows are the supply test. Price direction is never assumed from the geopolitical narrative alone.

Shipping Risk

Hormuz

Saturday 21 November: UKMTO warnings 215-26 (about 11 nm north of Khasab, 05:00 UTC) and 216-26 (about 17 nm east, 05:50 UTC). Regional reporting describes one tanker hit by an unknown projectile and a second struck in the engine room; earlier November incidents included a tanker near Bukha struck by a projectile with a fire later extinguished and crew safe. Vessel names, IMO numbers, cargo types and outcomes are published only where verified. Reuters/Kpler measured about 9.5m bpd of Hormuz oil flow across the ten days to 19 November against a 12.2m bpd October average on matched scope; older -18% and -32% decline figures used different baselines and remain unreconciled, so neither is published as current.

Gulf of Oman

Approach waters share the Hormuz risk premium. Traffic slowed at points in November as owners reassessed; some vessels ran dark on AIS, complicating verification. No new Gulf of Oman-specific incident was confirmed this weekend beyond the Khasab-area warnings.

Persian Gulf

Iran's IRGC earlier claimed four vessels caught fire in the northern Gulf and three struck mines west of Hormuz. Treated as an Iranian claim; cross-checking against UKMTO, operators, flag states and tracking continues vessel by vessel.

Gulf of Aden

Shabwa province, attacked earlier in the conflict, contains Yemen's Balhaf LNG complex and sits alongside the Gulf of Aden. The Shabwa attack occurred in a province containing the Balhaf complex, increasing its energy-security relevance without proving direct LNG infrastructure damage. Balhaf operating and export status is checked daily; no supply loss is confirmed.

Bab al-Mandeb

Commodity transits through Bab al-Mandeb have fallen on high-alert days this autumn. Houthi capability keeps the strait in the daily check. The security risk zone is not confined to Hormuz.

Red Sea

The Red Sea's importance rises as Hormuz reliability falls: Saudi crude can exit west via the East-West pipeline to Yanbu. Sunday's Rabigh claim sits directly on this corridor, which is why its verification status matters even though Rabigh is oil and petrochemicals, not LNG.

Yanbu / Red Sea corridor

Yanbu is the bypass that reduces Saudi reliance on Hormuz, and becomes more important as Hormuz becomes less reliable. France's protective focus sits here. A verified attack removing East-West pipeline or Yanbu loading capacity would materially alter the market; none is confirmed.

LNG carriers

For every incident the questions are: LNG carrier or not, laden or ballast, origin, destination, hit, diverted, damaged. Qatari loadings recovered to roughly half of pre-war levels in November; an LNG security incident matters more to UK gas than an unrelated general-cargo incident, and Qatar needs predictable safe passage, not occasional successful crossings.

Tanker war-risk insurance

A route can remain physically open and still carry a substantial war-risk premium. Friction gets priced: insurance, freight and rerouting raise delivered energy cost even where no cargo is lost. Premium direction after Saturday's warnings is the next data point to verify with the market.

Saudi East-West crude bypass flows and Qatar LNG passage through Hormuz are kept separate: oil has more rerouting options than LNG, but only current verified flows establish the net market effect. LNG remains the bridge between Gulf security and UK gas.

Supplier Price-Book Lag

Wholesale moved first. Supplier books may still follow.

Last week's pattern reversed hard: every offered gas and power season in the Friday-to-Monday comparison rose, and the W-26 working references are up about 10% since Thursday. Supplier price-book lag is the opportunity and the danger. Some books may still carry Friday's pricing; others will reprice as today's wholesale strength feeds through. The market does not need to rise again tomorrow for customers to pay more tomorrow: it only needs suppliers to catch up with what wholesale has already done.

Once a supplier withdraws a rate, the customer cannot go back and secure yesterday's price. Equally, this report does not claim every supplier has raised every tariff, or that any specific old quote remains available. What can be evidenced is the seasonal indicator direction (all supplied rows higher) and the wholesale rebound behind it.

What to verify on any live quote

  • Supplier name, quote date and time, and validity expiry (real, not assumed).
  • Start date relative to the customer's contract end date (CED); future-start contracts begin when the current agreement ends.
  • Unit rate, standing charge, pass-through charges, broker commission, term and any volume tolerance.
  • Credit and eligibility conditions, meter type and profile.
  • Comparison against at least one genuine alternative obtained the same day.

British Gas's 18-month renewal window remains relevant for eligible customers, but is confirmed per product and per customer, never assumed.

Waiting is not neutral

Waiting might help if Norwegian and US LNG supply improves, weather turns mild, wind rises, Gulf shipping normalises or suppliers hold or cut rates. Waiting might hurt if cold and low wind persist, storage draws faster, shipping losses remove physical LNG, Norway suffers renewed outages or supplier books reprice upward. Waiting only works if the market moves in the customer's favour. The customer's choice is a risk trade-off made with evidence-based quote comparison, never a guess at the market bottom.
