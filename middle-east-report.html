Section 5

Futures Market Explained

Wholesale gas and power are traded for future delivery periods: months, quarters and seasons (summer S, winter W, by year). A supplier's price book for a contract starting in 2027 or 2028 is built from these forward prices, not from today's prompt market. The prices a UK business is offered for a future-start contract therefore reflect the forward curve at the moment the supplier priced the book, plus the supplier's own risk allowance, hedging and commercial terms.

Today's curve is in steep backwardation: the front (W-26 working reference, 214.10p gas / £166.56 power) sits far above later delivery periods because the market is paying a war and winter risk premium for near-term supply. Backwardation does not mean wait. It means the market currently prices the future cheaper than the present; it says nothing about what that future period will cost to buy later. Cheaper further out does not mean cheaper later.

The Switch365 W-26 figures are internal working references used to track day-to-day movement: they are not ICE settlements and not executable customer rates. The % vs W-26 column in this report shows the current forward-value gap between each delivery period and the front working reference, nothing more. Customer bills also include standing charges, pass-through costs, network and policy charges, credit terms and commission, so a wholesale percentage move is never the same as a whole-bill percentage move.

The commercial chain that matters: event → physical energy consequence → wholesale contract / forward curve → supplier price book → customer action. Each link must be evidenced separately; none can be assumed from the one before.

Section 8

British Gas 18-Month Window

British Gas allows eligible business customers to agree a renewal up to 18 months before their current contract ends, an extended window compared with the more common 12-month market norm. The new agreement still starts on the existing contract's end date; signing early changes when the price is secured, not when supply switches.

That window turns today's market intelligence into a practical customer action. With 2027 and 2028 seasonal offers sitting 28-58% below the front working reference on this morning's internal sheet, a customer inside the window can test a forward-start price now rather than waiting for a renewal letter in a possibly tighter market. Eligibility is not universal: it depends on the customer's contract, credit and product, and it does not apply automatically across other suppliers.

British Gas 18-month eligibility turns this market intelligence into a practical customer action. Confirm the customer's window, pull the live BG future-start quote for the exact CED and term, and benchmark it against the same supplier's earlier offers and the wider market before recommending.

Caveats: an early fix locks in today's curve, including any remaining war premium in the 2027 seasons; if a genuine peace dividend materialises, prices could be lower later. Present both sides and let the customer's risk appetite decide.

Section 14

Ceasefire Reality Check

The 11 November primary test: Rubio's G7 statements and Qatar's briefing are policy and diplomatic signals, not a ceasefire and not a guarantee of safe LNG shipping.

For a genuine de-escalation dividend, the report requires, in order: an operative signed agreement; demonstrably reduced UKMTO incident risk; safe, insured, regular tanker and LNG transits; and measurable shipped tonnes. Today the evidence stands at: no signed agreement; incidents continuing (UKMTO #201 on Tuesday, ADNOC vessel damaged); transits a fraction of normal; October tonnes improved but roughly half of pre-war levels. Each step must be verified, not inferred from the one before.

Open verification questions

  • Have the 27 October interceptions (three missiles, four drones) and the Ras Tanura claim produced any verified refinery or export consequence? Independent site confirmations are still absent.
  • Are Saudi Eastern Region fields, Ras Tanura, Abqaiq, Dhahran, the East-West pipeline, Yanbu and Rabigh operating, with output/export evidence?
  • Has Egypt's 26 October authority converted into a Saudi-requested, verified deployment?
  • Have the late-October Iranian diplomatic tracks (Moscow GECF, the conditional four-day Hormuz proposal) moved beyond dialogue?
  • Do UKMTO and operators confirm repeatable, insured, safe LNG and oil passage through Hormuz, the Gulf of Aden and Bab al-Mandeb?
  • Are Qatari loadings rising sustainably, or are US spot cargoes still carrying the shortfall?
  • Is Norwegian flow recovering on schedule, and is Europe now firmly in withdrawal mode? (GIE says yes to the latter.)
  • Is softer Brent being matched by TTF/NBP and real supplier books, or is it only oil-market diplomacy relief?
Diplomatic contact is not a ceasefire, and a ceasefire announcement is not verified safe passage.
An intercepted missile is not a confirmed energy infrastructure hit, and a hit is not necessarily lost production.
An improvement in security can reduce a risk premium before damaged infrastructure is repaired, but physical flows are the supply test. Never assume price direction from the geopolitical narrative alone.

Section 17

Wholesale Has Moved. Have Supplier Books Caught Up?

The commercial question this Wednesday is precise: Tuesday's NBP Dec-26 rebound (+2.96%), this morning's internal front jump (+7.1%), continued G7 economic pressure and unsecured Hormuz shipping sit on one side; this morning's softer seasonal offers sit on the other. Which force reaches the next customer-specific supplier quote is an empirical question, answered only by pulling live books for the same CED, term, credit and product.

Yesterday proved the point in reverse: the front fell 9.4% while six of eight gas seasonal offers rose. This morning the front rose while six of eight fell. A move in an index or working comparator never equals the same move in a customer's whole bill. Supplier price-book lag is the opportunity and the danger: a supplier may still be offering yesterday's higher rate, or may have just repriced lower; some offers will be withdrawn, others will improve.

Wholesale markets move first. Supplier books often follow.
Once a supplier withdraws a rate, the customer cannot go back and secure yesterday's price.
The market does not need to rise again tomorrow for customers to pay more tomorrow. Ask for executable quotes and their validity windows; do not invent a "lock before 10am" deadline, and do not promise the dip survives the week.

Customer Action

Five commercial action cards. The action everywhere is "review live pricing now", never "blindly contract immediately".

1. Existing valid quote Top priority

Confirm the quote is still executable: product eligibility, remaining validity, supplier terms and customer consent. Prioritise genuinely exposed customers. There is no guarantee of how the supplier reprices next, in either direction.

2. Contract ends in 2026

Check currently available start dates, avoid out-of-contract exposure, and confirm the existing fixed agreement can end correctly with no overlapping supply obligation. With the front reference up +7.1% this morning, near-term exposure carries the full war premium.

3. Inside 12 months

Do not wait for renewal panic. Compare available fixed and future-start offers, supplier windows, the historic forward curve and the customer's usage and standing charges. Demonstrate the actual offered contract, not just wholesale prices.

4. British Gas inside 18 months

Confirm BG's extended renewal window applies to this customer and pull live pricing. The new agreement still starts at the existing contract's end date; eligibility is not automatic and not universal across suppliers.

5. 2027 / 2028 / 2029 / 2030 CED: your delivery period matters

Seasonal backwardation is steep and, on this morning's supplied sheet, still softening: gas S-28 at 89.50p is 58.2% below the 214.10p front reference; power S-28 at £75.50 is 54.7% below £166.56. Waiting is not neutral

Waiting could help if: diplomacy converts to insured transits, Qatar LNG improves further, Norway restarts cleanly, US LNG stays strong, storage draws stay modest, weather stays mild and supplier books keep softening. Waiting could hurt if: another tanker or LNG carrier is hit, Hormuz traffic falls back, Visund slips, cold calm weather lands, or suppliers withdraw and reissue books higher. Waiting only works if the market moves in the customer's favour.
