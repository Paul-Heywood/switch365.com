Summary

Fifty-eight people were killed and more than 200 injured when three ballistic missiles struck a government security forces training camp at al-Alam, in the Jardan district of Yemen's Shabwa province, during a Monday morning training exercise. Fourteen people remain missing. The strike, claimed by the Houthis and verified by Reuters footage, is the deadliest single attack since Yemen's fighting resumed in September. Shabwa is an energy-rich province overlooking the Gulf of Aden and contains the Balhaf LNG export complex, Yemen's largest industrial investment. The attack is significant not because LNG infrastructure was confirmed hit, but because major ballistic-missile fighting has moved into a province containing Yemen's principal LNG export complex and sitting beside the Gulf of Aden shipping system.

The Houthis separately claim six attacks on Saudi Arabia in 48 hours, including an Aramco facility at Yanbu, the Shaybah oilfield, and Riyadh and Abha airports. None of the energy strikes has been independently verified, and a claimed attack is not the same as lost supply. Yanbu matters because it is the Red Sea outlet of the East-West Pipeline that lets Saudi crude bypass Hormuz, and Shaybah matters because it is a major producing field. Saudi Arabia's flagship investment conference has been postponed to 2027, and Egypt's parliament has delayed until 26 October a vote on deploying forces to defend the kingdom. The security risk zone is not confined to Hormuz.

For UK gas, watch LNG, not just Brent. Oil through Hormuz has recovered to roughly 13 million barrels per day, around 23% below pre-war, while Qatar LNG exports through the strait managed only about 1.4 million tonnes in September, roughly a fifth of pre-war, and QatarEnergy force majeure now runs into November and December for several buyers. TTF front month has climbed to around €88.5/MWh and Brent is trading near $110/bbl. Switch365 internal offered pricing has moved up again overnight: W-26 gas is now 217.25p/therm and W-26 power £166.60/MWh, both up for a second consecutive session. Supplier price-book lag is the opportunity and the danger: wholesale markets move first, supplier books often follow.

Futures Market Explained

The futures market shows today's price for energy delivered in a future period. A customer does not have to wait until their existing contract ends to see today's future-delivery price.

Cheaper further out does not mean cheaper later. Backwardation does not mean wait. It means future delivery is cheaper today. Waiting gives the market time to remove today's opportunity. Waiting is not neutral.

The % vs W-26 column used throughout this report shows the current forward-value gap: how far each future delivery period is priced below the front winter working reference right now.

British Gas 18-Month Window

British Gas has extended its business energy renewal window from 12 months to 18 months. This gives eligible customers an earlier opportunity to review future-start pricing.

The contract does not start today. It begins when the existing agreement ends. The 18-month window turns today's forward curve into something customers may actually be able to secure.

British Gas 18-month eligibility turns this market intelligence into a practical customer action: with S-27 gas 22.4% below W-26 and 2028 delivery more than 50% below, the window reaches directly into the cheapest part of the curve. Not every customer is automatically eligible, so eligibility should be checked against the actual contract end date.

Once a supplier withdraws a rate, the customer cannot go back and secure yesterday's price.

Treating this as verified Switch365 internal evidence: the renewal window extension is live commercial fact, and it now covers delivery periods priced roughly half the front winter.

Top Five Warning Signs

1. BALHAF LNG OR ITS GAS INFRASTRUCTURE IS PHYSICALLY HIT

The 19 October strike was in Shabwa, not on Balhaf. A confirmed hit on the complex, its perimeter, or the Marib-Balhaf pipeline would convert geographic risk into physical energy risk and would change the hero of this report. Current status: not triggered.

2. YANBU / EAST-WEST PIPELINE OR SHAYBAH SUFFERS VERIFIED CAPACITY LOSS

The East-West Pipeline was already damaged once in September and restarted at reduced rates. Verified new damage to Yanbu, the pipeline, or Shaybah output would threaten Saudi Arabia's Hormuz bypass and repriced crude and products globally. Current status: claims only, not verified.

3. QATAR LNG DETERIORATES FROM THE SEPTEMBER RECOVERY

September's 1.4m tonnes was a seven-month high but still ~79% below pre-war. If October fails to build on it, or if laden carriers continue to hold off the strait, the European winter premium strengthens further. Current status: watch; two carriers holding off the UAE on 19 October.

4. AN LNG CARRIER IS HIT, DISABLED OR LOST

July's missile strike on the Qatari carrier Al Rekayyat showed the scenario. A repeat would likely halt the tentative September recovery in Qatari loadings immediately. No LNG carrier has been hit in the current October window. Current status: not triggered.

5. TTF / NBP RISE AND SUPPLIERS WITHDRAW BOOKS

TTF is up ~20% in a month and the internal W-26 reference has risen to 217.25p/therm. Past Switch365 experience shows some suppliers pulled books near 180p/therm. Any wave of book withdrawals would close customer windows before the wholesale move is finished. Current status: partially active; books elevated, not yet withdrawn.

Supplier Price-Book Lag: Wholesale Has Moved. Have Supplier Books Caught Up?

Supplier price-book lag is the opportunity and the danger. Wholesale markets move first. Supplier books often follow.

Switch365 internal commercial experience: when wholesale gas previously reached around 180p/therm, some suppliers pulled books, paused pricing and returned higher. That is internal commercial experience, not an official industry trigger. The internal W-26 working reference is now 217.25p/therm and has risen in consecutive sessions, from 210.29p on Friday to 214.79p on Monday to 217.25p today.

The practical consequence: a quote issued against last week's market may still be valid today, and it may be materially below the price the same supplier would issue tomorrow morning. The market does not need to rise again tomorrow for customers to pay more tomorrow. Once a supplier withdraws a rate, the customer cannot go back and secure yesterday's price.
