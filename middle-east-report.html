IF QATAR IS PROTECTING ITSELF AGAINST FUTURE ENERGY CRISES, SHOULDN'T UK BUSINESSES BE DOING THE SAME?

One of the world's biggest LNG exporters is locking in supplies for decades to protect against future Hormuz disruption, even as its export flows improve. A Qatari carrier attacked after reportedly receiving safe-passage approval shows why that protection matters now.

Hormuz route risk: high LNG carrier attacked 30 Oct EU storage 74.68% (GIE, 1 Nov) Switch365 curve: gas 8/8 up Diplomacy: Doha talks day 3 Review-now window

QatarEnergy is one of the world's largest LNG producers, with insight into international gas markets, infrastructure and shipping risks far beyond that of a typical UK business. Nevertheless, it is taking active steps to protect its future supply and revenue against another Strait of Hormuz disruption. If a major global gas producer is preparing for the next energy crisis while still navigating the current one, shouldn't UK businesses be reviewing how exposed they are to energy-price shocks?

QatarEnergy signed a 20-year agreement with Woodside on 29 October 2026 for 3 million tonnes a year of US LNG from 2030 (Reuters, 29 Oct; QNA, 29 Oct). It also signed two agreements with Cheniere on 22 October for up to 4 million tonnes annually, starting in the early 2030s and running through 2054 (Cheniere, 22 Oct). Together the agreements cover up to 7 million tonnes per year once contractual deliveries are established, and QatarEnergy says more long-term purchases are planned. These future contracts do not begin replacing missing LNG cargoes today, nor do they directly offset the damaged Qatari capacity tonne for tonne.

The reason is not guesswork. In his 29 October Reuters interview, QatarEnergy CEO Saad Sherida Al-Kaabi explained that purchasing long-term external LNG gives the company a trading and revenue hedge if its own exports are obstructed by future closures of Hormuz (Reuters, 29 Oct). QatarEnergy has the fleet and trading business to market LNG produced outside the Gulf even during disruption at home. The company is investing in resilience while other parts of its domestic production and export system recover. It is acting on a serious, recognised risk, not claiming it knows precisely when another crisis will occur.

On Friday 30 October, Qatar-linked LNG carrier Al Mayeda, reportedly carrying Qatari LNG destined for Pakistan, was attacked near Hormuz. Qatar's authorities acknowledged the incident; there were no reported crew injuries or environmental damage, and operator Nakilat confirmed on Sunday that the Marshall Islands-flagged vessel had been hit but remained operational. Bloomberg reported on Sunday 1 November that Pakistan had arranged safe passage and Iranian authorities had reportedly approved the transit shortly before the attack (Bloomberg via gCaptain, 1 Nov). The ship subsequently turned around. This is attributed reporting of purported approval, not independent confirmation that Iran authorised, directed or knew about the attack. But it underlines the commercial reality: permission to transit is not the same as repeatable, insured and safe LNG delivery.

Qatar's LNG vessel activity has improved from its worst months, but visibility remains incomplete because of ships travelling without public AIS transmissions. Damage to two Ras Laffan trains represents approximately 12.8 mtpa of nameplate capacity, with repair guidance around three years; some contracted deliveries are still affected by force majeure, with Edison's 27 October notice extending through 31 December. We must distinguish actual production, cargo loadings, arrivals, displaced customer obligations and safely completed voyages. Kpler tracking cited by Reuters put Qatari LNG exports at around 1.6 million tonnes in September, less than a quarter of the pre-war monthly average, with October tracking lower still at roughly 0.6 million tonnes. Qatar is not waiting for a perfect return to normal before preparing for future disruption.

Europe enters winter with a thin storage buffer. The GIE site status for gas day 1 November (06:00 CEST) showed EU gas storage at 74.68% and Germany at 61.17%, against roughly 83% a year ago and a five-year norm near 87% (GIE, 1 Nov). Dutch TTF front-month trading reached €100/MWh intraday on 28 October, its highest since December 2022, before easing on Thursday and then settling around €103.94 on Friday 30 October after the Al Mayeda attack. Europe and Asia are competing for flexible LNG at the same time QatarEnergy is also purchasing replacement spot cargoes from abroad. Weather, Norway, US LNG, demand response and successful diplomacy can ease pressure; replacement buying should not be counted twice as an independent global supply loss.

UK SMEs cannot control wars, the safe passage of LNG carriers, foreign gas infrastructure or wholesale futures. They can control when they review their contracts, the duration and scope of price protection they buy, and whether they expose themselves to another renewal at an uncertain future market level. The latest Switch365 Monday 2 November internal offered indicators remain significantly backwardated, with summer/winter 2028 gas approximately 56.1%/55.3% below the near-term W-26 gas working reference and summer/winter 2028 power approximately 54.4%/51.8% below a separately estimated W-26 power comparator. These are different-delivery-period price indicators, not guaranteed customer bill savings. A future-start fixed agreement may deserve consideration where eligible, because cheaper further out does not mean cheaper later.

QatarEnergy is buying physical LNG and hedging trading revenue, whereas an SME fixed electricity or gas contract hedges aspects of its energy bill. They are different instruments, so the analogy is risk management, not a claim that fixing for the maximum number of years is automatically best. Check the customer's contract end date, forecast consumption, current valid supplier offers, credit/site eligibility, what is truly fixed versus pass-through, standing charges, regulatory changes, price differentials, termination provisions and appetite for risk. British Gas's 18-month renewal window may allow eligible customers to review future-start rates well before the current contract ends. Compare alternative contract lengths rather than pressuring a customer.

QatarEnergy is protecting itself against the next disruption even as some current shipping flows recover. After a tanker attack involving a supposedly cleared transit, that strategy has immediate relevance. For a UK company, the central question is whether today's available, genuinely valid contract offers provide an appropriate degree of future price certainty at an acceptable total cost. Waiting without reviewing those choices leaves commercial risk unmanaged; reviewing now is sensible even if the eventual decision is not to sign a long fixed term.

UK electricity fundamentals

Gas matters to UK power, but generation conditions determine how much. Late-week UK market assessments (29 Oct) had wind output already below 4.5 GW and averaging under 5.5 GW into early November as high pressure builds, well below seasonal norms. French nuclear unavailability is scheduled at 14.1 GW for November, with EDF warning of possible generation limits at Saint-Alban on low Rhône flows. That combination raises the gas-fired share and tightens NESO margins, which is why the modelled W-26 power comparator must never be presented as verified wholesale evidence: verify wind, nuclear, interconnector and demand data at the point of any customer conversation.

05Futures Market Explained

Wholesale gas and power for future delivery periods trade as contracts on exchanges such as ICE. The prices in Sections 3 and 4 are Switch365's internal observations of supplier offered ranges for each delivery season, not exchange settlements, but they follow the same logic: each season has its own price, and together those prices form the forward curve.

Today's curve is backwardated: near-term delivery is expensive because the market is pricing live winter risk (Qatar LNG, Hormuz shipping, low storage), while delivery years further out are priced materially lower on the expectation of new supply and repaired infrastructure. Backwardation does not mean wait. Cheaper further out does not mean cheaper later: a lower S-28 offered quote today is not a forecast of what 2028 will cost to buy when 2028 arrives. The % vs W-26 column shows the current forward-value gap; supplier price-book validity decides whether a customer can actually capture it.

08British Gas 18-Month Window

British Gas business customers may be able to renew up to 18 months before their current contract end date, with the new agreement starting when the existing one ends. Eligibility is confirmed customer by customer and is not automatic; product availability, credit and site criteria apply.

In a market where every supplied 2027-2030 gas season is offered well below the near-term W-26 working reference, British Gas 18-month eligibility turns this market intelligence into a practical customer action: an eligible customer can test future-start pricing for 2027-2028 delivery today rather than waiting for a renewal window that may open into a repriced market. The new contract does not overlap the old one; it begins at the existing end date. Waiting is not neutral: if supplier books rise again, the window still exists but the prices inside it may not.

  • Did the weekend's Doha talks produce a signed, enforceable agreement, or a conditional proposal? Answer as of Monday morning: a proposal under discussion, no conclusion (Reuters, 1 Nov).

  • Is Norwegian gas flowing after outages, are US LNG deliveries continuing, and is European storage injecting or withdrawing? Norway carried extended unplanned outages in late-week assessments; US supply strong; GIE shows early withdrawals.
  • Is falling Brent accompanied by lower TTF/NBP and real supplier books? Not yet: oil eased ~2.9% Monday morning while TTF opened firmer near €104.
Diplomatic contact is not a ceasefire, and a ceasefire announcement is not verified safe passage.
An intercepted missile is not a confirmed energy infrastructure hit, and a hit is not necessarily lost production.
An improvement in security can reduce a risk premium before damaged infrastructure is repaired, but physical flows are the supply test.

15Shipping Risk

Route-by-route. The security risk zone is not confined to Hormuz. A route can remain open and still carry a substantial war-risk premium. Friction gets priced.

Hormuz

Al Mayeda (30 Oct, laden LNG, Marshall Islands-flagged, Qatar to Pakistan): attacked near the strait, reportedly hours after Iranian transit approval negotiated by Pakistan; turned back; crew safe; attackers unidentified. UKMTO #186/#187 (29 Oct): separate incidents, one projectile strike and one small-boat explosives attack. BBC Verify analysis of UKMTO data recorded 12 vessel attacks in the week 5-11 Oct, the highest weekly total of the conflict, with oil shipments through the strait at ~12.6m bpd on 13 Oct versus ~15m at end-September.

Gulf of Oman

Approaches remain an incident zone; Al Mayeda reversed course toward the Gulf rather than continuing east. Passage permission is not repeatable, insured delivery.

Persian Gulf

Iranian claims of vessels struck in the northern Gulf remain claims; cross-check against UKMTO, operators and tracking before counting any loss.

Gulf of Aden

Shabwa sits on this coast: the earlier training-camp attack (73 killed, 200+ injured) occurred in a province containing Yemen's Balhaf LNG complex, increasing its energy-security relevance without proving direct LNG infrastructure damage.

Bab al-Mandeb

Houthi capability keeps the southern Red Sea chokepoint in play; Egyptian and Turkish interest in Red Sea security reflects that exposure.

Red Sea

As Hormuz becomes less reliable, the Red Sea route becomes more valuable and more strategic: Saudi crude can move east-to-west via the East-West pipeline to Yanbu.

Yanbu / Red Sea corridor

No verified damage. A confirmed hit removing East-West or Yanbu capacity would materially alter the market; claims alone do not.

LNG carriers

LNG remains the bridge between Gulf security and UK gas. Bloomberg put October LNG shipments through Hormuz at ~30% of pre-war volumes; Qatar has used older vessels to cut insurance cost. An LNG security incident matters more to UK gas than an unrelated general-cargo incident.

Tanker war-risk insurance

War-risk premia and charter rates remain elevated even where cargo volumes have found alternative paths; reduced physical supply and elevated delivered cost are different things.

17Supplier Price-Book Lag

WHOLESALE HAS MOVED. HAVE SUPPLIER BOOKS CAUGHT UP?

TTF settled near €103.94 on Friday after the Al Mayeda attack and opened Monday around €104.10; the Switch365 offered range confirms supplier indicators moved with it: all eight gas seasons up Friday-to-Monday, led by S-27 +7.00p. Five of six power seasons rose; W-29 power fell £0.22, proof that books reprice individually rather than mechanically.

Supplier price-book lag is the opportunity and the danger.
Wholesale markets move first. Supplier books often follow.
Once a supplier withdraws a rate, the customer cannot go back and secure yesterday's price.
The market does not need to rise again tomorrow for customers to pay more tomorrow.

Practical test for every live conversation: is the quote still valid today, for this site, this consumption profile, this start date? If yes, the customer is looking at a book that may not have finished repricing. That is the opportunity. The danger is assuming it will still be there next week.

18Customer Action

1. Existing valid quote Top priority

Confirm the quote is still executable: product eligibility, remaining validity, supplier terms and customer consent. Prioritise genuinely exposed customers. No guarantee of future repricing; act while validity holds.

2. Contract ends in 2026

Check currently available start dates, avoid out-of-contract exposure, confirm any existing fixed agreement ends correctly and there is no overlapping supply obligation. With winter risk live and storage thin, deemed rates are the expensive default.

3. Inside 12 months

Compare available fixed and future-start offers, supplier windows, the historic forward curve and the customer's usage and standing charges. Demonstrate the actual offered contract, not just wholesale prices. Customers inside their 12-month renewal window should act now, not in 30 days.

4. British Gas inside 18 months

Confirm BG's renewal-window eligibility for the specific account; the new agreement still starts at the original contract end date. Where eligible, test future-start rates against today's 2027-2028 offered indicators rather than waiting for a window that opens into a repriced market.

5. 2027 / 2028 / 2029 / 2030 delivery

Explain current seasonal backwardation starting at 2027, with 2028/2029/2030 options only where valid and available to the customer. Quantify current versus Friday, 1 Year Ago, percentage vs W-26 (S-28 gas 56.1% lower; S-28 power 54.4% lower) and customer-specific terms. Longer terms buy certainty but can forego savings if markets fall.

Waiting is not neutral

Waiting could help if:

  • Shaybah remains fully operational
  • Air defence prevents further attacks
  • Yanbu and the East-West pipeline stay unaffected
  • Qatar LNG improves and Hormuz stabilises
  • Norway and US LNG stay strong
  • Storage performs well and weather turns mild
  • Supplier books fall

Waiting could hurt if:

  • Shaybah output falls or processing is damaged
  • Yanbu is hit or the East-West pipeline interrupted
  • Another LNG carrier is hit; Qatar LNG deteriorates
  • Hormuz shipping worsens; Saudi attacks accelerate
  • Supplier books rise again
WAITING ONLY WORKS IF THE MARKET MOVES IN THE CUSTOMER'S FAVOUR.
