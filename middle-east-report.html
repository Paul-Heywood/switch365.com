The Wall Street Journal reports that the United States is sending a third aircraft carrier and up to 10,000 additional personnel to the Middle East, with the Associated Press identifying the USS Theodore Roosevelt strike group and roughly 9,000 sailors and Marines expected in the region by November. Trump says he is weighing his options on Iran and has used an explicit threat of destruction if Tehran does not sign what he considers a fair agreement. Treat the scale and timing as reported until the Pentagon confirms the full operational detail: this is the freshest escalation and duration development, not proof that a new large-scale attack has begun.

Iranian officials told Reuters that Tehran has instructed allied groups in Yemen, Iraq and Lebanon to prepare to support a wider coordinated response if the United States launches another large-scale attack. Iran's Foreign Ministry did not confirm the preparations, and preparation is not the same as an attack. Alongside the military story, Chinese refiners have suspended October refined-product exports beyond Hong Kong and Macau until further notice, according to Reuters sources, while Russia's diesel-export restriction remains in force through October. Diesel availability is a separate physical problem from crude availability: refineries and product cargoes, not oilfields, are the constraint.

The physical counterweight is real. The East–West Pipeline restarted on 22 September and Yanbu loadings have resumed, Middle Eastern crude exports recovered to approximately 16.328 million b/d in September, and US LNG exports rose to 10.9 million tonnes with Europe taking 54% of cargoes. Active Brent December is holding around $102 after Thursday's $4.28 rise, while the indicative NBP front-month reference near 180.6p/therm remains below the September peak. Expectations of renewed fighting and a tighter refined-fuel market have restored the oil risk premium, even though physical crude and LNG supply show important signs of recovery. Futures Market Explained

The futures market shows today's price for energy delivered in a future period.

A customer does not have to wait until their contract end date to see today's price for future delivery. A future-start contract can be secured now where supplier eligibility and procurement rules allow, and the tables in Sections 3 and 4 are exactly that: today's offered price for gas and power delivered in Winter 2026 through to Summer 2030.

Cheaper further out does not mean cheaper later. Backwardation does not mean wait.

Backwardation means nearer delivery is priced above later delivery. On today's internal evidence, Winter 2026 gas at 186.00p/therm sits 63.7% above Summer 2030 at 67.49p. That gap creates an opportunity for future-start procurement; it does not guarantee that those prices remain available. The relevant question is: can the customer secure the cheaper future delivery today, not will energy definitely be cheaper when that date arrives.

08

British Gas 18-Month Window

British Gas may allow an eligible customer to secure a future-start contract up to 18 months before their current contract ends. The new contract starts after the existing contract finishes, not today.

Eligibility must be checked, and supplier availability, term and credit remain relevant. The price is not protected until the contract is accepted and confirmed. What makes the window commercially powerful this morning is the shape of the curve: a customer whose contract ends within 18 months can test today's backwardated forward prices, where Summer 2028 gas is offered 54.6% below the front winter and Summer 2029 power is 54.6% below, rather than waiting to renew into whatever the front of the curve looks like at expiry.

British Gas 18-month eligibility turns this market intelligence into a practical customer action. Against supplier volatility and rising duration risk, a future-start contract removes the renewal date as a point of exposure: the customer is no longer betting on what the market looks like on one specific day in 2027.

Supplier Price-Book Lag

Wholesale risk has changed again. Have supplier books?

Supplier price-book lag is the opportunity and the danger.

Wholesale markets move first. Supplier books often follow. The verified progression this week: European gas softened through late September as Qatar-linked LNG movement improved and Gulf crude exports recovered, then Thursday 1 October delivered the third-carrier report, the Iranian preparation story and China's product-export suspension in a single session. Brent December settled $4.28 higher; the internal Switch365 offered range shows Winter 2026 gas up 5.00p and Winter 2026 power up £1.00 between Thursday and Friday mornings. Friday 2 October is the live checkpoint: any supplier book that has not yet repriced is quoting off yesterday's market.

Once a supplier withdraws a rate, the customer cannot go back and secure yesterday's price. The market does not need to rise again tomorrow for customers to pay more tomorrow. Fresh-check on every quote today: which suppliers held prices, which repriced, which pulled or suspended products, whether validity shortened, and whether any existing quote remains live.
