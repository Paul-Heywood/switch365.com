Summary

Today's commercial position: diplomacy is finally moving in the right direction, but nothing physical has changed yet. Qatar LNG is still stranded, Hormuz traffic is still a fraction of normal, European storage is still the weakest for this point in the year in records back to 2009, and the internal Switch365 offered curve for Winter 2026 edged higher again on Tuesday morning. This is a review-now window, not a wait-and-see market.

Overnight in 30 seconds

  • Iran and Oman agreed to work on a temporary joint navigational corridor through Hormuz and on a joint mine-clearing project, in a joint statement after talks in Tehran on Tuesday. Technical talks continue. This is meaningful diplomatic progress, not physical normalisation.
  • An oil tanker was struck by an unknown projectile and disabled about 9 nautical miles northeast of Ash Shishah, Oman, at the entrance to the strait, UKMTO reported (Warning 120-26). The crew are safe. The strait remains dangerous even as diplomats talk.
  • Washington launched Operation Economic Outcast on Monday, sanctioning nearly 60 Iran-linked entities, individuals and vessels and widening secondary-sanctions risk across digital assets, technology, gold, aviation and shipping.
  • Qatar has exported only 18 LNG cargoes during the comparable conflict period versus 509 a year ago, a cumulative reduction of roughly 96%, Reuters/ICIS data shows. Two Qatari tankers have been attacked. QatarEnergy has not commented.
  • Hormuz saw only around 5 tracked commodity-vessel movements on Tuesday (Kpler-based tracking), versus a pre-war norm of roughly 130 to 140 transits a day. Five tracked commodity vessels is an improvement from four, but it is nowhere near normal trade.
  • Brent fell more than $2 a barrel on Wednesday morning to about $86, its lowest since 13 August, after losing over 3% on Tuesday, as the Iran-Oman talks fed reopening hopes. Brent is reacting to diplomacy. Brent is not the customer's gas quote.

What it means

Oil can fall while European gas remains expensive. LNG remains the bridge between Hormuz and UK gas: while Qatari cargoes stay stranded and storage sits roughly 20 percentage points below the seasonal norm, Europe and Asia keep competing for replacement LNG, TTF stays exposed, NBP stays exposed, and UK gas-fired generation costs stay elevated. The internal offered curve confirms it: gas W-26 at 165.50 p/therm and power W-26 at £139.00/MWh are both roughly double where they were priced a year ago. The next decisive signal is physical: do Qatar LNG tankers actually start moving, does TTF fall, does NBP follow, and do supplier books improve.

Section 5

Futures Market Explained

The futures market shows today's price for energy delivered in a future period. A customer does not need to wait until their contract end date to see today's price for that future delivery.

Right now the curve is in steep backwardation: every later delivery period is offered well below Winter 2026, because the front of the curve carries the live Hormuz/Qatar risk premium. Cheaper further out does not mean cheaper later. Backwardation does not mean wait. The customer mistake is thinking a cheaper future period means they should wait. It does not. It means we should check whether that future period can be secured now.

The future market is only useful while the customer can still buy that future delivery period. Waiting gives the market time to remove today's forward-value opportunity. Waiting is not neutral.

British Gas has extended its business energy renewal window from 12 months to 18 months. This gives eligible customers an earlier chance to review future-start pricing. Not every customer is automatically eligible, so eligibility must be checked first.

The 18-month British Gas window matters because it turns the forward market into a practical customer action. Your new contract would not start today. It would begin when your current agreement ends. Waiting is not neutral. Shipping Risk

Hormuz

Hormuz is not open as usual. Tuesday's count was ~5 tracked commodity vessels versus ~4 on Monday and a recent 10-day average of ~15; independent AIS trackers show total crossings in the mid-teens, down from a 36-vessel peak on 22 August. Pre-war norm: roughly 130-140 transits a day. Tuesday's tracked composition: two LPG tankers exiting, one bitumen tanker exiting, two empty product tankers entering. No confirmed LNG transits. Some vessels sail dark (AIS off), so counts are tracked minimums, and a vessel count is not an energy volume. A route can remain physically navigable while remaining commercially impaired.

Qatar LNG

The decisive figure: 18 cargoes versus 509 over the comparable conflict period (ICIS via Reuters), a ~96% cumulative reduction, ~$24bn in lost sales, two tankers attacked. Note methodology: earlier Kpler-based research showed Qatar LNG more than 60% below prior-year levels over a narrower window; the 18-vs-509 comparison covers a broader period. Different windows, not contradictions. The 96% figure is cumulative; do not read it as "Qatar is exporting 4% of normal today".

Bab el-Mandeb

Approximately 31 tracked commodity vessels Tuesday (29 Monday; 10-day average ~31), though the Houthis' declared blockade of Saudi-linked shipping since 20 July has cut tanker transits roughly 40% from July peaks and a cargo ship sank after a USV attack on 4 August. Bab el-Mandeb remains risky but physical traffic is currently considerably healthier than Hormuz.

Workarounds

Saudi East-West pipeline to Yanbu, SUMED/Sidi Kerir, Fujairah storage, Cape of Good Hope rerouting and dark voyages (ADNOC's Mubaraz and Al Hamra have each completed multiple Hormuz exits) are carrying part of the load. Workarounds reduce outright shortage risk but increase time, freight and complexity. Friction gets priced.

Freight / insurance

War-risk cover and OFAC's updated safe-passage guidance remain the commercial gatekeepers: even soliciting Iranian passage "services" is flagged as a sanctions risk, which will slow any corridor take-up. No fresh insurer withdrawal announcements were verified overnight.

Section 17

Supplier Price-Book Lag

Supplier price-book lag is the opportunity and the danger. Wholesale markets move first. Supplier books often follow. This morning is a textbook case: public benchmarks (Brent, TTF, NBP CFDs) fell 2-4% on the Oman news, while the latest internal offered snapshot, taken Tuesday morning, still shows W-26 gas and power rising. Suppliers price probability, not certainty.

Two-sided risk follows. If diplomacy converts into physical LNG flows, books may soften and today's quotes become a benchmark to beat. If the corridor stalls, or another tanker is hit, books can reprice upward overnight. Once a supplier withdraws a rate, the customer cannot go back and secure yesterday's price.

Action: check live prices rather than assume. Diplomatic progress can lower wholesale markets quickly; persistent Qatar LNG and storage risk can keep risk premiums elevated; supplier books may react at a different speed to both.
