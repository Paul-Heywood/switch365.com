Futures Market Explained

The futures market shows the price for energy delivered in future periods. A customer renewing from 2027, 2028, 2029 or 2030 is not only interested in today's price. They should look at what the market is currently offering for their actual delivery period.

Cheaper further out does not mean cheaper later.

The future market is only useful while the customer can still buy that future delivery period. Waiting lets the market make the decision for the customer. As time passes, that future period becomes the near-term market and can absorb the same risk premium.

The 1 Year Ago line is context. The latest line is the customer's decision point.

British Gas 18-Month Window

British Gas has extended its business energy renewal window from 12 months to 18 months. This gives eligible customers an earlier chance to review future-start pricing.

The 18-month British Gas window matters because it turns the forward market into a practical customer action.

Your new contract would not start today. It would begin when your current agreement ends.

Waiting is not neutral. If the market moves or British Gas withdraws rates, customers cannot go back and secure today's pricing later.

Cheaper further out does not mean cheaper later. It means check whether the future period can be secured now.

Top Five Warning Signs

  1. The US is only semi-negotiating and waiting for economic pressure to worsen. Trump told Axios the US is "low-keying it" with Iran. This is pressure versus pressure, not clean peace. The strategy remains economic and military leverage, not rapid de-escalation.
  2. Tehran says Hormuz reopening depends on US concessions. Iran's security chief says Hormuz will not fully reopen until the US "corrects its behaviour." The Oman route deal is close but not equal to full normalisation. Hormuz is not open as usual.
  3. The Saudi / Turkiye / Pakistan defence pact raises the consequence of any Saudi energy attack. Signed on 07 August, the Mecca Joint Defence Agreement treats an attack on one as an attack on all. It does not mean automatic military retaliation by all three states, but it widens the political and military backdrop.
  4. Houthis claimed an attack on Saudi Aramco's Jazan refinery. The claim came two days after the defence pact. Saudi confirmed a fire and extinguished it, but did not confirm cause. The refinery processes 400,000 bpd. Saudi energy risk remains in the market.
  5. Supplier price-book lag is the opportunity and the danger. Wholesale markets moved first. Supplier books may still follow. Once suppliers withdraw rates or shorten quote validity, customers cannot go back and secure previous pricing. Forward-value gaps against W-26 can disappear quickly.

Lower oil does not remove the warning signs. Relief pricing is not the same as route normalisation. Talks being back in the headline is positive, but it is not the same as a signed deal.

The biggest route warning is that Hormuz is not yet open as usual. The Saudi defence pact does not guarantee automatic retaliation, but it raises the consequence of an attack on Saudi energy infrastructure. The Iraq and Saudi energy risks are now connected through proxy activity, defence commitments and route pressure. Things are spreading out geographically, and that is exactly the type of risk suppliers can price quickly.

Do not confuse diplomacy being discussed with de-escalation being delivered. Route normalisation matters more than political optimism.

Talks are in the headline, but that does not mean the risk is off.

Supplier Price-Book Lag

Supplier price-book lag is the opportunity and the danger.

Wholesale markets move first. Supplier books often follow. Waiting for certainty can mean missing the price movement. Once a supplier withdraws a rate, the customer cannot go back and buy yesterday's price.

Even if oil has fallen from the highs, supplier books may still react to the move that has already happened. Suppliers do not need a full closure of Hormuz or Bab el-Mandeb before they reprice. Suppliers also do not need diplomacy to fail completely before they price renewed conflict risk.

When conflict spreads geographically, suppliers can price the increased probability of disruption before physical closure happens. 1 Year Ago prices are useful context, but they do not protect the customer today.
