Futures Market Explained

Why the curve looks like this · and why falling offers can coexist with rising physical risk

UK business gas and electricity contracts are priced off forward markets: NBP for British gas, TTF for the Dutch benchmark that anchors European pricing. Each contract is a delivery period. W-26 is winter 2026 (the front season, covering now through March 2027), S-27 is summer 2027, W-27 winter 2027, and so on out to W-30.

Why winter costs more than summer

Winter delivery carries heating demand, storage-withdrawal risk and less margin for error, so W-26 and W-27 trade above the summer seasons between them. That downward slope into summer is called backwardation. Thursday's sheet is a textbook example: gas indicated at 205.73p for W-26 against 85.75p for S-28 and 67.50p for S-29. Backwardation does not mean wait. A lower price for delivery in summer 2028 is not a prediction that buying in summer 2028 will be cheap; it is today's price for that delivery period, available only while a supplier will actually write it.

Why prices can fall while the security news gets worse

Two tankers were attacked in the Strait of Hormuz inside three days, yet TTF December closed Wednesday down 4.75% and Thursday's internal sheet fell across every row. Traders price the whole balance at once: US crude inventories reportedly rose 3.4 million barrels in the latest weekly report, the EIA expects global inventories to build through the fourth quarter, Brent is nearer $92 than its late-October peak near $118, and supply workarounds (ship-to-ship transfers off Oman, the Saudi East-West pipeline to Yanbu, the UAE's Habshan–Fujairah line) keep molecules moving. Physical risk lifts the premium; inventory, demand and alternative supply press it down. Thursday the pressuring side won. That can reverse quickly, which is why quote validity matters more than narrative.

From wholesale print to a customer's price

The chain is: event → independently established physical energy consequence → named, dated, same-contract wholesale move → supplier price-book movement → a genuinely available, customer-specific contract with a start date and full costs. A wholesale move is the start of that chain, not the end of it. Suppliers add margin, shape, credit and risk allowances, and they can withdraw or reprice a book at any time. The W-26 working reference and the offered seasons are internal indicators of where books sit; the only number that protects a customer is a valid, executable quote for their meter, usage and contract end date.

“Cheaper further out does not mean cheaper later.” Lower later-season indicators are an opportunity to review eligible future-start terms against customer-specific costs and risk appetite. The market might fall further or rebound.

Procurement Window

Two-sided, evidence first · no declared bottom, no universal fix length, no fabricated deadline

Thursday's sheet strengthened the buying-window case on price alone: the front fell 2.87% and every offered season fell with it. The physical risk picture moved the other way, with a second Hormuz tanker attack inside three days and US–Iran exchanges widening to Kuwait. Both things are true at once. The commercial question is which force moves your supplier's book before you act.

Risks that could make future supplier prices increase

  • Further UKMTO/operator-confirmed tanker incidents, sustained war-risk insurance pressure or reduced laden LNG transit, distinguished from extra unsupported Iranian claims. Wednesday's Khasab strike shows the threat is current, not archival.
  • A new operator-confirmed outage at Qatar's Ras Laffan, the Saudi East-West pipeline or Yanbu, as opposed to a strike claim or interception. QatarEnergy's force majeure already runs to end-December 2026 on damaged trains.
  • Storage and weather: EU 69.95% and Germany 57.11% on the 17 November gas day, in net withdrawal, with winter demand ahead, weaker wind or stronger Asian LNG competition all tightening the balance. Storage is a buffer, not immunity.
  • Norwegian supply shocks or supplier withdrawals/repricing confirmed with named, dated supplier data rather than inferred from Brent or the W-26 proxy alone.

Conditions that could make prices fall further

  • Verified restoration and resilience of supply: strong Norwegian flows can cushion missing LNG; US LNG is a major buffer, not automatically a complete replacement for Qatar.
  • Demand side: milder weather, stronger wind cutting gas-fired generation, moderated storage withdrawals.
  • Oil sentiment: the EIA expects global inventories to build in Q4 2026 and Middle East shut-ins have fallen from a 10.9m bpd May peak to 2.5m bpd in October. Oil relief helps sentiment, but it is not automatically the same percentage off UK NBP.
  • New actual supplier tariffs, showing whether Thursday's offered declines appear in executable quotes for each eligible contract.
Waiting is not neutral. Waiting might help if supply keeps recovering and winter stays mild; it might hurt if shipping losses bite, storage draws accelerate or supplier books reverse. Waiting only works if the market moves in the customer's favour. The decision belongs to the customer, made on full forecast costs, quote validity and risk tolerance, not on a headline.

British Gas 18-Month Window

Extended renewal window · eligibility checked per customer, never assumed

British Gas business customers can now review and agree renewal terms up to 18 months before their current contract ends, extended from the previous 12-month window. The future-start contract begins when the existing contract expires; agreeing it today does not change what the customer pays today.

Thursday's market context makes that window directly relevant: the offered curve has fallen across every supplied season while physical risk has deteriorated, a combination that historically does not persist quietly. A British Gas customer inside 18 months of contract end can test whether today's lower offered levels are available to them now, rather than discovering in month 12 that the window was used up waiting.

Eligibility is per product and per customer. Not every British Gas customer is automatically eligible, and eligibility must be confirmed against the actual account, meter profile, consumption and credit position before any figure is quoted. British Gas 18-month eligibility turns this market intelligence into a practical customer action.

Once a supplier withdraws a rate, the customer cannot go back and secure yesterday's price. Checking eligibility and live pricing costs nothing; assuming either costs real money when it is wrong.

Ceasefire Reality Check

Diplomacy is not delivery · the physical tests remain unmet

There is no ceasefire. This week produced direct US–Iran military exchanges, Iranian-linked attacks on two more Gulf states' airspace, coalition strikes across Yemen and two successful tanker attacks in three days. Whatever diplomatic channels exist, the physical direction of travel this week was escalation, not de-escalation.

The tests, honestly answered

  • Signed, operative agreement? No. Tuesday's Oman–Iran maritime discussions and earlier G7 language are diplomatic contact, not an enforceable agreement. Diplomatic contact is not a ceasefire, and a ceasefire announcement is not verified safe passage.
  • Reduced incident risk? No. UKMTO's log gained another entry on Wednesday. Iran's unverified claim of three more vessels on fire sits unresolved against it.
  • Safe, insured, repeated tanker and LNG transits? Not established. Preliminary November Strait oil flows near half the pre-war comparator say the market does not believe passage is normal, whatever diplomats say.
  • Measurable shipped tonnes recovering? Not for LNG: Qatar's force majeure runs to end-December and repair of damaged trains is measured in years. Oil has more rerouting options than LNG, but only current verified flows establish the net market effect.
  • Physical supply holding? Partly. Middle East shut-ins have fallen sharply since May (EIA), Saudi bypass routes are carrying barrels, and US LNG remains a major buffer. An improvement in security can reduce a risk premium before damaged infrastructure is repaired, but physical flows are the supply test.
Commercial translation: never assume price direction from the geopolitical narrative alone. Thursday's falling sheet during an escalating week is the proof. Price the quote, not the headline.

Supplier Price-Book Lag

Wholesale markets move first. Supplier books often follow.

This week is a live demonstration of the lag in both directions. Tuesday's sheet fell across the board; Wednesday's front W-26 rebounded +1.40% while the 2027 seasons kept falling and later seasons rose; Thursday's sheet fell everywhere. A supplier book repriced on any one of those days is already out of date on the next.

What Thursday's sheet proves: the offered books behind the Switch365 comparison did move down between Wednesday AM and Thursday AM, on every supplied row. That removes Wednesday's open question about whether seasonal books had repriced. What it does not prove is that any specific supplier will write that level for your customer's meter, start date, credit position and consumption profile this afternoon. Offered indicators and executable quotes are related, not identical.

What to watch for: named, dated supplier changes: withdrawals, repricing upward on the back of the security news, or longer-dated terms quietly disappearing. A book pulled “for repricing” on a falling curve day is often the first sign the floor is being defended. Equally, if Thursday's lower levels are still executable on Friday, the window is real.

  • Check quote validity timestamps, not just prices: a quote generated Wednesday afternoon may already exclude Thursday's move.
  • Compare full forecast bills: standing charges, pass-throughs, capacity and non-energy costs sit outside these wholesale indicators.
  • Once a supplier withdraws a rate, the customer cannot go back and secure yesterday's price. The market does not need to rise again tomorrow for customers to pay more tomorrow.
Supplier price-book lag is the opportunity and the danger. The lag that lets a customer catch a falling book is the same lag that lets a supplier pull it before the paperwork clears.
