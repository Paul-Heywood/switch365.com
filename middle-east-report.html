Hormuz Tightens as Houthi Grip Around Bab al-Mandeb Weakens

Saudi-backed Yemeni government forces have retaken strategically important territory around Bab al-Mandeb and advanced to Mocha, weakening the Houthis' recent grip on parts of the Red Sea coast. At the same time the Saudi, Turkish and Pakistani Makkah Alliance has moved to practical implementation of its collective-defence commitments. But the more immediate energy risk remains Hormuz, where the IRGC ordered an inbound tanker to turn back on Monday and another tanker was later struck by an unknown projectile, causing an engine-room fire.

The two chokepoints are moving in different directions: Houthi territorial leverage around Bab al-Mandeb may be falling, while direct interference with commercial navigation through Hormuz is increasing. Gulf crude and LNG flows remain surprisingly resilient, so the key UK gas question is whether worsening Hormuz security begins to reverse the recent improvement in physical LNG movement.

SECTION 01

Summary

Saudi-backed Yemeni government forces staged a lightning advance on Monday to retake the coast around the Bab el-Mandeb Strait up to Mocha, with the Defence Ministry saying the city had fallen and Houthi fighters retreating into the highlands, Reuters reported. Government forces had already taken key positions including an airstrip in the Dhubab district at the mouth of the strait, and Saudi Arabia said it attacked hundreds of targets to provide air cover. Reuters could not independently verify the full extent of the gains, and the Houthis called the claims baseless. If confirmed, the advance loosens the Houthi grip on a vital Red Sea route, though the group could still strike vessels with drones from other positions. Territorial leverage and physical shipping are different measures.

The Makkah Alliance has moved from political coordination towards the practical implementation of collective-defence commitments, including agreed military forces and capabilities for rapid deployment inside Saudi Arabia. Saudi Arabia, Türkiye and Pakistan agreed the move at an emergency Riyadh meeting on 5 October and appointed Pakistan's Lieutenant General Nauman Mahmood (Ret.) as the pact's first Secretary-General, according to the joint statement reported by Al Jazeera. Practical implementation is more than political support, but it is not automatically offensive participation: the stated deployment is into the Kingdom, and no Turkish or Pakistani forces have been confirmed in combat in Yemen.

Hormuz, though, is where the direct physical risk to UK energy sits. On Monday the UKMTO reported the IRGC hailing an inbound tanker 11 nautical miles north of Khasab and ordering it to turn back or be targeted; the master complied. Hours later a second tanker, identified by maritime security sources as the Liberia-flagged LIPSI, was struck by an unknown projectile, causing an engine-room fire. Qatar LNG cargoes are still getting through, but UK gas and power benchmarks rose again on Tuesday morning, and the offered ranges below show what that means for the price a customer can actually secure today.

How to read today's gas curve

Every contract on the offered range rose between Friday morning and Monday morning, led by the front of the curve: W-26 reference +2.30p, S-27 +6.00p and W-27 +5.50p. That pattern is consistent with a market pricing worsening Hormuz security faster than it is crediting improving LNG flow. The public benchmark confirms the direction: the NBP benchmark traded at 187.30p/therm on Tuesday morning, up 2.02% on the day, and TTF reached €75.42/MWh, up 2.59%.

The curve remains heavily backwardated. S-27 at 138.00p is 26.2% below the W-26 reference, and S-28 at 87.00p is 53.5% below it. Backwardation does not mean wait: it means today's forward price for those delivery periods can be secured now, while it is still offered. Every season from S-27 to W-30 is also priced above its 1 Year Ago line, so the whole curve is dearer than the same delivery periods were priced last year, even though the far curve is much cheaper than the front.

Fundamentals are pulling both ways. Bearish: Qatar LNG cargoes are moving, Norway is strong, US LNG is at record levels and Gulf crude has recovered. Bullish: direct IRGC interference with commercial navigation, at least seven tanker attacks in the first week of October, storage weak for the season, and the winter risk premium rebuilding. LNG remains the bridge between Hormuz and UK gas, and supplier books are the final step before the customer's price.

Power is its own market today

Gas matters to UK electricity, but generation conditions determine how strongly. Today's specific power driver is wind: Great Britain's wind fleet is forecast to produce only around 1,169 MW late this morning, roughly 8.1% of 14,488 MW installed capacity, against 7,225 MW forecast for Monday, according to OpenWindCast's day-ahead forecast (NESO's own forecast: 1,360 MW). A lull that deep forces gas-fired plant to set the marginal price through the day, and market commentators noted on Monday that day-ahead power was already being offered higher as the wind dropped.

The offered range reflects that: every power season rose between Friday and Monday morning, led by the W-26 reference at +£5.26 and W-29 at +£1.96. Power backwardation is steep as well: S-27 at £110.00 sits 29.0% below the W-26 reference, and S-28 at £74.00 is 52.3% below it.

Watch items for power this week: actual wind outturn versus forecast, CCGT response and day-ahead baseload, French nuclear availability into winter, and interconnector flows. Capacity is not flow: interconnectors only help the UK margin when power actually moves across them.

SECTION 10

Top Five Warning Signs

1. IRGC turn-backs become routine

One enforced turn-back is an incident. Repetition would mean physical restriction growing beyond isolated interference, with schedule, insurance and freight consequences even where no shot is fired.

2. LNG cargoes begin to fall again

October Qatari volumes were tracking towards roughly 25% of pre-war levels. Any reversal is the most direct UK gas consequence available in this crisis. Watch Kpler and LSEG tracking, not statements.

3. Yemen offensive stalls or a Houthi counterattack regains Bab positions

The Houthis are advancing near Taiz while retreating on the coast. If the coastal gains reverse, second-chokepoint risk rises again and Red Sea insurance pricing follows.

4. Verified Saudi energy infrastructure loss

Houthi claims on Rabigh and Riyadh remain unverified. An attack claim is not verified capacity loss. Confirmation of real damage at a refinery or export route would be an actual supply consequence, not a headline.

5. NBP / TTF rise and suppliers pull books

The direct customer consequence. With the NBP benchmark at 187.30p and the internal W-26 reference at 187.00p, the market sits in the zone where Switch365 has previously seen books withdrawn, suspended or returned higher.

SECTION 14

Ceasefire Reality Check

There is no ceasefire to check this morning: direct military action is expanding in Yemen, Hormuz interference is intensifying, and diplomacy has produced contacts rather than agreements. A pause in bombing is not the same as normalisation of the energy system, and route normalisation matters more than political optimism.

  • Direct military action: expanding. Saudi air campaign, government ground offensive, coalition naval operation in Hodeidah, Houthi counterattacks near Taiz.
  • Hormuz: more restrictive. Turn-back enforcement plus a fresh projectile strike in the same day.
  • LNG: still moving. The bearish buffer survives, but each transit now runs a gauntlet the market is repricing.
  • Yemen / Bab al-Mandeb: geography improving for the government, shipping not yet normalised.
  • Saudi infrastructure: under claimed attack, no verified capacity loss. Attack claim is not verified capacity loss.
  • Makkah Alliance: deeper military alignment cuts both ways: deterrence up, escalation ceiling up.
  • Diplomacy: contacts, not talks, not agreement, not implementation.
The evidence may support planning for prolonged disruption, but it does not tell us exactly when political pressure will finally produce an agreement. A shorter period of bombing would not necessarily mean a shorter energy crisis. Physical flows can recover before the political conflict ends.

Shipping Risk

Hormuz Restricted

  • IRGC turn-back: inbound tanker hailed 11 NM north of Khasab on 5 Oct, ordered to turn back or be targeted; the master complied. Direct interference with commercial navigation, stronger evidence than threats or warnings.
  • Tanker strikes: LIPSI (Liberia-flagged, Dynacom-managed) struck by an unknown projectile at 1637 UTC on 5 Oct, engine-room fire, crew safe, no environmental impact reported. Time-late reports added an LPG tanker and two crude tankers struck on 3-4 Oct. UKMTO's weekly overview counted 48 projectile strikes since early July, 25 of them damaging engine rooms.
  • Crude flow: still moving at scale. The seven-day average for Middle Eastern crude exports stood at about 18.5 million b/d on 1 Oct, with shuttle convoys to the Fujairah anchorage having largely restored net Gulf exports to pre-war volumes by end-September on satellite-based counts.
  • Insurance and freight: war-risk premiums surging, crews harder to recruit, some owners avoiding Hormuz entirely on longer routes. A route can remain physically open and still carry a material war-risk premium.
  • Dark traffic: multiple vessels transiting with AIS disabled. Visible vessel count and physical energy volume are different measures.

Qatar LNG Moving, exposed

  • Four LNG vessels carrying Qatari cargoes reappeared outside Hormuz around 2-3 Oct: Bu Samra, Al Gattara (signalling Zhejiang, China), Al Sadd (delivered Dahej, India, 3 Oct) and Al Kharsaah. A fifth, Al Marrouna, reappeared in ballast inside the strait on 2 Oct.
  • S&P Global Energy estimates October volumes could reach roughly 25% of pre-war levels if the recent trend holds; pre-war the route carried about three cargoes a day.
  • Ras Laffan is operating with reduced capacity after the March missile attack took out about 17% of Qatar's annual export capacity for up to five years; allocations have been indicated around 50% of contracted quantities.
  • No force majeure or cancelled-cargo announcements from QatarEnergy in this research run.
  • One Qatar-linked cargo getting through is evidence of resilience, not evidence of normalisation. Equally, consistent multi-cargo movement is a genuine bearish physical improvement, and we report both.

Bab al-Mandeb Geography improving

  • Government forces retook the Dhubab airstrip and advanced to Mocha; Houthis retreating to the highlands on government accounts. Perim and Hanish status not independently re-verified this morning.
  • A tanker 60 NM south of Al Mukha observed explosions close by on 4 Oct; crew safe. The strait is still a live threat zone even as territory changes hands.
  • Do not read the government advance as Bab al-Mandeb being safe: the Houthis can still strike vessels with drones from other positions.

Saudi Red Sea and workarounds Watch

  • East-West Pipeline operating after its September shutdown and restart; Yanbu loading resumed. Current Yanbu inventory not verified in this run.
  • Houthi claims against the Rabigh refinery put Red Sea coast infrastructure back in the frame; no verified damage at the time of writing.
  • Workarounds: UAE eastern terminals and Fujairah anchorage are carrying the shuttle system; Suez can help, but it is not a magic fix, and fully laden VLCCs are not a simple Suez solution; SUMED, STS and the Cape of Good Hope remain the fallback stack.
  • If Hormuz is the problem, Saudi and Red Sea infrastructure are part of the workaround. Pressure on the workaround matters even before the workaround stops working. Workarounds show resilience and disruption at the same time.
Hormuz is not open as usual. Friction gets priced. The commercially important question is no longer how many attacks occurred; it is whether security incidents begin to reduce physical flow.

SECTION 17

Supplier Price-Book Lag

Wholesale has moved. Have supplier books caught up?

The wholesale chain has repriced since Friday morning: the internal offered range shows gas up across every contract (W-26 reference +2.30p, S-27 +6.00p, W-27 +5.50p) and power up across every contract (W-26 reference +£5.26, W-27 +£3.00). Public benchmarks confirmed the move on Tuesday morning, with NBP at 187.30p and TTF at €75.42.

Switch365 internal commercial evidence: when wholesale gas previously reached around 180p/therm, some suppliers withdrew books, suspended pricing or returned higher. That is broker experience, not an industry threshold, but the market is now sitting in that zone.

  • Wholesale markets move first. Supplier books often follow.
  • Supplier price-book lag is the opportunity and the danger.
  • Once a supplier withdraws a rate, the customer cannot go back and secure yesterday's price.
  • The market does not need to rise again tomorrow for customers to pay more tomorrow.
  • Wholesale has already moved. The question now is when supplier books catch up.
Chain: wholesale moves → supplier book may remain temporarily unchanged → supplier catches up → rate withdrawn or repriced → customer loses previous price. A valid quote issued before this move is a live asset today.

Waiting is not neutral

Waiting can help if Hormuz interference reduces, LNG keeps improving, Bab risk falls, Norway and US LNG stay strong and suppliers reduce. Waiting can hurt if turn-backs increase, tanker attacks cut flows, Saudi infrastructure is damaged, the supplier book rises or a quote expires. Waiting only works if the market moves in the customer's favour.

The physical picture is moving in both directions. Houthi territorial leverage around Bab al-Mandeb may be falling, but commercial interference through Hormuz has intensified. Customer decisions should therefore be based on the live supplier price available today rather than assuming either automatic escalation or automatic normalisation.
