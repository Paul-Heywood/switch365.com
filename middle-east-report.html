THE MARKET DOES NOT NEED TO KNOW HOW THE WAR ENDS BEFORE IT PRICES HOW LONG THE DISRUPTION MAY LAST.

01Summary

THE QUICK-RESOLUTION CASE IS WEAKENING. The important change is duration.

The central energy-market issue is increasingly duration. The latest US-Iran peace framework has expired without a permanent settlement, both sides remain entrenched, Washington is preparing what the Treasury describes as its toughest Iran sanctions package yet and the US naval blockade remains in place. Meanwhile commercial traffic through Hormuz has fallen sharply again, with the latest Kpler data showing only seven tracked commodity vessels on Thursday and no tracked LNG tankers or VLCC crude carriers. Bab el-Mandeb traffic also weakened. Oil remains above $90 and commercial operators continue to build expensive workarounds around the conflict rather than returning to normal routes. This does not prove the war will last for years. It does mean the evidence for businesses simply waiting a few weeks for normality is becoming weaker.

For UK businesses, the commercial question is not whether we can predict the end of the war. It is whether today's gas or electricity price for their actual future delivery period provides acceptable value before prolonged disruption is absorbed further into wholesale and supplier pricing.

The evidence for a quick resolution is getting weaker. The market is increasingly having to treat Hormuz disruption as a prolonged operating condition rather than a short-lived shock. The conflict does not need to last for years for today's forward energy pricing to move. Suppliers do not need certainty about the final duration before repricing the probability of prolonged disruption.

  • For 2026 renewals, the time available to wait is shrinking while duration risk is rising.
For customers whose contracts end in 2026, the risk is becoming increasingly asymmetric. If prices fall, they may benefit from waiting. But every week they wait gives them less time to recover if prices rise. Waiting is not neutral.

05Futures Market Explained

The futures market shows today's price for energy delivered in a future period. A 2028 customer does not need to wait until 2028 to see what 2028 energy costs. If suppliers are prepared to sell that future period today, today's forward price is actionable.

What the curve is saying

The internal curve is in steep backwardation: W-26 gas at 160.50 p/therm versus S-29 at 62.50. The front of the curve carries today's geopolitical premium; the back of the curve carries less of it. Backwardation does not mean wait.

Why duration matters here

A prolonged conflict matters because today's future period can absorb risk long before it becomes the customer's near-term market. Suppliers do not need certainty about the final duration before repricing the probability of prolonged disruption.

The customer mistake

The customer mistake is assuming a cheaper future period means they should wait. It means we should check whether that cheaper future delivery period can be secured now. Cheaper further out does not mean cheaper later.

The 1 Year Ago test

The 1 Year Ago line shows how much the market has moved, but the latest line is the customer's decision point. Every internal season is now priced above its year-ago level, so part of the forward-value opportunity has already been removed.

08British Gas 18-Month Window

British Gas has extended its business energy renewal window from 12 months to 18 months. Internal broker / supplier evidence

What it means

The 18-month British Gas window matters because it turns future-market intelligence into practical action. Customers whose contracts end within 18 months may be able to review and secure future-start pricing today rather than waiting for the old 12-month window to open.

How the start date works

Your new contract would not start today. It begins when the existing contract ends. Securing a future-start price now locks today's forward level for a delivery period that begins later.

Why it matters now

Waiting is not neutral. Customers cannot go back later and buy a withdrawn price. With the internal curve in steep backwardation, the 18-month window lets eligible customers test today's cheaper future delivery periods while they still exist.

The check to run

Cheaper further out does not mean cheaper later. It means check whether that future delivery can be secured now. British Gas 18-month eligibility turns this market intelligence into a practical customer action. Not every customer is automatically eligible; eligibility must be checked against the account.

2. US Sanctions Escalation · MIXED

Fact: Bessent promised the "toughest sanctions in history" with details Monday, paired with the naval blockade. Why it matters: a bullish duration signal, but Washington argues maximum economic pressure reduces the likelihood of a large-scale kinetic restart, a genuinely bearish immediate-war nuance. UK impact: sustained pressure supports the risk premium even if near-term escalation odds fall.

3. Hormuz Commercial Impairment · BULLISH

Fact: tracked commodity transits fell from 14 to 7 on Thursday; no tracked LNG tanker or VLCC. Why it matters: the composition of traffic matters as much as the headline count. UK impact: LNG replacement cost is the transmission line into NBP and supplier gas pricing.

4. Expired Peace Framework · BULLISH

Fact: the June MoU lapsed without extension or permanent deal; no formal talks are currently confirmed. Why it matters: it removed the timetable for quick resolution. UK impact: weakens the case for simply waiting a few weeks for normality.

5. Qatar LNG Force Majeure · BULLISH

Fact: force majeure has run since March; Edison has 24 cargoes (about 3 bcm) affected through end-September; Ras Laffan trains damaged. Why it matters: Qatar is the world's second-largest LNG exporter and Hormuz is its only sea route. UK impact: replacement buying lifts the global LNG floor that UK gas competes within.

6. UAE-Iran Economic Rupture · BULLISH

Fact: the UAE suspended all trade, commercial exchanges and financial transactions with Iran on 18 August. Why it matters: the UAE has historically been one of Iran's most important commercial channels; the confrontation is broadening into trade and finance. UK impact: another duration signal.

7. Bab el-Mandeb · MIXED

Fact: traffic fell to 23 tracked commodity vessels Thursday from 34, but the strait remains operational; two Suezmax crude tankers exited. Why it matters: both key chokepoints weakening simultaneously is a stronger route-risk signal than either in isolation. UK impact: the alternative route is not fully replacing the impaired primary route.

8. Workarounds & Saudi Flows · BEARISH COUNTERWEIGHT

Fact: the East-West pipeline to Yanbu is running at full capacity and SUMED interest is rising; some detours add roughly 30-40 days and, in Reuters examples, around $2.5 million per voyage. Why it matters: workarounds reduce the probability of total physical supply loss. UK impact: friction gets priced: longer voyages tie up tankers and reduce effective availability.

9. Oil Price · MIXED

Fact: Brent ~$93.5/bbl, WTI ~$86, on course for a second weekly rise. Why it matters: the market is not treating the conflict as a one-session spike. UK impact: oil is context; the customer decision runs through NBP, TTF, UK power and supplier books.

10. Storage, Norway & French Nuclear · BEARISH COUNTERWEIGHT

Fact: EU storage 61.6% full; Norwegian seasonal maintenance is the watch item; French nuclear is recovering from heat and jellyfish curbs. Why it matters: buffers soften, but do not remove, prolonged disruption risk. UK impact: these are the reasons not every session rises; they cap, rather than cancel, duration risk.

Permanent settlement?

No. The June framework expired without one. No permanent settlement exists.

Formal negotiations?

Not confirmed. Public negotiations remain stalled; the IRGC denies any backchannel. Private diplomacy cannot be ruled out, but it has not produced physical normalisation.

Blockade lifted?

No. The US naval blockade remains in place and is being paired with a tougher sanctions package.

Hormuz normal?

No. Seven tracked commodity vessels on Thursday against a pre-war flow carrying roughly a fifth of traded oil and LNG.

LNG / VLCC normal?

No. No tracked LNG tanker or VLCC crossed Hormuz on Thursday; none crossed Bab el-Mandeb either.

Qatar force majeure ended?

No. Declarations continue; Edison has cargoes affected through end-September.

Red Sea normal?

Sanctions easing?

15Shipping Risk

Strait of Hormuz

  • Thursday 20 Aug: 7 tracked commodity vessels (4 entered, 3 exited), half of Wednesday's 14. Reuters / Kpler
  • No tracked LNG tanker crossed. No tracked VLCC crossed. One very large gas carrier carrying propane and butane exited via the Iranian route; an LPG carrier is not the same as an LNG tanker.
  • Kpler's 60-day review shows the dark / unknown share of crossings closing the window above 80%: AIS-dark vessels are not captured in these counts.
  • The composition of Hormuz traffic matters as much as the headline vessel count. A few smaller commodity vessels crossing does not demonstrate normal crude and LNG export capacity.
  • No tracked VLCC does not prove zero crude left the Gulf. But it is powerful evidence that normal large-scale crude tanker traffic has not returned.

Bab el-Mandeb

  • Thursday 20 Aug: 23 tracked commodity vessels (16 entered, 7 exited) versus 34 on each of the previous two days. Reuters / Kpler
  • Two Suezmax crude tankers (Stoic Warrior, Dokos) exited bound for Vietnam and India; no tracked VLCC or LNG tanker.
  • Bab el-Mandeb remains operational, but traffic also deteriorated Thursday. Both key chokepoints weakening simultaneously is a stronger route-risk signal than either in isolation.

Workarounds

  • Saudi East-West pipeline (Petroline) to Yanbu: running at or near full capacity (about 7 mb/d); the largest Hormuz bypass. It feeds the Red Sea, which still means Bab el-Mandeb risk.
  • SUMED / Sidi Kerir: rising interest; Ain Sukhna receipts ran near 1.6 mb/d in July with Iraqi crude at multi-year highs.
  • Cape of Good Hope: avoids the Red Sea entirely at the cost of a much longer voyage.
  • Fujairah / Oman ship-to-ship transfers: shuttle tankers move crude to transfer points outside the Gulf so VLCCs avoid the highest-risk area.
  • Replacement supply: buyers sourcing crude from the US, Africa and the Atlantic Basin, and LNG from the US, Oman, Nigeria and Angola.
  • Reuters examples show some Saudi-to-Asia diversions stretching from roughly 20 days to around 50-60 days, with additional costs around $2.5 million on some detoured voyages; Oman-China tanker freight has been reported near $140,000 per day during the disruption. Not every voyage carries exactly these costs.
Hormuz is not open as usual. Bab el-Mandeb is not a clean alternative. Workarounds prove supply can move; they also prove normal logistics are not available. An extra month at sea and millions of dollars of extra cost is not normal trade. Friction gets priced.

17Supplier Price-Book Lag

Supplier price-book lag is the opportunity and the danger.

Wholesale markets move first. Supplier books often follow. That lag is why a customer can sometimes still secure a forward delivery period close to yesterday's level after the wholesale curve has already moved. It is also why waiting for certainty can mean missing the price movement entirely. Once a supplier withdraws a rate, the customer cannot go back and secure yesterday's pricing.

What suppliers actually price

Suppliers do not need the conflict to officially last until 2027 before repricing duration risk. They do not need Hormuz to be literally sealed. They do not need every Qatari LNG cargo to disappear. They price probability. They price volatility. They price freight. They price replacement cost. They price collateral. They price liquidity. They price duration.

Today's lag risk

The strongest risk today may not be another missile. It may be the market deciding that disruption is going to last longer than previously assumed. Thursday's evidence (Hormuz at 7 tracked vessels, sanctions coming Monday, no talks) is exactly the kind of input that moves a price book on a lag, not a headline.

Wholesale moved first. Supplier books may still follow. For a customer with a 2026 end date, the lag is a closing window: the time available to wait is shrinking at exactly the point that duration risk is rising.

18Customer Action

The evidence that the Middle East energy crisis will resolve quickly is getting weaker. The sensible action is to review what your next contract can be secured at today and then decide whether continuing to wait remains commercially justified.

1. Contract ends in 2026 · Immediate Review

This is the priority group. For customers ending this year, waiting is becoming a more concentrated gamble. The time available to wait is getting shorter while the evidence of a quick resolution is getting weaker. If prices fall, waiting may help. If prices rise, they have progressively less time to recover. Every week that passes reduces their flexibility. Review live options and decide whether the remaining potential downside is worth the increasing duration and timing risk. THE TIME AVAILABLE TO WAIT IS SHRINKING WHILE THE DURATION RISK IS RISING.

2. Inside 12 months · Do Not Wait for Renewal Panic

Review the current market now against the actual contract end date. Check today's future-start pricing rather than waiting for the renewal letter. Avoid renewal panic: the goal is a calm comparison of live options while choice still exists.

3. British Gas inside 18 months · Extended Renewal Window

Check eligibility under the extended 18-month window and review future-start pricing. The new contract begins when the existing one ends, so acting early does not mean paying early. Not every customer is automatically eligible; check the account.

4. High usage outside 12 months · Look Further Forward

Test supplier forward-start availability. High-consumption sites feel duration risk hardest; where suppliers will sell 2027 or 2028 delivery today, that forward price is actionable intelligence, not just information.

5. 2027 / 2028 / 2029 / 2030 CED · Your Delivery Period Matters

Compare the actual delivery period against W-26 and 1 Year Ago. Internal gas for 2028-2030 delivery sits 50-61% below W-26; power 42-52% below. Check backwardation, then check whether that future delivery period can be secured now. The future market is only useful while it can still be bought.

Waiting is not neutral. Cheaper further out does not mean cheaper later. Backwardation does not mean wait. This is a review-now window, not a wait-and-see market.
