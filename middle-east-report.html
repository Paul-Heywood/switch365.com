Futures Market Explained

The futures market shows the price for energy delivered in future periods. A customer renewing from 2027, 2028, 2029 or 2030 is not only interested in today’s price. They should look at what the market is currently offering for their actual delivery period. On the supplied curves, the difference is stark: W-26 gas is offered at 143.00p, while S-29 gas is offered at 62.00p — a -56.6% gap. W-26 power is £122.00/MWh while S-29 power is £63.50/MWh — a -48.0% gap.

  • Cheaper further out does not mean cheaper later. The future market is only useful while the customer can still buy that future delivery period.
  • Waiting lets the market make the decision for the customer. As time passes, that future period becomes the near-term market and can absorb the same risk premium.
  • The 1 Year Ago line is context. The latest line is the customer’s decision point.

British Gas has extended its business energy renewal window from 12 months to 18 months. This gives eligible customers an earlier chance to review future-start pricing. This is verified user-provided internal evidence and is treated as live unless the user says it has changed.

  • The 18-month British Gas window matters because it turns the forward market into a practical customer action. A customer whose contract ends in 2027 can now be inside a live renewal window while S-27 gas sits -35.0% and S-27 power -32.0% vs W-26.
  • Your new contract would not start today. It would begin when your current agreement ends.
  • Waiting is not neutral. If the market moves or British Gas withdraws rates, customers cannot go back and secure today’s pricing later.
  • Cheaper further out does not mean cheaper later. It means check whether the future period can be secured now.

Hormuz was expected to open as usual — verified traffic does not show normalisation

Cargo vessel reported struck near Hormuz

UKMTO Warning 104-26 classifies the Al Khasab incident as an attack — the third security event in the same waters since Saturday, after the 1 August tanker strike near Limah and the 2 August explosion near Khasab.

3

Trump’s “last chance” wording raises the cost of failure

“Every last chance before decapitation” and “last chance to sign a good document” mean failed talks point back toward the “biggest attacks since World War II” he says he called off. Talks being back in the headline is positive, but it is not the same as a signed deal.

Iran denies direct US talks — and says Hormuz will never return to pre-war status

Kataib Hezbollah’s 6 August deadline creates countdown risk

The Iraq countdown risk is still live. The group calls a response to the US-Saudi strikes “inevitable” and has put American “tools” in Saudi Arabia in scope. Things are spreading out geographically, and that is exactly the type of risk suppliers can price quickly.

  • Lower oil does not remove the warning signs. Monday’s selloff reversed in a single session.

  • Today’s prices are higher than 1 Year Ago across the key front periods — the market has already repriced risk once this year.

Ceasefire Reality Check

The June memorandum of understanding is now the centre of a factual dispute: Washington says it required Iran to open the waterway; Tehran says the text explicitly preserved its authority. Talks being alive is better than collapse, but no breakthrough is not implementation. Oil can move on mediation headlines, but shipping confidence is what matters for supplier pricing.

  • Talks are in the headline, but that does not mean the risk is off.
  • Fresh diplomacy has created relief pricing, but Hormuz and wider route confidence have not fully normalised.
  • Trump’s last-chance language raises the consequence of talks failing.
  • Iran denying direct US talks weakens the clean de-escalation story.
  • Kataib Hezbollah’s 6 August deadline means the market still has to price countdown risk in Iraq.
  • Do not confuse diplomacy being discussed with de-escalation being delivered. Route normalisation matters more than political optimism.

Shipping Risk

Strait of Hormuz

  • Hormuz is not shut, but it is not open as usual. Six transits Monday — three tankers, three bulk carriers; five entries, one exit; all via the Iranian-designated route requiring permission and service fees. Down from seven Sunday; nine on Sunday per Kpler including sanctioned and shadow-fleet vessels.
  • Hormuz may be moving, but moving is not the same as normal. Before the war the strait carried roughly one-fifth of global crude oil and LNG shipments; Barclays estimates net exports averaged 4.2m b/d last week versus 3.2m b/d the week before — improved, not normalised.
  • One LNG tanker moving does not mean LNG movement has normalised. Hormuz remains the biggest structural risk for LNG confidence.
  • UKMTO Warning 104-26 (attack): cargo vessel struck by an unknown projectile 20nm NE of Al Khasab at 2200 UTC Monday; VHF Channel 16 report; no claim of responsibility; damage and casualties unconfirmed.
  • Weekend incidents: 1 Aug — tanker struck 11nm NE of Limah, engine-room damage, vessel not under command, no casualties; 2 Aug — master reported explosion close to a tanker 20nm NE of Khasab, vessel and crew safe.
  • US blockade active: CENTCOM reports 44 vessels redirected, two disabled, two boarded.

Bab el-Mandeb · Red Sea · Suez

  • Bab el-Mandeb is not fully closed, but it is still a route-risk market. Twenty transits Monday — 12 tankers, eight bulk carriers, mostly with transponders on — steady day-on-day but down from 41 on 31 July.
  • The Red Sea route is open, but it is not clean. Six Saudi-flagged supertankers changed course in the Gulf of Aden for southern Africa; two tankers laden with Saudi crude did cross Bab el-Mandeb.
  • Suez can help, but it is not a magic fix. Fully laden VLCCs are not a simple Suez solution. The workaround adds friction, and friction gets priced — longer voyages, higher insurance, occasional diversions (KCM Trade).
  • Egypt / Damietta: a drone attack set fires on the Energos Winter FSRU (~450 MMcf/d regas capacity, ~7% of Egypt’s daily gas consumption) and the Gaslog Salem. Unclaimed; Houthis deny responsibility. Attribution must be treated carefully unless a responsible actor is confirmed.
  • SUMED / Yanbu remain the Saudi bypass conversation — no new verified development in this window.
  • Kazakhstan / CPC / Black Sea: no fresh development verified in this research run — secondary priority today.
  • War-risk insurance and freight remain elevated across both chokepoints; Kpler advises operators to treat both as “contested rather than normalised”.

Supplier Price-Book Lag

  • Supplier price-book lag is the opportunity and the danger.
  • Wholesale markets move first. Supplier books often follow. Waiting for certainty can mean missing the price movement.
  • Once a supplier withdraws a rate, the customer cannot go back and buy yesterday’s price.
  • Even if oil has fallen from the highs, supplier books may still react to the move that has already happened.
  • Suppliers do not need a full closure of Hormuz or Bab el-Mandeb before they reprice. They also do not need diplomacy to fail completely before they price renewed conflict risk.
  • When conflict spreads geographically — Iraq, Saudi Arabia, Egypt — suppliers can price the increased probability of disruption before physical closure happens.
  • 1 Year Ago prices are useful context, but they do not protect the customer today.
