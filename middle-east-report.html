Futures Market Explained

The futures market shows the price for energy delivered in future periods. A customer renewing from 2027, 2028, 2029 or 2030 is not only interested in today's price. They should look at what the market is currently offering for their actual delivery period.

Key Principles

  • Cheaper further out does not mean cheaper later.
  • The future market is only useful while the customer can still buy that future delivery period.
  • Waiting lets the market make the decision for the customer.
  • As time passes, that future period becomes the near-term market and can absorb the same risk premium.
  • The 1 Year Ago line is context. The latest line is the customer's decision point.

What Backwardation Means

  • When future delivery periods trade below the front period (W-26), the curve is backwardated.
  • This creates a forward-value opportunity for customers with later contract end dates.
  • Backwardation can disappear quickly if the front period falls or future periods rise.
  • Do not confuse a backwardated curve with a guarantee that prices will stay low.
  • The % vs W-26 column quantifies the gap. Use it to guide customer conversations.

British Gas 18-Month Window

British Gas has extended its business energy renewal window from 12 months to 18 months. This gives eligible customers an earlier chance to review future-start pricing. This is verified internal evidence and should be treated as live.

Why It Matters
The 18-month British Gas window matters because it turns the forward market into a practical customer action. Your new contract would not start today. It would begin when your current agreement ends.
The Risk
Waiting is not neutral. If the market moves or British Gas withdraws rates, customers cannot go back and secure today's pricing later. Cheaper further out does not mean cheaper later. It means check whether the future period can be secured now.
Action: Check British Gas eligibility for any customer inside 18 months of contract end. The forward-value gap on S-27, S-28 and S-29 may be accessible through this extended window. Once the window closes or rates reprice, the opportunity is gone.

Supplier Price-Book Lag

Supplier price-book lag is the opportunity and the danger.

  • Wholesale markets move first. Supplier books often follow.
  • Waiting for certainty can mean missing the price movement.
  • Once a supplier withdraws a rate, the customer cannot go back and buy yesterday's price.
  • Even if oil has fallen from the highs, supplier books may still react to the move that has already happened.
  • Even if oil rebounds, supplier books may react faster than customers expect.
  • Suppliers do not need a full closure of Hormuz or Bab el-Mandeb before they reprice.
  • Suppliers also do not need diplomacy to fail completely before they price renewed conflict risk.
  • When conflict spreads geographically, suppliers can price the increased probability of disruption before physical closure happens.
  • 1 Year Ago prices are useful context, but they do not protect the customer today.
The Lag Rule: The gap between wholesale movement and supplier book reaction is where the best pricing lives. It is also where the most risk lives. Customers who wait for supplier books to fully reprice upwards have already missed the move. Customers who act while the lag exists secure better rates. The British Gas 18-month window is one practical way to turn this lag into a customer win.

Final Commercial Takeaway

Hormuz is shifting from closure risk to conditional-corridor risk. A managed corridor is not the same as open-as-usual shipping. Restrictions, permissions, fees or fines are not normalisation. Oil has moved back up because the market is questioning whether reopening would be clean. The US wants no tolls, no permissions and no charges. Iran's proposed hostile-vessel restrictions could keep war-risk premium in the market. The 6 August Kataib Hezbollah deadline has passed, but the risk has not disappeared. No verified response is not the same as risk removed. The risk has moved from countdown risk to unresolved retaliation risk. Iraq is still a retaliation risk, not just a place where strikes happened. Customers should not read lower oil, higher oil or a single daily move as the full business-energy story. The energy bill conversation is about NBP, TTF, UK power, supplier books, contract end date and forward delivery period. The 1 Year Ago line shows how far the curve has moved. The % vs W-26 column shows the forward-value gap. A negative % vs W-26 means that future delivery period is currently cheaper than the W-26 front period. Cheaper further out does not mean cheaper later. The British Gas 18-month renewal window matters because it turns forward-market value into a practical customer action. Supplier price-book lag is the opportunity and the danger. Suppliers do not need a full closure before repricing. Customers waiting because diplomacy is back in the headline are missing the point. This is a review-now window, not a wait-and-see market.
