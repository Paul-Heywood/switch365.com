Iran: "Era of Proportionate Responses Is Over" as Commercial Tankers Enter the Firing Line

Iran says future retaliation will be faster, heavier and more painful after direct US and Iranian attacks involving oil tankers over the weekend. Hormuz traffic has fallen to its lowest sustained level since May, Tehran is preparing a new restricted zone and the Houthi conflict is simultaneously raising risk around Bab al-Mandab. UK gas has opened the week higher, and Monday's immediate commercial question is how much of that new risk is now being reflected in supplier price books.

Research window: Friday 4 September to Monday 7 September 2026 (Europe/London). Internal pricing snapshots: Thursday 3 September AM versus Friday 4 September AM. Public market data timestamped Monday 7 September 2026, morning run.

01Summary

What changed over the weekend that could change the price a UK business can actually secure today.

Iranian Parliament Speaker Mohammad Bagher Qalibaf declared on Sunday that "the era of proportionate responses has come to an end", warning that any further attack on Iranian interests will receive a "faster, heavier and more painful" response and that recent strikes were "only the beginning". Iran is signalling that it no longer intends to constrain future retaliation to what it considers proportionate responses. That raises the escalation tail-risk around every subsequent US-Iran exchange, though it does not by itself prove that the next strike will be larger or that Hormuz will close completely.

The statement matters because of what happened before it. On Saturday, US Central Command said US forces struck three Iranian oil tankers, one of them off Kharg Island, Iran's principal crude export hub, after the IRGC launched ballistic missiles at two US Navy warships (both evaded, no US personnel harmed). The IRGC Navy said it retaliated by targeting three tankers on routes Tehran considered unauthorised, and claimed further attacks on US-linked vessels that Washington has not confirmed. Maritime intelligence firm Marisks described the weekend as a major escalation because the distinction between military confrontation and commercial shipping has materially weakened. Kpler data shows only two commodity vessels transited Hormuz on Saturday and six on Sunday, with the 10-day average down to ten per day, the lowest since May, and no VLCC has exited the Strait since Wednesday.

UK gas has answered the weekend on Monday morning: NBP traded above 183 pence per therm, its highest level in more than three and a half years and around 2.7% above Friday's reference. Physical risk has risen and gas is adding risk premium. Wholesale has already moved; the question now is when supplier books catch up. Friday's supplier prices were produced before the weekend escalation, so the immediate commercial test is which books are still being held this morning and which are pulled or repriced.

The Weekend in 30 Seconds

  • Iran says the era of proportionate responses is over and threatens faster, heavier and more painful retaliation. Confirmed statement
  • The US struck three Iranian oil tankers on Saturday (Downy, Stark I and Kylo/Noxen, per maritime intelligence); Iran says it targeted three tankers using unauthorised Hormuz routes. US and Iranian claims remain separately attributed.
  • Hormuz traffic fell to two vessels on Saturday and six on Sunday; the 10-day average of ten per day is the lowest since May. No VLCC has exited since Wednesday.
  • Iran says a new restricted zone beyond the Strait will be announced in the coming days, with entering vessels added to its sanctions list; the blacklist already stands at 56 vessels after 11 were added.
  • Yemen's Houthi conflict intensified sharply towards Mokha and the approaches to Bab al-Mandab, with more than 300 reported killed since Thursday (military and medical source tallies, not independently verified).
  • Brent is around $97 per barrel (roughly +10% last week), UK gas opened above 183p/therm, and the Monday NBP and supplier-book reaction is now the immediate commercial test.

What It Means for UK Energy

Oil headlines are dramatic, but LNG remains the bridge between Hormuz and UK gas. Qatar LNG is still severely constrained (18 cargoes exported during the comparable conflict period versus 509 a year earlier, roughly a 96% reduction), European storage is only around 62% full and below the seasonal norm, and winter is approaching. Europe has less room to absorb another physical shock. For UK businesses the transmission runs Hormuz, Qatar LNG, global LNG competition, TTF, NBP, gas-fired power, supplier price books, customer contract. The weekend did not produce a resolution; it produced more direct maritime escalation, and it arrived after Friday's supplier books were already printed.

05Futures Market Explained

Why a customer can see, and buy, future delivery periods today.

The futures market shows today's price for energy delivered in a future period. A customer does not have to wait until their contract end date to see today's price for future delivery.

Near-term gas remains extremely expensive relative to many later delivery periods: latest W-26 gas is 178.50p/therm against 118.50p for S-27, 115.25p for W-27, 78.50p for S-28, 86.00p for W-28 and 64.47p for S-30. Do not let 180p-plus near-term gas create the impression that all future gas is 180p-plus.

Cheaper further out does not mean cheaper later. The customer mistake is thinking a cheaper future period means they should wait. It does not. It means we should check whether that future period can be secured now. Backwardation does not mean wait. The future market is only useful while the customer can still buy that future delivery period, and waiting gives the market time to remove today's forward-value opportunity. Waiting is not neutral.

A customer 12 to 18 months from renewal can now test pricing for a delivery period the market currently values far below the distressed front, before Monday's wholesale move works its way into supplier books.

10Top Five Warning Signs

Exactly five, ranked by potential UK price impact.

  • 1. Iran implements the new restricted zone. Especially if commercial ships are turned back, detained or added to the sanctions list; the zone could effectively expand the blacklist system well beyond the named 56 vessels. Announced, not yet operational.

14Ceasefire Reality Check

Testing the wider conflict against what is actually happening.

The wider conflict is not in a stable ceasefire. The June framework failed to produce permanent normalisation, and the 60-day fee-free memorandum lapsed in mid-August without replacement. Current state of play: direct US-Iran strikes have resumed (tankers hit Saturday); tanker attacks are now part of the exchange; the US blockade continues (92 commercial vessels redirected, three disabled, two boarded in connection with it, per CENTCOM's Sunday figures, subject to fresh verification); Iran's transit restrictions and 56-vessel blacklist continue and are expanding; Hormuz traffic is at its lowest since May; talks with Iran are described by Washington as non-existent, while the Iran-Oman corridor track is alive; Qatar LNG remains disrupted; and the Houthi front is escalating.

A pause in bombing is not the same as normalisation of the energy system. Even if direct fire pauses temporarily, maritime pressure, sanctions, the blockade, Iran's restrictions and the Houthi front all continue. Only report reopening when it is fresh, independently verified, operational and reflected in shipping data.

The energy risk is no longer concentrated around one Middle East chokepoint: if Hormuz is the problem, Bab al-Mandab is part of the workaround. A route can remain physically open and still carry a material war-risk premium. Workarounds reduce outright shortage risk, but increase time, cost and complexity.

17Supplier Price-Book Lag

The most commercially important section today.

Friday's Price Book Was Written Before the Weekend Escalation

Friday: customer quote exists (internal gas W-26 offered at 178.50p/therm)
↓
Saturday / Sunday: tankers attacked, Hormuz traffic deteriorates, Iran threatens heavier response
↓
Monday: wholesale market opens higher (NBP above 183p, a 3.5-year high)
↓
Supplier decides whether to hold, pull or reprice the book
↓
Friday's price can disappear even though the customer did nothing over the weekend

The question is not just where wholesale is. It is which supplier books have caught up. Switch365 internal experience shows that when wholesale gas approaches the 180p/therm area, some suppliers withdraw existing books, temporarily suspend rates or return with higher prices; that is internal commercial evidence, and 180p is a useful internal repricing warning level, not an official supplier threshold. Public NBP traded through that level this morning.

Supplier price-book lag is the opportunity and the danger. Wholesale markets move first. Supplier books often follow. Once a supplier withdraws a rate, the customer cannot go back and secure yesterday's price. The market does not need to rise again tomorrow for customers to pay more tomorrow.
Price accuracy: a wholesale move does not translate pound-for-pound into the customer's total energy bill. Wholesale energy is only one component of customer pricing, alongside network charges, policy costs and supplier margin. Never read a 10% Brent move as a 10% bill move: Brent is not the customer's gas quote.
