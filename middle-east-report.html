Summary

President Trump has now made clear that Washington is not working to a short timetable for ending the confrontation with Iran. Speaking to Al Jazeera on Wednesday, he said he was "not in a hurry" and had "no time schedule, none", arguing that Iran's deteriorating economy showed US pressure was working, and that economic and military measures are "both effective".

That matters enormously for energy. The market has spent months hoping that diplomacy would rapidly restore normal shipping through Hormuz. Instead, the conflict reaches six months on Friday, Qatar's LNG exports remain approximately 96% lower across the reported comparable period, commercial shipping remains far below normal, and Washington is increasingly relying on sanctions and economic pressure designed to work over time rather than overnight.

This does not mean the conflict must continue for years. It does mean businesses should be increasingly cautious about building procurement decisions around the assumption that everything returns to normal in the next few weeks. The important change is duration: Washington is no longer presenting a rapid end as essential to its strategy. A quieter military period does not necessarily mean a shorter energy crisis.

Futures Market Explained

The futures market shows today's price for energy delivered in a future period. A customer does not have to wait until their contract end date to see today's market for future delivery: a business whose contract starts in April 2027 can see, today, what Summer-27 gas and power cost, and in many cases can secure that period now.

Today's curve is in steep backwardation: future delivery periods are priced well below the front. Cheaper further out does not mean cheaper later. Backwardation does not mean wait. It means the market currently expects the Hormuz disruption to ease eventually, while still charging a heavy premium for any energy delivered this winter.

The customer mistake is thinking a cheaper future period means they should wait. It does not. It means we should check whether that future period can be secured now. The future market is only useful while the customer can still buy that future delivery period, and waiting gives the market time to remove today's forward-value opportunity. Waiting is not neutral.

HORMUZ→ QATAR LNG→ GLOBAL LNG COMPETITION→ TTF→ NBP→ UK GAS→ GAS-FIRED UK POWER→ SUPPLIER BOOKS→ CUSTOMER CONTRACT

LNG remains the bridge between Hormuz and UK gas. Brent is not the customer's gas quote. The energy bill conversation is about NBP, TTF, UK power, supplier books, contract end date and forward delivery period.

Backwardation begins immediately: S-27 gas is 35.1% below W-26 and the deepest point of the gas curve is S-29 at 62.0% below the front. Power shows the same shape, with S-29 53.7% below W-26. The recovery has started at the front, but the far end also moved: every gas season rose, so duration risk is visible in longer delivery periods.

Compared with one year ago the whole curve remains dramatically repriced: W-26 gas is 98% above its year-ago level and W-26 power 73% above. Your delivery period matters: a 2027, 2028, 2029 or 2030 contract end date should be compared against its own delivery period, not against today's front price and not against last year's.

Forward-value conclusion: the market is currently offering 2027-2030 delivery at 35-62% below the front season for gas and 32-54% below for power. That is today's forward-value gap, and it just widened because the front rebounded. It exists while the conflict premium sits at the front; if Hormuz genuinely normalises the whole curve can fall, but if duration risk hardens, the far end rises towards the front. Waiting is not neutral in either direction.

Top Five Warning Signs

1
Trump is prepared to wait

No short US timetable means the economic confrontation may persist for months. Sanctions can weaken an economy considerably faster than they can force a government to surrender, and Iran has decades of experience operating under US sanctions. Economic pain does not automatically translate into rapid political capitulation.

2
Qatar LNG does not recover

18 cargoes versus 509 remains extraordinary, and Ras Laffan repair timelines measured in years mean full normalisation is not a switch that gets flipped by a ceasefire headline. The six-month duration of the conflict is now visible in physical gas fundamentals.

3
Hormuz diplomacy fails to create safe physical shipping

Talks without tankers are not enough. The Iran-Oman track is unfinalised, the US has not accepted any toll arrangement, and a bilateral Iran-Oman shipping arrangement does not automatically remove the US blockade or US sanctions.

4
Tanker attacks continue despite diplomatic progress

Overnight's projectile strike near Khasab is at least the third vessel incident in ten days, after the Metro Venetian strike on about 24 Aug and the fatal Minoan Dignity attack around 17-18 Aug. The IMO counts 70 incidents and 19 seafarers killed since 28 Feb. Commercial confidence remains impaired.

5
Storage, Norway or NBP deteriorate into autumn

Storage is a buffer, not immunity. At about 61% full in late August, Europe is exposed to a cold winter, a Norwegian outage or stronger Asian LNG demand. The European gas buffers matter more than oil headlines.

Qatar LNG

Qatar supplied about one-fifth of world daily LNG before the conflict, and Hormuz carried about one-fifth of global LNG trade; most LNG historically moving through Hormuz came from Qatar, so the Qatar 20% and Hormuz 20% figures describe largely the same supply exposure, not two separate 20% losses. Britain does not need to lose a specific Qatari cargo for Qatar disruption to increase UK gas prices: the price effect arrives through competition for replacement LNG, as Asia and Europe bid for US and Atlantic cargoes. LNG is the bridge between Hormuz and UK gas.

Bab el-Mandeb

Workarounds

Saudi East-West pipeline and Yanbu, SUMED and Sidi Kerir, Cape of Good Hope rerouting, Fujairah storage and ship-to-ship transfers all continue to move replacement barrels and cargoes. Workarounds reduce outright shortage risk but increase time, cost and complexity. Friction gets priced. The important question is no longer whether anything can get through Hormuz; it is whether normal commercial LNG and oil flows can return and stay normal. A route can be physically navigable and commercially impaired. Hormuz is not open as usual, and diplomatic progress has not yet produced safe normal shipping.

Supplier Price-Book Lag

Supplier price-book lag is the opportunity and the danger. Wholesale markets move first. Supplier books often follow. This week shows the mechanism in both directions: NBP day-ahead settled at 166.15p on Monday, ICE UK gas closed at 161.02p on Wednesday, internal offered W-26 came off 4.25p to 161.25p on Wednesday morning, then rebounded 5.25p to 166.50p by Thursday morning. The diplomatic dip reached supplier offers and was withdrawn within about a day.

The chain just ran in reverse: diplomatic optimism pulled wholesale down, a temporary supplier opportunity opened, and then one tanker strike plus a no-timetable statement from Washington closed it again, while duration risk, constrained Qatar LNG and weak storage remained unresolved underneath. Suppliers price probability, not certainty; suppliers do not need the war to intensify tomorrow for prices to rise. A change in the probability of a prolonged conflict can be enough, and overnight provided exactly that change.

Why speed matters: once a supplier withdraws a rate, the customer cannot go back and secure yesterday's price. Wednesday's 161.25p W-26 offer no longer exists. There are multiple credible events capable of moving today's board again before tomorrow. Waiting with news like this is not neutral.
