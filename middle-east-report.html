Winter gas rises, but every forward gas season falls. Why are prices moving in opposite directions?

The near-term winter gas reference has moved higher while every supplied 2027 to 2030 gas season has become cheaper. Europe's shrinking reserves help explain why prompt risk can differ from the longer-dated curve.

Prompt Winter Risk: Elevated Forward Gas Curve: Falling Power Curve: Mixed Route Risk: High Review-Now Window

Switch365's Winter 2026 gas working reference rose from 218.000p to 221.750p/therm, up 1.72% on Thursday. Yet all eight supplied gas seasons from Summer 2027 to Winter 2030 fell, including Summer 2027 down 2.50p and Winter 2029 down 2.00p. The immediate winter signal and the later-delivery curve are diverging. These are internal indicators, however, not proof that every supplier has changed an executable tariff.

Europe's gas storage stood at 67.84% on the 24 November gas day, with Germany at 55.25%. Cold weather and lower wind can increase demand for heating and gas-fired electricity, although actual generation and imports must be checked. Reuters also documented an attack and smoke at Saudi Arabia's Ras Tanura refinery, but no confirmed production loss. More reliable Norwegian deliveries, US LNG and milder weather could counter the pressure. None of these factors, taken alone, establishes why every future season fell.

For UK businesses, contract start date is crucial. Summer 2028 gas is indicated at 80.50p/therm against today's 221.750p Winter 2026 reference, but they cover different delivery periods and do not represent an automatic whole-bill saving. Electricity is more mixed: both 2027 seasons fell, while four later seasons rose. The practical response is to compare valid future-start quotes, standing charges, broker fees, pass-through terms and total cost. Prices could move either way, so avoid assuming that waiting or fixing is always best.

The decision is not whether winter looks risky; it is whether the actual forward price available to this customer is worth securing for their contract start date.

Section 5

Futures Market Explained

Why today's prompt winter price and the 2027 to 2030 seasons can move in opposite directions

The instruments are not the same thing

  • NBP is the UK gas benchmark; TTF is the Dutch benchmark that now anchors most European trading. They move similarly but not identically.
  • Day-ahead / spot prices gas for tomorrow. Front-month (Dec-26) prices next month's delivery. Seasons (S-27, W-27…) price six-month delivery blocks that have not started yet.
  • The Switch365 W-26 reference is an internal working indicator for the winter now under way. It is not a public exchange settlement, a day-ahead quote or a retail tariff.
  • The W-26 power figure is modelled: Wednesday's £169.43 scaled by 90% of the gas relative move to £172.05. It is not an observed electricity future or an executable customer offer.

Why the curve can split

  • Prompt winter pricing reacts to today's balance: low EU storage (67.84%, 24 Nov gas day), cold forecasts, wind levels, Norwegian flows and LNG arrivals.
  • 2028 to 2030 seasons price expected average conditions years ahead: new LNG supply, repair timelines, demand trends and policy. Those can ease even while this winter tightens.
  • A sharp forward-curve move can happen on a day when Brent falls, because gas, wind, weather, storage and LNG balances differ from crude's supply-demand balance. Brent is not the customer's gas quote.
  • Supplier rate books lag wholesale screens. An offered-season indicator is not an executable supplier rate until a named, timestamped, customer-eligible quote exists.
No forward-curve value is a guaranteed future spot price, and no two distinct delivery seasons combine into a total-bill savings percentage. Backwardation does not mean wait.

British Gas 18-Month Window

Switch365 internal commercial statement | eligibility rules apply

British Gas has extended its business energy renewal window from 12 months to 18 months. This gives eligible customers an earlier opportunity to review future-start pricing, which matters directly on a day when every supplied 2027 to 2030 gas season sits well below the current winter reference.

US LNG is a major bearish buffer, but cannot automatically replace all lost Qatari LNG. QatarEnergy's force majeure is extended to the end of December 2026 and Qatar has become the largest contracted buyer of US LNG (up to 7 mtpa) while it waits for safe, repeatable Hormuz passage. Future 2030/2031 SPA volumes are not today's delivered cargoes.

7. Wholesale contracts and supplier lag Mixed

TTF Dec-26 traded near €88.1/MWh early Thursday after settling Wednesday at €88.765; UK gas CFDs were indicated around 222 to 225p/therm. Named instruments and timestamps only. Wholesale markets move first; supplier books often follow, and supplier price-book lag is the opportunity and the danger.

Counterfactual Two-sided

Lower future prices could persist if physical supply and weather improve; cold, supply interruptions or a verified infrastructure loss could reverse them. There is no certainty either way, and no market bottom is established.

Section 14

Ceasefire Reality Check

Diplomacy is active; physical shipping is the test

Tehran has told Washington that normal shipping through the Strait of Hormuz will not resume until its demands are met: an end to the US naval blockade of Iranian ports and a halt to the war on all fronts. Through Qatari mediators, Iran has offered to reopen the waterway within 48 hours if Washington agrees. The US is seeking concessions on Iran's nuclear programme, while Tehran also demands sanctions relief and the release of frozen assets.

  • Physical test: Kpler-cited data put Hormuz oil flows at about half of pre-war levels as of 24 Nov, and November attack frequency is at its highest since the war began. A route can remain physically open and still carry a material war-risk premium. Friction gets priced.
  • Defence build-up: a multinational defensive architecture continues to form around Saudi Arabia, which could reduce successful attacks even as it signals severity. Both effects run at once.
  • OPEC+ wildcard: ministers meet Sunday 29 November to set 2027 output policy, with Iraq and Kazakhstan pressing for higher capacity assessments. The outcome could move crude either way; crude is not NBP.
  • Qatar readiness: QatarEnergy says it can resume normal LNG operations within weeks of a Hormuz reopening, but LNG is the bridge between Hormuz and UK gas, and a contract signing is not a tanker delivered.
Do not treat declarations as de-escalation. De-escalation shows up first in measured departures, insured transits and falling war-risk premiums.

Shipping Risk

Strait of Hormuz: SEVERE

JMIC (Update 116, 22 Nov) keeps deliberate attacks highly likely. Three outbound tankers were struck off Khasab on 20 to 21 Nov, two within an hour; seven tankers struck since 16 Nov. AIS-visible cargo transits dropped to near zero on 19 to 21 Nov with about 218 commercial vessels stationary at anchorages east of the strait. Arabian Gulf: SUBSTANTIAL

Thursday's projectile incident 50 nautical miles west of Dubai shows the threat is no longer confined to the strait: loading routes that had looked relatively safe are back in the risk picture, feeding war-risk premiums and freight rates before headline prices. GNSS interference remains strongest along the UAE coast, Bahrain and the Saudi east coast.

Gulf of Aden: SUBSTANTIAL

Two armed skiff approaches southwest of Al Mukalla in four days (17 and 20 Nov), both ended by warning shots from embarked security teams. The security risk zone is not confined to Hormuz.

Bab el-Mandeb / Red Sea: volatile

Open to commercial traffic but volatile: about 23 transits per day on 19 to 21 Nov, up from about 19 on 15 to 18 Nov. Houthi forces hold Red Sea coastal positions vacated on 15 Nov. Yanbu and the East-West pipeline make the Red Sea route more valuable as Hormuz stays unreliable.

An LNG security incident matters more to UK gas than an unrelated general-cargo incident. None of this week's confirmed strikes involved a laden LNG carrier; the Qatar force majeure to end-December remains the binding LNG constraint for Europe.

Section 17

Supplier Price-Book Lag

Why the wholesale curve and the customer's quote are not the same number on the same day

Wholesale markets move first. Supplier books often follow. The offered-season indicators in Sections 3 and 4 show what may be available in the supplier market, subject to live quote validity and eligibility; they are not universal executable tariffs. Public wholesale explains why; the Switch365 offered-price indicators show what may be available; a named, timestamped, customer-eligible quote is what a business can actually sign.

  • Supplier price-book lag is the opportunity and the danger: a future-start rate can still reflect older, higher wholesale levels, or it can be revised upward before a customer acts.
  • Once a supplier withdraws a rate, the customer cannot go back and secure yesterday's price.
  • Waiting is not neutral. Waiting only works if the market moves in the customer's favour.
  • Cheaper further out does not mean cheaper later. Backwardation does not mean wait.
Today's practical check: does a valid future-start quote for this customer's actual contract end date reflect Thursday's lower 2027 to 2030 indications? Only the supplier's live book answers that.
