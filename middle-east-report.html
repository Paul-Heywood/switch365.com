THE MARKET IS PRICING IN PEACE, BUT THE GAS CRISIS IS FAR FROM OVER. IS THIS A BUYING WINDOW?

UK wholesale gas has fallen sharply on hopes of diplomacy, while the underlying LNG and winter supply risks remain unresolved.

UK gas has fallen from roughly 250p to 214p per therm, around 14.4% in less than a week on the user's approximate market comparison. Following the US midterm elections, which gave Democrats control of both chambers of Congress, markets have priced a greater possibility of diplomacy with Iran rather than further escalation. Much of this decline reflects a reduction in the geopolitical risk premium. Separately, on a like-for-like public series, the NBP December 2026 contract settled at 212.25p on Friday 6 November, down 17.6% from 257.60p on Friday 30 October.

But lower perceived risk is not the same as restored supply. Qatar's damaged LNG trains remain offline, normal commercial shipping through Hormuz has not resumed, with two further UKMTO-confirmed tanker incidents over the weekend, and European gas storage is around 73% full, with Germany near 59.5%, as winter withdrawals begin. Norway's Visund platform is offline after a fire. More US and non-Gulf LNG is providing real relief, but the IEA still forecasts lower global LNG supply this year if Hormuz remains restricted. Weekend diplomacy is fragile: President Trump meets Qatar's prime minister today, yet Secretary of State Rubio says the sides are not on the verge of a breakthrough.

For UK businesses, this could be a buying window, not a guaranteed market bottom. Switch365's latest offered-range indicators, updated this morning, show every gas and power row edging higher versus Friday's close, a reminder that quotes can reprice in either direction. Failed negotiations, colder weather or another supply disruption could reverse the fall; a lasting peace agreement could push prices lower still. The practical response is to compare valid supplier quotes, contract lengths and whole-bill terms, then decide whether today's price protection suits the customer's circumstances.

05

Futures Market Explained

What a forward season price is

A seasonal contract (S-27, W-27 and so on) is gas or power for delivery in a future six-month period, priced today. Summer (S) runs April to September, winter (W) October to March. When future seasons trade below the current front period, the curve is in backwardation: the market expects today's tightness to ease. Backwardation does not mean wait. A forward price is a deal available today, not a forecast of what that period will cost to buy later.

Why it matters to a business

Suppliers build fixed-price business tariffs from these wholesale seasons, then add non-commodity costs, margins and risk premiums. Wholesale markets move first. Supplier books often follow, with a lag. A lower S-28 offered quote is not a prediction that bills will be cheaper in 2028: cheaper further out does not mean cheaper later. What matters is the actual, valid, executable quote for the customer's meter, start date and terms.

British Gas 18-Month Window

British Gas business customers can typically agree a renewal up to 18 months before their current contract ends. The new agreement starts when the existing one ends; it does not replace it early, and not every customer is automatically eligible. British Gas 18-month eligibility turns this market intelligence into a practical customer action: with the curve still backwardated, an eligible customer can test today's forward-start pricing against the S-27/W-27 seasons rather than waiting for the renewal window to collide with a possible winter premium rebuild.

Checks before quoting: confirm BG eligibility and the exact contract end date, obtain a live executable rate, compare unit rate and standing charge against other supplier books, and record quote validity. If the offer is unattractive, say so and explain the trade-off.

Supplier Price-Book Lag

Wholesale has moved. Have supplier books caught up?

A ~14-18% weekly fall in prompt wholesale does not mean a 14-18% fall in any retail unit rate or annual bill. Business tariffs blend multiple delivery seasons, non-commodity costs, standing charges, credit margins and risk premiums. Supplier price-book lag is the opportunity and the danger: a supplier may still be offering last week's higher rate, or may have repriced lower, or, as this morning's estimated internal tick-up hints, may already be edging rates back up with the market.

Two-sided test for every customer: obtain a live executable quote with its expiry; compare like-for-like against the last quote (same start date, meter, profile, unit rate, standing charge, fixed vs pass-through); and record the difference. Once a supplier withdraws a rate, the customer cannot go back and secure yesterday's price. The market does not need to rise again tomorrow for customers to pay more tomorrow.

Wholesale markets move first. Supplier books often follow.

Customer Action

The action everywhere is "review live pricing now", never "blindly contract immediately". This is a review-now window, not a wait-and-see market.

1 · Existing valid quote Top priority

Confirm the quote is still executable: product eligibility, remaining validity, supplier terms and customer consent. Prioritise genuinely exposed customers. A quoted rate can be withdrawn without notice; there is no guarantee of future repricing in the customer's favour.

2 · Contract ends in 2026

Check available start dates now; avoid out-of-contract exposure on deemed rates; confirm the existing fixed agreement can end cleanly with no overlapping supply obligation. With W-26 gas at 214.00p (est.) against 243.12p last Tuesday, current near-term offers merit immediate testing.

The curve is backwardated: 2028 seasons are ~52-57% below W-26 working references on today's estimated internal snapshot, and 2030 gas ~63-68% below. Longer terms can be attractive for certainty but can forego savings if markets fall further. Only valid, available offers count.

Waiting is not neutral

Waiting could help if diplomacy delivers a signed deal, Qatar LNG recovers, Hormuz stabilises, Norway stays strong, US LNG keeps growing, weather stays mild and supplier books fall. Waiting could hurt if talks collapse, Saudi sites are hit with real capacity loss, an LNG carrier is struck, storage drains in a cold snap or supplier books rise. Waiting only works if the market moves in the customer's favour.
